# The Headset, the Harness, and a 112-Year-Old Law
*August 22, 2026*

Apple just cut more than two hundred jobs from the two products it told us were the future: Vision Pro and Siri. The gaming team is largely gone. That's the loudest signal yet about what spatial computing actually became, and it's where we start today.

## Bloomberg reported Friday that Apple is cutting staff on both the Vision Pro and Siri teams. More than two hundred jobs total. The specifics matter more than the number: Apple is largely shutting down the team building games for Vision Pro, and shrinking the team that produces immersive video content for it. Apple's statement was that it's evolving the business to deliver the best experiences for users, and that new roles are being created elsewhere.

Here's why the shape of these cuts tells you more than the headcount. Vision Pro launched in early 2024 at three thousand four hundred ninety-nine dollars. The pitch was spatial computing, but the honest answer to why anyone bought one came down to two things: immersive video, and games. Those were the experiences that justified strapping a device to your face. Apple just cut the teams making both. You don't cut first-party content for a platform that already has enough content. You cut it when you've decided the platform isn't going to reach the audience that would make that content pay for itself. The Siri cuts land differently, and I'd resist folding them into the same story. Siri is being rebuilt around large language models, and that rebuild makes a lot of the old machinery irrelevant. A team that maintained hand-written intent parsing and rule-based responses has genuinely less to do when the system becomes a model with tools attached. Some of those cuts are a technology transition, not a retreat. The Vision Pro cuts are a retreat.

My read: Apple isn't leaving headsets, it's leaving this headset. The three-thousand-dollar developer kit did its job, which was to teach Apple what the hardware needs to become. Watch for something lighter, cheaper, and much closer to glasses, and expect Apple to lean on other people's content when it arrives. Speaking of expensive bets under scrutiny, the venture capital industry got some unwelcome attention this week.

## The Department of Justice has reportedly spent almost a year investigating Andreessen Horowitz. The issue is board seats. Ben Horowitz sits on the board of Databricks. Martin Casado sits on the board of Fivetran. Those two companies now compete with each other.

The law being used here is the part of the Clayton Act that bans interlocking directorates. It's from 1912, and it says the same person can't sit on the boards of two competing companies. It's been enforced against corporations for decades. It has almost never been pointed at venture capital. The reason it's awkward for venture is that spreading bets across a sector is the entire model. You back several companies in data infrastructure precisely because you don't know which one wins, and you take board seats because that's how you get information and influence. The standard defense has been that these aren't the same person, they're two different partners, and that the companies weren't competitors when the money went in. Both of those are true here. Databricks and Fivetran started in different lanes and grew into each other, which is what successful infrastructure companies do. But the law is about the seat, not the intent, and growing into a competitor doesn't make the conflict disappear. If the DOJ presses this, the practical outcome isn't a fine. It's that big firms have to give up board seats when portfolio companies converge, and they have to do it on a regulator's timeline instead of their own.

The chilling part isn't the investigation, it's the year. A year of quiet scrutiny means the DOJ found something worth being patient about. Every large multi-stage firm should be auditing their own board maps this weekend, and I'd bet several already are. Now, to the AI market, where prices went one direction again.

## Two model releases worth pairing. OpenAI cut the price of GPT 5.6 Sol by twenty percent. And DeepSeek shipped an experimental release of a small, fast vision model in the v4 line.

Price cuts at the frontier are not generosity, and they're not really about competition with the other frontier lab either. They're about what happens underneath. Every time an open-weight lab ships a model that's good enough for a common task at a fraction of the cost, the paid tier above it has to justify the gap. A fast vision model that you can run yourself puts direct pressure on the cheapest paid vision endpoints, and that pressure travels up. The twenty percent figure is more interesting than it sounds, because it's roughly the threshold where product decisions flip. Things that don't pencil out at the old price start to: running a model over every document a user uploads instead of the ones they ask about, background agents that check work continuously instead of on demand, evaluation runs that are thorough instead of sampled. Twenty percent doesn't change what's possible. It changes what's affordable to do by default, and default behavior is where products actually get better. The word experimental on the DeepSeek release is doing real work, though. Small vision models are notoriously uneven: strong on charts and screenshots, weak on dense documents and anything requiring counting. Test it on your actual inputs before you believe any benchmark about it.

The useful habit here is to stop watching the top of the price list and start watching the bottom. The cheap tier is where the cost curve is visible, and the cheap tier is what determines whether your idea ships. Which brings us to a finding that suggests the model might not be the important part at all.

## Nvidia published research this week with a conclusion the industry has been circling for a year. AI agents can perform well, and stay on task instead of spiraling, through fine-tuning of the surrounding system, even when the underlying model isn't especially strong at that task. The harness matters more than the hero.

By harness, I mean everything wrapped around the model: the tools it can call, the sandbox it runs in, what it sees after each action, how errors come back, when it gets stopped. For two years the working assumption was that agent quality is a function of model quality, and the way to get a better agent is to wait for a better model. The evidence keeps pointing somewhere else. Two projects on Hacker News today are the practical version of the same idea. Autolith is a programming agent with a live runtime, meaning the agent isn't writing code into the void and hoping. It executes, inspects the actual state of the running program, and works from what came back. That single change kills an enormous class of failure, because most agent flailing is the model reasoning confidently about a program state it has never observed. And a detailed writeup on building an almost fully self-hosted, sandboxed agentic software factory covers the other half: isolation, reproducible environments, and the plumbing to run this on your own machines instead of someone else's. Neither is a model advance. Both are the parts that determine whether the thing works.

The strategic read for anyone building here: the model is the component you can swap out, and you will swap it, probably three times this year. The harness is the thing you own, and it's where your leverage compounds. Fine-tuning a good scaffold around a mediocre model is a better use of a quarter than waiting for a better model. Now, some evidence about what AI does to people rather than programs.

## A new working paper found that giving students AI assistance raised their homework scores and then lowered their exam scores. Same students, same course. Help went up, learning went down.

This is the cleanest statement yet of a problem everyone half-suspected. Homework was never the point. Homework is a proxy, a stand-in for the hours of struggle that build the mental model. When AI produces the artifact without the struggle, the proxy still looks great and the underlying thing quietly doesn't happen. Then the exam, which measures the underlying thing, tells the truth. What I like about this design is that the failure only shows up because they measured both. If you only looked at assignment scores, this intervention looks like a triumph, and I guarantee there are schools right now reporting exactly that triumph. Two cautions before anyone runs with it. It's one study, and effects like this depend heavily on the subject, how the assistance was offered, and how the exam was written. The direction is the interesting part, not the magnitude. And the fix almost certainly isn't a ban, because bans don't survive contact with a phone. The fix is assessment that measures the thing you actually want, which mostly means more in-person and oral evaluation and less take-home work.

The uncomfortable extension is that none of this is about students. The same mechanism applies to an engineer who ships accepted completions for six months and never builds the model of the system in their head. The homework scores look great right up until the incident. Let's talk about tools that give you more control over the machines.

## Three projects that are all about bending models to your terms rather than accepting the defaults. Fit, speed, and voice.

First, Shoehorn, a Show HN that quantizes any model down to run on your hardware. Quantizing means storing the model's numbers at lower precision, so it takes less memory, at some cost to quality. Doing this well normally means a command line, a lot of reading, and at least one failed attempt. Shoehorn wraps it in a graphical interface that also helps you find models and set them up, working on Mac, Linux and Windows. That last part is the contribution. The gap between people who could run local models and people who actually do is almost entirely tooling friction, not hardware. Second, a writeup on getting a text-to-speech model responding in under fifty milliseconds. That number is not a vanity metric. Human conversation turns over in about two hundred milliseconds, and anything past roughly three hundred reads as a pause, which is why voice assistants feel like walkie-talkies. Getting the speech engine down to fifty milliseconds leaves the rest of the budget for the model that decides what to say. Voice interfaces stop feeling like a transaction somewhere right around there. Third, and much smaller but I enjoyed it: Claudette, a tool to make Claude stop writing like a BuzzFeed article. No more it's not just this, it's that. No more three-item rhetorical crescendos. Every model has a house style baked in by post-training, and that style leaks into everything you produce with it.

The through-line is ownership. Run it on your hardware, get it fast enough to feel real, and make it sound like you instead of like the training data. That's a healthier relationship with these tools than accepting whatever the endpoint hands back. And ownership of performance is having a broader moment right now.

## A post arguing there's no reason for software to be slow anymore hit the front page today, alongside two projects that are the argument in practice.

The essay's case is familiar but keeps being true: hardware got faster by orders of magnitude, and software absorbed all of it and then some. Text editors that stutter, chat apps that take four seconds to start, settings pages that load a spinner. Every one of those is a choice, usually made by a team optimizing for shipping speed rather than running speed, and usually defensible in isolation. In aggregate it's the entire experience of using a computer. Now the practice. Rust Glancer, from the person who built rust-analyzer, is a language server for Rust that claims to use one hundred times less memory. A language server is the background program that gives your editor autocomplete and jump-to-definition. The reason they eat gigabytes is that they hold a full compiler's understanding of your project in memory to answer any question instantly. Glancer takes the other side of the trade: know less, answer the common questions, stay small. On a large project that can be the difference between an editor that works and one that swaps. And Kino is a new web server for Ruby 4.0 built on Ractors. Ractors are Ruby's mechanism for genuine parallelism, running code on multiple cores without sharing the global lock that has effectively made Ruby web servers single-core for their whole existence. Ractors have been in Ruby for years and mostly unused because the constraints are strict. A serious web server built on them is the first real test of whether that design pays off.

There's a reason performance is fashionable again. Hardware isn't rescuing anyone anymore, and generated code makes bloat cheaper than ever to produce. When it costs nothing to add a layer, someone has to be paying attention to the total. Databases have been quietly doing this work all along.

## Two database stories that are both unglamorous foundation work, which is usually where the durable stuff lives. Cassandra is getting real transactions in version 6, and DuckDB rewrote its SQL parser.

Cassandra's whole identity was the trade it made: give up transactions, get availability and scale that nothing else could match. If you needed to write to two rows and have both succeed or both fail, you went somewhere else, or you built the guarantee yourself in application code and got it subtly wrong. Version 6 brings general transactions across partitions using a consensus protocol designed to work without a leader, so the transaction support doesn't reintroduce the single point of coordination that Cassandra existed to avoid. If it holds up under real load, it removes the main reason teams paired Cassandra with a second database. The DuckDB change is smaller but affects every user daily. They're moving from a parser inherited from Postgres to one built on a parsing expression grammar. The old style of parser is fast and battle-tested and produces error messages like syntax error at or near, which tells you almost nothing about what you actually did wrong. The new approach makes it far easier to say what was expected at that position, to extend the syntax without fighting grammar conflicts, and to power autocomplete that understands where you are in the statement. Error messages are a real feature. People just don't put them on roadmaps.

Both of these are years of work with no demo. That's the tell for infrastructure that will still be here in a decade. Not everything in infrastructure is going that smoothly, though.

## A post titled, simply, OpenTelemetry isn't going well, and the discussion suggests a lot of people agree.

OpenTelemetry is the vendor-neutral standard for traces, metrics and logs. The promise was excellent: instrument your code once, send the data anywhere, and never be held hostage by a monitoring vendor's proprietary agent again. And it won. It's the default, the vendors support it, the cloud providers support it. The complaints are what comes after winning. The specification keeps growing, the naming conventions for common fields keep changing underneath people, the software development kits have grown complicated enough that instrumenting an app is a project rather than an afternoon, and the collector component in the middle has become a configuration language that teams have to learn and operate. There's also the cost problem, which isn't OpenTelemetry's fault but shows up on its bill: it makes it easy to emit enormous volumes of data, and someone pays for storing all of it. Worth naming the alternative honestly, though. Before this, every vendor had its own agent, switching was a rewrite, and nobody could compare anything. The mess is real and it's still better than what it replaced.

The pattern to notice: a standard's problems become universal exactly when the standard succeeds. Nobody complained about the fragmented old world at this volume, because the complaints were spread across twelve incompatible camps. Let's zoom out to where the money is going.

## Two infrastructure bets on very different timescales. Micron is putting ten billion dollars into a research hub in Boise. And Starcloud raised two hundred fifty million dollars to build data centers in orbit.

The Micron number is the one to take seriously. Ten billion dollars for research, in Boise, where the company is already headquartered and already has fabrication capacity. Memory is the actual constraint on AI accelerators right now, not compute. The stacked high-bandwidth memory that sits next to a modern GPU is the part that's supply-limited, and the part that determines how large a model you can serve and how fast. There are effectively three companies in the world that can make it. A commitment of that size, aimed at research rather than a single production line, is a bet that memory stays the bottleneck for a decade, and it's a bet that the advanced work happens in the United States. Starcloud is a much longer shot, and the sharpest line in the coverage was about launch options drying up. That's the actual story. There's about to be a serious fight over access to space, because everyone with an orbital ambition is competing for a small number of heavy-lift slots. On the merits of orbital data centers, I'd stay skeptical for a specific reason: cooling. In a vacuum, you can only shed heat by radiating it, which means large heavy radiators, which means launch mass, which is the expensive part. Unlimited solar power is the easy half of the pitch. Getting rid of the waste heat is the hard half, and it's the half the pitch decks are quietest about.

What both have in common is a wager that demand for compute outruns the two things that constrain it on Earth: the power grid and the number of fabs. One of those bets is boring and probably right. Before we wrap, something practical.

## A collection of small native web tricks worth remembering made the front page, and it's the kind of list that pays for itself the next time you open an editor.

The theme is that the browser quietly absorbed about a decade of JavaScript libraries and most working developers haven't updated their instincts. Modal dialogs, accordions, tooltips and popovers that used to require a library and a pile of accessibility fixes are now single HTML elements or attributes that handle focus trapping and keyboard behavior correctly on their own. Layout patterns that needed a scroll library are two lines of CSS. Selecting a parent based on what's inside it, which was famously impossible and drove people to JavaScript, is now a normal CSS selector. Lazy loading of images is an attribute. Every one of these replaces a dependency, and dependencies are the thing that make sites slow, break during upgrades, and turn into security advisories at inconvenient moments. The habit worth building is checking whether the platform does it before reaching for a package, because the answer has changed and it keeps changing.

That connects straight back to the performance conversation from earlier. Most slow software isn't slow because someone wrote a bad loop. It's slow because it shipped four hundred kilobytes to do what the browser does for free.
