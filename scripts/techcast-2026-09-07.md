# Neuralese, Universal Embeddings, and the Bill Coming Due
*September 07, 2026*

Today: the case that we are about to lose the ability to read our own AI systems' minds, right as we hand them more of the work. Also, a paper suggesting every model that has ever been built is quietly converging on the same internal map of meaning. And two newspapers walk into a federal court. This is TechCast Daily for Monday, September seventh.

## Start with a word you are going to hear a lot this year: neuralese. It sounds like jargon invented to sell a conference talk. It is not. It names a specific engineering decision that a handful of labs are actively considering, and the argument against it is one of the cleanest safety arguments anyone has made.

Here is the setup. When a reasoning model works through a hard problem today, it writes its intermediate steps out in English. It says something like, the user asked for X, but first I need to check Y. That text is not decoration. It is the actual computation. The model reads its own words back in and keeps going. Which means we get something almost unheard of in machine learning: a running transcript of the machine's process, in a language we speak. Researchers call this a monitorable chain of thought, and several labs have said out loud that it is the single most useful safety property they currently have. If a model is planning something it should not, quite often it just says so in the scratchpad. Now the problem. English is a terrible medium for a neural network to think in. Every step, the model compresses a rich internal state, thousands of continuous numbers, down into one discrete token. That is a massive bottleneck. So the obvious optimization is to skip the words. Let the model pass its raw internal state forward instead of a token. That is neuralese: reasoning in vectors rather than sentences. It is faster and it carries far more information per step. Early research in this direction shows real gains. And the cost is that the transcript disappears. What you get instead is a sequence of vectors that mean nothing to a human reader. You cannot grep it. You cannot audit it. You cannot notice that step forty took an alarming turn. The transparency we currently enjoy is not a design choice anyone made. It is a lucky accident of the training setup, and neuralese is the thing that ends it.

The uncomfortable part is that nobody has to be reckless for this to happen. Neuralese wins on benchmarks and on cost. The window where a model's reasoning is legible to us is closing for ordinary competitive reasons, which is exactly the kind of problem that does not get solved by anyone deciding to be careful.

## If neuralese asks whether we can read what a model is thinking, this next paper asks a stranger question: whether every model is secretly thinking in the same shape. It is called Harnessing the Universal Geometry of Embeddings, and I would put it on the short list of the most interesting results of the last year.

Some background you need. Every AI system that handles text turns it into embeddings, lists of numbers where similar meanings land near each other. Each model builds its own. They have different dimensions, different training data, different vendors. An embedding from one model is conventionally gibberish to another. There is no shared dictionary. The researchers show that you can translate between them anyway, without any matched pairs. No examples of this vector here corresponds to that vector there. They train a translator using only unpaired collections of vectors from each side, and it lands close enough that you can take an embedding from one model and read it with another. The theory behind it is the Platonic Representation Hypothesis: that models trained on enough real-world text converge on roughly the same underlying geometry of meaning, regardless of architecture. Different coordinate systems, same territory. The paper is evidence that this is not just a nice idea. Now the part that matters commercially. An enormous number of companies have loaded their private documents into a vector database. The comfortable assumption has been that those vectors are safe, because they are just numbers and only our model knows what they mean. That assumption is gone. The authors demonstrate reconstructing meaningful content, including sensitive attributes, from embeddings alone, with no access to the model that produced them. Your vector store is not obfuscated. It is your documents in a different font.

Two directions out of one result. If you build AI infrastructure, universal translation between embedding spaces is a genuine gift. If you run security, go find out today whether your vector database is treated as sensitive data or as a cache. Almost everywhere I have seen, it is a cache.

## OpenAI published a piece about research acceleration, essentially an inside look at how the company uses its own models to do its own work. Take the marketing gloss off and there is a real claim in there worth arguing about.

The claim is that AI is now materially speeding up AI research itself, not in the science fiction sense of a model redesigning its successor, but in the grinding practical sense. Researchers describe handing off the parts of the job that were never the interesting part. Writing the data loader. Sweeping hyperparameters. Reading a paper from another subfield and reporting back what is useful. Building the analysis script for an experiment you are not sure will work. The honest version of the bottleneck in machine learning research was never brilliant ideas. It was that testing an idea took four days of unglamorous engineering, so you only tested the ideas you were fairly confident about. Cut that to four hours and you do not just work faster, you take more speculative shots, because a bad guess now costs an afternoon. That is a real change in how a research org behaves. The skepticism is equally real, and you should hold both. A company selling reasoning models telling you that reasoning models transformed its internal research is not a neutral report. There is no counterfactual lab where the same people worked the same year without the tools. And there is a known failure mode: generated code that runs fine and quietly implements the wrong thing, which in a research setting produces a result that looks publishable and is wrong. The people describing the speedup are also the people best equipped to catch that. Most teams adopting the same workflow are not.

My read is that the direction is correct and the magnitude is unverifiable. Useful anyway, because the mechanism they describe transfers. Anything where the idea is cheap and the setup is expensive is where these tools currently pay. That is not only AI research. That is most of engineering.

## Two posts on the front page today are about the same discomfort from opposite ends. One is a person who was asked to train the system built to do their job. The other is a blunt piece arguing that publishing text an AI wrote for you is a kind of self-exposure you have not noticed.

The first is a firsthand account, and its power is in a detail people skip past. This was not a layoff with a robot in the wings. It was a request to sit down and label, correct, and demonstrate, so a system could learn the judgment the writer had spent a career developing. The part that stings is that doing the work well makes the replacement better. Refusing means someone less careful trains it instead, and the thing ships anyway, worse. There is no version of the choice that protects the craft. Only versions that decide who gets to feel complicit. And the compensation question is left wide open: expertise transferred into a permanent asset, paid at an hourly rate, with no share of what it produces afterward. The second piece is meaner and funnier. The argument is that when you publish something a language model wrote, readers who know the tells can see it immediately, and what they see is not that you used a tool. It is a decision about what you thought was worth your attention. The line the author draws is between using a model to think and using one to avoid thinking. Nobody objects to spell check or to a model catching a broken argument. What lands badly is asking for someone's time to read three hundred words that cost you nothing to produce. The signal is not the prose quality. It is the asymmetry of effort.

Put them next to each other and there is one question underneath: what exactly are we paying for when we pay for expertise. The training story says we are paying for judgment, right up until we can copy it. The writing story says readers were always paying for attention, and they can tell when it was not spent.

## The legal front moved twice this week, in opposite directions, and together they show the whole arc of how this ends.

First, The Seattle Times and Newsday sued OpenAI, and named Microsoft as a co-defendant because Copilot runs on OpenAI's technology. Two allegations: that their journalism was used as training data without permission, and that the models reproduce passages of their reporting back to users. They join The New York Times, Ziff Davis, Merriam-Webster, Encyclopedia Britannica, and roughly four hundred local newspapers that recently filed. This is no longer a handful of large publishers testing a theory. It is an industry-wide posture, arriving the same week OpenAI began rolling out its newest model. Second, and more instructive, the fight over the Anthropic settlement. Anthropic agreed to a very large payout to authors over pirated books, the biggest copyright settlement of its kind. So the money exists. And now authors say publishers and literary agents are claiming a bigger share of it than they are owed. Read that carefully, because it is the preview. Contracts written for print and film rights do not have a clause for a machine learning settlement. Nobody drafted for this. So the question of who owns the training value of a book, the person who wrote it or the company that published it, is being settled after the fact, by whoever has the better lawyers and the more aggressive reading of a twenty-year-old agreement.

The lawsuits get the headlines and the split fight is where the actual rule gets made. Once money is on the table, the argument stops being about whether training on someone's work is fair and starts being about who gets the check. That is a much less romantic question, and a much more permanent one.

## Now to a corner of systems programming with a title I genuinely enjoyed: is there input-output after death, and what happens to io uring when a process dies. This is deep kernel material, and it is worth two minutes even if you never touch it.

Quick background. Io uring is the modern Linux interface for asynchronous input and output. Older approaches meant a system call for every read and write, and system calls are expensive. Io uring gives you two shared queues between your program and the kernel: you push requests onto one, the kernel puts results on the other, and at full tilt you can do real work without crossing into the kernel at all. It is one of the largest performance wins in Linux in a decade. The question the article asks is what happens when the process on one end of that arrangement simply dies. And the answer is more interesting than the tidy one. The kernel does not stop mid-flight. Operations already handed off keep running to completion, because interrupting a write halfway is worse than finishing it. There is no reader left for the results, so completions land in a queue nobody will ever look at. Cleanup is deferred rather than immediate, and if you configured a dedicated polling thread, that thread has its own lifecycle to unwind. The practical consequence is that killing a process is not the clean instantaneous boundary most of us picture. Work you thought was cancelled can still hit the disk. Resources can stay held briefly after the process is gone from your process list.

The general lesson outlives the specific interface. Every time we move work off the critical path for speed, we create a window where the thing that requested the work no longer exists but the work does. Shared memory queues, job runners, async everything. Same shape, different layer.

## Two shipping stories from the browser side, and they pair nicely because one is about making the web faster and the other is about how long faster actually takes.

Vidact is a compiler that takes React code and turns it into direct DOM operations. The pitch is that you keep writing components the way you already do, and the output skips the virtual DOM entirely, updating the page directly. This is the Svelte insight, applied to the ecosystem people are actually stuck in. The virtual DOM was never the goal. It was the price of a programming model where you describe the whole interface and let the framework diff it against last time. If a compiler can work out at build time exactly which text node changes when a variable changes, that diffing at runtime is pure overhead. The hard part, and the reason this is difficult rather than obvious, is that React is a general-purpose language runtime pretending to be a template system. Hooks, conditionals, dynamic composition. A compiler has to be right about all of it or you get subtle wrongness, which is worse than slow. Then the other post: it took a year to ship WebAssembly in Anubis. Anubis is the tool many sites now use to keep aggressive AI scrapers out, by making every visitor's browser solve a small proof-of-work puzzle first. Moving that puzzle from JavaScript to WebAssembly is, on paper, a straightforward speedup. In practice it took a year, and the write-up is about why: build tooling, browser differences, and the fact that a security-adjacent component running on every request has almost no tolerance for a regression.

That second post is the one I would send to a manager. The work was not hard because the idea was hard. It was hard because shipping into the real installed base is a completely different discipline from making it work on your laptop, and we consistently budget for the wrong one.

## A palate cleanser, and then a question I think a lot of you are quietly struggling with. First: making a Python interpreter in one thousand twenty-four bytes.

One kilobyte. That is smaller than the docstring on most functions you have written this year. Obviously it is not CPython. The exercise is to pick the smallest subset of Python that still deserves the name, then implement it with an obsessive attention to every byte. What makes this genuinely educational rather than merely cute is that extreme size limits force you to confront what a language actually needs. Parsing, an evaluation loop, variable bindings, some notion of scope. When you cannot afford a proper tokenizer, you learn exactly what a tokenizer was buying you. It is the same value as writing a toy compiler, compressed into an afternoon. And a related thread worth surfacing: someone asked how people manage their skills files. For those not deep in agent tooling, skills are reusable instruction files you hand a coding assistant so it knows your conventions, your build steps, your house style. The asker's framing was sharp. They said they expect skills to eventually be absorbed into model capability, but until then they want a better way to organize them. The replies circle a real problem. These files rot. They get copied between projects, they contradict each other, and there is no test suite that tells you a skill stopped working. You find out because the output got worse and you cannot say when.

The honest state of the art on skills is a folder, some copy-paste, and vibes. That is roughly where JavaScript dependency management sat in two thousand nine. Somebody is going to build the package manager for this, and they are going to do well.

## Apple corner, three items, and the first is a genuine milestone. Asahi Linux now officially supports the M three.

Asahi is the project reverse-engineering Apple silicon so Linux runs natively on it. Undocumented hardware, no vendor cooperation, an entire graphics stack written from scratch, and it produced a conformant OpenGL and Vulkan driver that in some cases beat what Apple ships. The M three support matters partly because of what the project has been through. It lost its founder in early twenty twenty five, and the usual outcome for a reverse-engineering effort that loses its central figure is a slow fade. Instead it kept shipping and closed the gap on a newer chip generation. That is a statement about the project's health more than about the chip. Second, a post that resonated with an enormous number of people: Finder is frustrating and has been since day one. The complaints are specific and old. Cut and paste for files that works differently from everywhere else. Sorting and view settings that will not stay put per folder. Search that defaults to your whole machine when you clearly meant this directory. A files-remaining estimate that has been a running joke for two decades. The interesting part is not the list, it is the stability of the list. These are not regressions. They are the same complaints from ten years ago, in an application every Mac user touches daily. Third, quickly: a Show HN for Kadō, an open-source habit tracker for iOS with a non-binary habit score. Instead of a checkbox, partial credit. Twenty minutes of a planned hour counts as twenty minutes, not a failed day.

The Kadō idea is smarter than it looks. Binary habit tracking punishes the partial effort exactly as hard as doing nothing, which teaches people that a compromised day is a wasted day. That is backwards, and it is the reason most streak apps end with a broken streak and a deleted app.

## Two endings and one beginning to close on, both about what happens when infrastructure changes hands.

A slash I is shutting down. Autistici and Inventati, the Italian collective that has run email, hosting, and privacy tools for activists and independent projects since the early two thousands. Volunteer-run, funded by donations, built on the conviction that political organizing needs infrastructure that is not owned by anyone with an incentive to hand over the logs. They survived a server seizure, years of hostile attention, and the entire era in which everyone else moved to platforms. Twenty-plus years of that is an extraordinary run, and a shutdown notice is still a shutdown notice. If you have data there, move it now, not after the deadline. And Travis Kalanick's new venture, Atoms, is reportedly looking at robotaxis. Kalanick has described Atoms as unfinished business, and the reference is not subtle. Self-driving was the strategic bet at Uber, and the founder left before it played out. What is genuinely different now is that the hard question has changed. When Kalanick left, autonomy was a research problem. Today several companies operate driverless commercial service in real cities. The remaining problem is capital, manufacturing, regulatory relationships in dozens of jurisdictions, and depot operations at scale. That is an operations and financing problem, which happens to be the specific thing Kalanick was actually extraordinary at, alongside everything else that ended that tenure.

One collective spends twenty years keeping a small piece of the internet independent and closes quietly. One founder returns to a market that has moved from science to logistics. Both stories are about the same thing: infrastructure eventually belongs to whoever can afford to run it.
