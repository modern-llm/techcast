# Nvidia's Board Weighs Its Founder, TSMC Buys In, and DeepMind Checks Signal's Math
*November 12, 2026*

Welcome to TechCast Daily for Thursday, November 12. Nvidia's board met in a hurry last night to decide whether Jensen Huang, the man who founded the company 33 years ago, keeps his job. We also have TSMC, the company that manufactures most of the world's advanced AI chips, buying into OpenAI and Anthropic, OpenAI passing ten billion dollars of revenue in one quarter, and Google DeepMind using AI to prove that parts of Signal's encryption do what they promise. Let's get into it.

## Nvidia first, and the news is a second relationship. A quick reminder: on Sunday Huang admitted an affair with an executive at an Nvidia customer whom US officials believe is a Chinese intelligence asset, and the board opened an investigation.

The new part comes from the Financial Times. Huang also had a romantic relationship with Kimberly Powell, Nvidia's vice president of healthcare. That changes the kind of problem the board has. An affair with an outsider is a question of judgment. A relationship with one of your own executives breaks the rules at nearly every large company, and there is precedent for what happens next. Intel's Brian Krzanich resigned over one in 2018. McDonald's fired Steve Easterbrook over one in 2019. Neither of them was also tied to a suspected spy. The board held urgent talks on Wednesday night, according to people familiar with the matter, and TechCrunch reported it could decide as soon as that evening whether to remove him. Nvidia's stock fell on the reports. Our sources end before any decision was announced.

Nvidia is a 4.2 trillion dollar company, and investors have long priced Huang and Nvidia as one thing. That is why a board would hesitate. It is also why a board may decide that keeping him is the bigger risk. If a decision lands, we will have it on the next show.

## Now money, and three numbers that belong together. One reminder first: this week Anthropic set the price range for its stock market debut.

TSMC, the Taiwanese company that manufactures the most advanced chips for Nvidia, Apple and nearly everyone else, has approved up to one billion dollars of investment in AI labs, according to Reuters. Half goes into OpenAI, as preferred stock, the class of shares reserved for large investors that gets paid back first if things go wrong. The other half buys ordinary Anthropic shares at whatever price its public debut sets. So the world's most important chipmaker will own a piece of both OpenAI and Anthropic. TSMC has made enormous profits from the AI data center boom. Now it gets a second way to win from the same boom, and a second way to lose. The Wall Street Journal said the deal could give markets a shot of confidence just as investors' bets on AI companies show signs of strain. The same report carried the warning that matters more. When suppliers invest in their customers, and customers buy from their investors, it becomes hard to tell how much of the demand is real. Nvidia has invested in AI labs that buy its chips. Labs have signed cloud deals with companies that own pieces of them. Every link adds revenue on paper. Nobody can easily say how much of it would exist if the money stopped going round. Here is a number from paying customers. OpenAI's sales passed ten billion dollars in a single quarter for the first time, according to a report. That is up almost fifty percent from 6.7 billion in the second quarter. Whatever you think of the spending, customers are paying OpenAI more, and fast. Then the other side. CoreWeave, which rents out data centers full of Nvidia chips to AI companies, reported its third quarter results, and they show how all that building gets paid for. CoreWeave is asking more customers to pay up front. It is borrowing against specific contracts, using one customer's promise to pay as the security for a loan. And it is looking at shorter contracts, which could let it charge more. Shorter contracts look odd for a company that needs years of certain income to pay off its debt. They make sense if CoreWeave thinks prices for computing will rise and does not want to be locked in at today's rates. They also mean less certain income, and lenders will notice. Asking for cash up front is the detail to watch. A company asks for money first when it needs money first. One last money item. Fractile, a British startup designing chips built only for running AI models, raised 720 million dollars. That puts it among the best-funded companies trying to take some of the market Nvidia now owns. We have only the headline figure, not the investors or the chip's performance.

Put the first three together. The labs are earning more. Their suppliers are buying into them. And the company in the middle, renting out the chips, wants its money sooner. All three can be true in a healthy boom. They can also all be true just before one ends.

## Now a security story with good news in it. Yesterday we asked who checks the answer when AI does math. Here is one case where a machine checks every step.

Google DeepMind researchers used AI agents to formally verify several of the key guarantees in the Signal protocol, Wired reports. The Signal protocol is the encryption design behind the Signal app, and also behind WhatsApp and Google Messages, so it protects a very large share of the world's private messages. Formal verification means writing a mathematical proof that the software does exactly what it promises, and then having a computer program check every step of that proof. A test can only show that the cases you tried worked. A proof covers every case. That is why security people have long called formal verification the holy grail. It is also rare, because writing these proofs by hand takes specialists months or years, even for small amounts of code. DeepMind's team did this as a trial project, in four weeks, using a new AI framework called AlphaProof Code. The name points back to AlphaProof, the DeepMind system that solved problems at the International Mathematical Olympiad. Two things come out of it. First, Signal's design gets another independent check, and it held up. That is good news if you use any of those apps. Second, it shows AI doing the slow, expert work that has kept formal verification out of most software. Now the limits, which Wired is honest about. Nobody knows yet how practical this will be at scale, or how well it works on large amounts of ordinary code. Signal's protocol is small, carefully designed, and already studied by cryptographers for a decade. Most software is none of those things. And a proof only covers what you asked it to prove. If the statement you wrote down misses a real attack, the proof will pass and the attack will still work.

My take: this is the most useful kind of AI result, because you do not have to trust the AI. The proof checker is ordinary software that people already trust. The AI only has to find a proof that passes it.

## Staying with DeepMind, a second release, this one about biology.

AlphaGenome 2 is the successor to AlphaGenome, the model DeepMind released last year to predict what changes in DNA actually do. The problem it works on is this. Nearly every cell in your body carries the same DNA. A heart muscle cell, a skin cell and an immune cell fighting an infection differ because each one switches different genes on and off. Those patterns also change as a cell responds to what is happening around it. So a single change in your DNA can be harmless in one kind of cell and cause disease in another. Most of the DNA changes linked to disease sit in the stretches that control those switches, not in the parts of genes that make proteins, which is why they are so hard to read. The new version, The Verge reports, looks at individual cell types, giving scientists a more detailed view of where a DNA change does its damage. The Verge summary we have does not include accuracy numbers or say who can use the model, so treat the claims as Google's for now.

This is where AI in biology pays off, not by finding a cure on its own, but by telling a lab which of thousands of DNA changes is worth an experiment. That can save years, if the predictions hold up.

## Now coding agents, and two pieces that answer the same question from opposite ends: what is an engineer for, once AI writes the code?

First, Amazon. The company published a post on how autonomous AI agents built an AWS emulator. An emulator here is software that imitates Amazon's cloud services on your own machine, so you can test a program against a fake copy of AWS instead of paying for, and possibly breaking, the real thing. Tools like that already exist, built by people over years, and they are never complete, because AWS has hundreds of services and each one has its own quirks. Amazon's point is that agents built this one. Werner Vogels, Amazon's chief technology officer, wrote a companion essay on his blog with the title AWS emulator and the engineer's job when agents write the code. We only have the titles and links, not the details, so we can't tell you how much human work went in, how complete the emulator is, or how it was checked. Those are the three questions to ask of any claim that agents built something big. Second, a talk posted to Hacker News called AI, activity, and outcomes in software engineering. Again, we have only the title and a video link. But the title alone names the problem. Activity is what is easy to measure: lines of code, pull requests, meaning proposed changes, and commits. Outcomes are what a business actually wants: software that works and keeps working. AI tools push activity up fast. Whether outcomes rise with it is the question companies keep failing to measure.

Put them side by side and Amazon's post is an activity claim until someone shows the outcome. I would like to see how many real bugs the emulator caught, or caused, for the teams using it.

## From agents that write code to one that reports to the boss. Databricks, the data and AI company, says its chief executive now learns what is going wrong from an AI, not from his staff.

Databricks has about 12,000 employees. Ali Ghodsi, its chief executive, admits he cannot know them all, and that they are unlikely to tell him what goes wrong. So he uses Genie, Databricks' agent for asking questions of company data in plain English, which the company has just revised. The answers come from the data itself, not from a report that has passed through several layers of managers on its way to him. There is a real idea here. Bad news gets softened as it travels up any big organization, and a tool that goes straight to the numbers skips that step. But note what it cannot do. An agent can only report what is in the data. If a project is failing because two teams do not trust each other, that rarely shows up in a database. And there is a less comfortable side. A tool that tells the boss what employees did not say is also a tool for watching them. Employees will learn quickly what Genie can see, and they will change what goes into the systems it reads.

Also remember who is telling this story. Databricks sells Genie, and a chief executive who says he relies on it is the best ad it could ask for.

## Agents on the customer side now, where one big company is saying no.

A Hacker News headline says United Airlines has banned AI agents from booking flights on behalf of travelers. We have only the headline, so we do not know how United will enforce it, whether it covers every agent or only some, or what happens to a ticket an agent already bought. The reasons are easy to guess, though. An airline makes money by showing you extra choices as you book: seat upgrades, bags, the slightly pricier fare. An agent skips all of that and buys the cheapest thing that fits your request. Airlines also already block programs that copy their fares in bulk, and an AI agent looks a lot like one of those programs. There is a customer service problem too. If an agent books the wrong date, who made the mistake, you or the AI, and who pays to fix it?

Expect more of these. Every company whose business depends on what you see while you shop has the same reason to keep agents out, or to charge them to come in.

## Three security headlines now. All three come with no details, so here is what each one means if it holds up, and what you can do.

First, a post reports a critical remote code execution flaw in the Apache HTTP Server. Remote code execution means an attacker can make your server run their own commands, over the network, which is about as bad as a bug gets. Apache is one of the oldest and most widely used web servers, so the number of exposed machines could be large. We do not know which versions are affected or whether a fix is out. If you run Apache, check the project's security announcements today and patch as soon as an update ships. Second, a post titled Lock Your GitHub Actions. GitHub Actions is the system that runs automated jobs on your code, like tests and releases. Those jobs often pull in small building blocks written by other people, and many projects point to them by a label that the author can move to new code at any time. If an attacker takes over one of those building blocks, every project using it runs the attacker's code, often with access to the project's secrets. Locking means pointing to one exact version instead, using the long code that identifies it and cannot be moved. We do not know what prompted this post, but the advice has been sound for years, and it takes an afternoon. Third, the loudest headline: Humble Bundle just killed most of their security through vibecoding. Vibecoding means building software by asking an AI for code and shipping what comes back without reading it closely. Humble Bundle sells games and books online, so it holds customer accounts and the game keys people paid for. We have no article behind the headline, no confirmation from the company, and no description of what broke. So treat it as a claim. But it fits a pattern from recent shows: AI coding tools are fast, and the security mistakes they make ship just as fast.

Of the three, the GitHub one is the job worth doing this week. It takes little time, and it blocks a kind of attack that has already leaked secrets from real projects, more than once.

## Now a run of tools that keep AI on your own machine. The first is a meeting note taker, the same kind of product as the Claude Meetings launch we covered yesterday.

It is called Yogurt, and it is a free, open-source alternative to Granola, a popular paid app that takes notes during your calls. Like Granola, Yogurt runs in the background and does not join the call as a bot. The difference is where your data goes. Yogurt runs small AI models on your own computer to transcribe the meeting, summarize it, and answer questions about it. Recordings, transcripts and summaries never leave the machine. Its developer built it as a side project and makes a fair point: you do not need a frontier model, meaning the biggest and most expensive AI, to summarize meeting notes. When you do want one, Yogurt runs a local MCP server. MCP is a standard way for AI assistants to reach your files and tools, so you can hand your notes to Claude or ChatGPT directly, without a paid service in the middle. It also connects to your calendar and needs no account. One caution: a free app that records every meeting still needs you to tell the other people on the call. Second, Atuin Threads. Atuin is a popular open-source tool that saves your terminal command history and syncs it across computers. Threads, by the headline, does the same for your AI agent sessions: every conversation with a coding agent, on every machine you use. If you switch between a laptop and a desktop, that is a real gap today, since most agents keep their history on the computer where the session ran. Third, headline only: Huawei has released MACaQ, the quantizer it uses internally for running AI locally. A quantizer shrinks a model by storing its numbers with less precision, so it fits in less memory and runs on ordinary hardware, usually at some cost in accuracy. We do not know how MACaQ compares with the free tools people already use for this. Huawei sells its own AI chips, so it has a reason to make models run well on hardware that is not Nvidia's.

The thread through all three: for everyday jobs like meeting notes, small models on your own machine are now good enough. That is bad news for any company whose whole product is a thin layer between you and a cloud model.

## Two last headlines, both with nothing behind them but the title.

The first is a paper called Attention to Mamba: A Recipe for Cross-Architecture Distillation. Some background. Almost every large AI model today is a transformer, built around a step called attention, which compares every word with every other word. That gets expensive as the text gets long, because the work grows with the square of the length. Mamba is a different design whose cost grows only in step with the length, so it handles long documents more cheaply. The catch is that the best trained models are transformers, and nobody wants to pay to train a big Mamba model from scratch. Distillation means training a new model to copy an existing one's answers. A reliable recipe for copying a transformer into a Mamba model would mean cheaper AI for long documents without starting over. We do not know how much quality the recipe loses, and that is the whole question. The second: a general-purpose Apple Silicon layer for x86-64 PCs. x86-64 is the chip family in ordinary Intel and AMD computers. Apple built Rosetta to run Intel programs on its own chips. This headline suggests the reverse, a layer that runs software built for Apple's chips on a normal PC. That would be hard, because Mac software also depends on Apple's graphics system and operating system, not just the processor. It is the kind of project that is either very impressive or quietly limited, and a title alone cannot tell you which.

Both are attempts to move work built for one design onto another without paying to rebuild it.

Behind it, watch the money: TSMC buying into its customers' customers, and CoreWeave asking for cash up front. And if you run Apache, go check for that patch. See you next time.
