# The Rewrite Test, the Trust List, and a Frog with a Habsburg Jaw
*August 03, 2026*

The person who built Claude Code pointed it at Anthropic's own consumer app and asked it to rewrite the thing. That experiment is a better evaluation than any leaderboard, and the failure modes are the interesting part. Also today: Sam Altman is now the one asking the industry to slow down, and the EU's age verification project just made a design choice that could quietly lock a whole class of devices out of the internet. It's Monday, August third, 2026. This is TechCast Daily.

## Boris Cherny is the person most associated with Claude Code, the terminal-based coding agent that converted a lot of skeptics into daily users over the last couple of years. So when they sit down to talk about trying to get Claude Code to rewrite the Claude app itself, that isn't a marketing anecdote. That's the hardest test the tool can be given, run by someone who knows exactly where the seams are.

Here's why this specific experiment matters more than any benchmark score. Almost every impressive coding-agent demo you've seen is greenfield. Build me a to-do app. Build me a landing page. Build me a Wordle clone. Those tasks share one property: there's no existing system whose constraints you have to respect. Nothing to break, no users, no unwritten invariants encoded in five years of bug fixes.

A production consumer app is the opposite. The codebase contains decisions nobody documented, made for reasons nobody remembers, that are still load-bearing. There's the thing the code does, and there's the thing the code is careful not to do, and the second one is almost never written down anywhere. A model reading that repository sees the first and is blind to the second. It will happily delete the weird retry loop that exists because one carrier's proxy drops connections at forty seconds.

The second problem is context at scale. Rewriting an app isn't one large task, it's ten thousand small decisions that all have to stay consistent with each other. Agents have gotten genuinely good at the individual decision. They're still weak at holding a coherent architectural position across a week of work, because there's nowhere persistent for that position to live except the code itself, and the code is the thing being rewritten.

In the same news cycle, Alibaba shipped Qwen three point eight Max, pitched as a new bar for coding and for what they're calling cowork. Take the framing seriously for a second, because the framing is the news: the frontier labs have stopped selling you a chat model and started selling you a colleague. But notice the mismatch. That claim is measured on benchmarks. The thing Cherny was testing is precisely the part benchmarks don't capture.

My read: treat any new coding-model release as a claim about the first ninety percent, and treat internal rewrite attempts as the only honest data on the last ten. When a lab publishes a candid retrospective about their own tool struggling with their own app, that's worth more than a leaderboard. Believe the postmortems, not the press releases.

## Sam Altman is calling on the industry to pace the rate of AI development. That's the subject of the latest Equity episode, and it deserves more than an eye roll, though an eye roll is a reasonable first reaction.

Let's be precise about who's saying what. Altman runs the company that did more than any other to compress the timeline. The strategic thesis of OpenAI's last several years has been that capability gets shipped and society adapts around it. So a call for pacing is either a genuine update or it's positioning. Probably some of both.

The charitable read: the people closest to these systems have the best view of where the capability curve is bending, and if that view has gotten uncomfortable, saying so publicly is correct even when it's late and self-serving. Leaders who wait until the discomfort is obvious to everyone else have waited too long.

The cynical read is the one worth understanding mechanically, because it isn't only cynicism, it's a known dynamic. When a company has a lead, slowing the field is worth more to them than to anyone else. Pacing rhetoric converts naturally into compliance requirements, licensing, evaluation regimes, reporting obligations. Every one of those carries a fixed cost that a large lab absorbs and a startup can't. So a sincere safety concern and a competitive moat can generate identical policy asks, and you cannot tell them apart from the outside by reading the speech. You tell them apart by what the speaker gives up.

The test is simple. Does the pacing call come with a unilateral commitment that costs this company something specific and verifiable, with a date attached? Or is it a call for everyone to slow down together, enforced by a body that doesn't exist yet? The first is a decision. The second is a press cycle.

There's a related post circulating today called Don't be a meat proxy, and the title carries the argument. It's about the failure mode where a person becomes a dumb relay between a model and other humans, pasting output they haven't evaluated into places where their judgment was supposed to be the value. That's the individual-scale version of exactly the question Altman is asking at industry scale: at what point does moving fast mean you've stopped being the one deciding.

The connection isn't decorative. Both are about where human judgment sits in the loop. And in both cases the useful answer isn't slow down in the abstract. It's name the specific decision you're still going to make yourself, and be willing to be slower in order to make it.

## Three items on the list today are really one item, and it's about what happens to evaluation when the public benchmarks stop being trustworthy.

Karpathy posted about a pelican. Separately, someone published a piece titled: my personal AI benchmark, generate an SVG of a frog with a Habsburg jaw. Neither of these is a paper. Both are more informative than most papers.

Here's the logic behind the genre. Public benchmarks leak. Once a test set exists on the open web, some version of it ends up in training data, directly or through contamination in scraped discussion about it. The score goes up, the underlying capability may not, and you can't easily tell which happened. So people build private, weird, un-Googleable tests instead.

The SVG genre in particular is well chosen, and it's worth understanding why. Asking a model to emit SVG for a pelican riding a bicycle, or a frog with a Habsburg jaw, forces something specific. The model has to hold a spatial and anatomical concept and express it as coordinates in text, with no visual feedback loop. It can't pattern-match an image, because it isn't producing pixels. It's producing a program that produces a picture. The Habsburg frog adds compositionality on top: two concepts that essentially never co-occur in any training corpus, which means the model has to actually combine them rather than retrieve them.

Then at the exact opposite end of the spectrum, somebody got an autoregressive language model running on the sixty-five oh two, the eight-bit processor from nineteen seventy-five that powered the Apple two and the Commodore sixty-four. This is a toy. It's also a genuinely useful corrective. The architecture is not complicated. Multiply, add, softmax, sample, repeat. Everything that makes modern models remarkable is scale, data, and engineering discipline wrapped around a mathematically small idea. When people start describing these systems as alchemy, the sixty-five oh two demo is a good thing to remember.

Keep a private benchmark. Genuinely. Three or four tasks from your actual work, written down in a file, rerun against every new model release. It costs ten minutes and it will tell you more than any vendor chart, because it's the one test that has never been in anybody's training set.

## There's a post going around arguing that developers are attached to their tools because tools encode trust. It reads like a throwaway line. I think it's the most useful frame of the day, and two other stories today are basically case studies for it.

The claim is this. When people defend an editor, a language, or a framework, the surface argument is about features, but what's actually being defended is calibration. Years of use teach you what a tool does when things go wrong. You know which error message is real and which is noise. You know which failures are yours and which are the tool's. That knowledge isn't transferable and it doesn't show up in a feature comparison, which is why tool arguments always look disproportionate to bystanders. People aren't arguing about tabs. They're arguing about how much of their hard-won intuition is about to be invalidated.

Now, SwiftUI at seven years. Seven years is long enough that the early-adopter excuses have expired. And the interesting question isn't whether SwiftUI is good, it's whether developers have finished calibrating to it. The persistent complaint about SwiftUI has always been unpredictability: layouts that don't behave the way the mental model says they should, performance cliffs that are hard to attribute, behavior that shifts across OS versions. That's a trust problem, not a feature problem. A framework with fewer features and predictable failure modes is easier to trust than a framework with more features and surprising ones. Apple shipped a declarative paradigm before the implementation underneath it was complete, and the bill has been paid in developer calibration, annually, for seven years.

Which brings us to the myth of Snow Leopard. Mac OS ten point six is remembered as the release where Apple shipped no new features and just fixed everything. The revisionist case, and I think it's right, is that this memory is largely constructed. Snow Leopard contained substantial new engineering, and it shipped with plenty of its own bugs. What people are actually remembering is a stretch where the platform stopped moving fast enough that their calibration caught up. Stability isn't the absence of change. It's change slow enough that trust can accumulate.

That's the real argument underneath every please just fix the bugs post. Nobody actually wants a release with nothing in it. They want the rate of change to drop below the rate at which they can rebuild their mental model. Which, if you're keeping track, is the Altman argument again, one layer down.

## Three language stories today, and together they map out the range of what people are still trying to do with programming languages in twenty twenty-six.

F star, spelled with an asterisk, is a proof-oriented language out of Microsoft Research, and the important thing about it is that it isn't academic in the dismissive sense. It has shipped. The verified cryptography written in F star, the HACL star project, has ended up in Firefox, in the Linux kernel, and in Windows. Real code, running on your machine right now, whose correctness was proven rather than tested.

The core idea is that types carry proofs. You don't just declare that a function takes an integer, you declare that it takes an integer and returns a value satisfying some property, and the compiler refuses your implementation until it can verify the claim. For most software that overhead isn't worth it. For parsers, for crypto primitives, for anything where an off-by-one becomes remote code execution, it's one of the very few techniques that eliminates a class of bug rather than reducing its frequency.

Then there's Fuse, a Show HN from a developer who has spent five years building a statically typed, purely functional language. It's written in Scala, started from System F as presented in Types and Programming Languages, and extended out into bidirectional type checking with higher-rank polymorphism. It compiles through GRIN, a whole-program optimizer, down to LLVM native code. The stated goal is Rust's ergonomics, algebraic data types, traits, impl blocks, pattern matching, but with pure functional semantics and no mutation.

I want to flag the number, because the number is the story: five years, solo, on type theory. Language design remains one of the last corners of software where one sufficiently stubborn person can still produce something with real depth.

And on the community side, the Rust project published its All Hands twenty twenty-six retrospective. These are worth reading even if you never write Rust, because Rust is the largest ongoing experiment in governing a language through a foundation and volunteer teams rather than a corporate owner. The recurring themes in that experiment are the ones every large open source project eventually hits: maintainer capacity, decision latency, and the gap between what companies depend on and what they actually fund.

Fuse and F star are the two poles of language design. One asks how expressive can I make this, the other asks how much of this can I prove. Nearly every language you use in production is a compromise between them, usually arrived at by accident. It's worth occasionally checking what the ends of the spectrum have figured out.

## Three Show HN projects today, and they share a theme: each one refuses a boundary that a vendor drew.

First, Syncular, offline-first SQL sync with TypeScript and Rust cores. Local-first is one of those ideas that's obviously correct and brutally hard. Obviously correct because latency to a local database is zero and your app keeps working on a plane. Brutally hard because the moment two devices can both write, you have a distributed systems problem, and the specific problem is that SQL was never designed for one. Relational integrity assumes a single authoritative ordering of writes. Offline editing breaks that assumption at the root. Foreign keys and uniqueness constraints are exactly the things that don't merge cleanly. So the question to ask about any project in this space isn't does it sync, it's what does it do when two devices both insert a row with the same unique key. That's where these libraries live or die, and it's the first thing I'd go look at.

Second, NixOS on the DGX Spark. NVIDIA's small desktop AI box ships with a vendor-blessed OS image, and if you've ever tried to reproduce a CUDA environment on ARM you know why that's simultaneously convenient and infuriating. Convenient because it works. Infuriating because it works for exactly one configuration, and the moment you deviate you're hand-resolving driver and toolkit versions at midnight. Nix is the right shape of answer: declare the environment, get the same environment every time, roll back cleanly when it breaks. The project ships USB images and a NixOS module tuned for the Spark, and it also covers the Asus Ascent GX ten.

Third, Kakehashi, an experimental userspace for running macOS binaries on Linux ARM. Think Wine, but for Darwin, and on ARM instead of x86. The reason this is newly plausible is Apple Silicon. For years, macOS on anything else meant emulating x86 as well as reimplementing the OS. Now the instruction set matches commodity ARM hardware, so the hard part shifts to reimplementing Darwin system calls and frameworks. That's an enormous amount of work, but it's a tractable kind of work, which is a meaningful difference.

The through-line: your data shouldn't require their server, your driver stack shouldn't require their disk image, and their binaries shouldn't require their hardware. None of these three will be smooth. Attempting them at all is the point.

## This next one got a modest score and deserves a much bigger one, because it's the story on today's list with the longest tail. The EU's age verification project has specified hardware-bound attestation.

Strip the jargon. Your device holds a private key inside a secure element, a chip the operating system itself cannot extract that key from. When a website asks whether you're over eighteen, your device doesn't merely assert it. It produces a cryptographic statement signed by that hardware key, proving the claim came from a device the verifier considers legitimate.

Now the consequence, which is the entire story. Somebody has to decide which devices count as legitimate. On the phones people actually own, that decision is made by two companies. On Android it flows through Google's Play Integrity infrastructure. On iPhone it flows through Apple's attestation services. So a rule that reads as prove you're an adult becomes, in implementation, prove you're running an operating system Google or Apple has blessed.

That excludes a specific and non-hypothetical set of people. De-Googled Android builds. GrapheneOS and similar hardened forks, which are used disproportionately by journalists, activists, and abuse survivors, which is to say precisely the users the EU generally says it wants to protect. Linux phones. Rooted devices. Older hardware that's aged out of attestation support. Every one of those users fails the check not because they're underage, but because their computer isn't on the list.

There's a real irony in the design. The original promise of the EU approach was privacy-preserving age proof using zero-knowledge techniques, so a site learns you're over eighteen and nothing else. That part is genuinely good cryptography and worth defending. Hardware binding got added to close an obvious hole, an adult minting tokens and handing them to minors. But it closes that hole by installing a gatekeeper who decides which computers may participate in the network, which is a far larger power than the problem required. And note that this is the same fight as Web Environment Integrity, which the web rejected loudly two years ago, now arriving through a different door with child safety attached to it.

If you care about this, the thing to push on isn't the age check. It's the trust list. Ask who maintains it, what the appeals process is, and whether an operating system built by people with no commercial relationship to Google or Apple can get onto it. If there's no answer to that third question, the policy has quietly picked winners in a market it was never supposed to be regulating.

## Two consumer stories to close, and both are really about timing.

First, foldables. The Verge's argument is that the category has become boring, and that this is excellent news for Apple. Remember how it started. Royole, now bankrupt, shipped the first commercial folding phone. Samsung's first Galaxy Fold followed and review units broke within days. Seven years on, creases are faint, hinges survive pockets, and the software mostly knows what to do with a second screen. Boring means solved.

Solved is Apple's ideal entry condition. This is the pattern with the music player, the phone, the tablet, and the watch. Apple is almost never first. Apple arrives after somebody else has absorbed the cost of discovering the failure modes, then wins on execution and integration. A foldable market where the physics is settled is a market where Apple's genuine advantages, supply chain leverage and control of the operating system, count for the most.

The counterpoint worth holding onto: when a category becomes boring it also becomes a commodity, and commodities compete on price. Apple does not compete on price. So the bet has to be that a premium segment will pay for the last ten percent of refinement, which, historically, is a bet Apple wins.

Second, the artist question. There's a wave of AI startups, Pippa among them, positioning themselves as the ethical option by paying artists for training data rather than scraping it. The reporting asks whether paying is enough to bring artists on board, and I think that framing needs sharpening, because two separate objections keep getting collapsed into one. The first is consent and compensation, which money genuinely does address. The second is substitution: a model trained on your work competes with you for the next commission. Payment doesn't touch that at all. It only determines whether you got a check on the way out the door.

So the structural question is the one to watch. Is it a one-time licensing fee, or an ongoing share tied to usage? A one-time fee buys your career outright. A revenue share makes you a stakeholder in the thing replacing you. Those are very different products wearing the same ethical label.

Watch which model the ethical startups actually settle on once licensing costs start to bite. That's where you find out whether the ethics was a principle or a market position.

That's the show. If you take one thing from today, take the private benchmark idea. Three or four prompts drawn from your own real work, saved in a file, rerun on every model release. Ten minutes of effort, and it makes you effectively immune to an entire industry's worth of marketing.

Two things I'd keep an eye on. The EU attestation spec, because the trust list question is going to get answered quietly in a working group, and it's far easier to fight before it ships than after. And whatever the next candid engineering writeup is about pointing a coding agent at a real production codebase, because that's where the actual capability curve is visible, and it's nowhere near the leaderboards.
