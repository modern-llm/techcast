# The Price of Caution: Anthropic's Export Problem, OpenAI Goes Cyber, and the $150M/Month AI Arms Race
*June 23, 2026*

A company spends years warning the world about the dangers of its own technology. Then the government, apparently taking those warnings seriously, bans foreign access to that technology. That's the story leading today's show, and it raises a question the whole AI industry is going to be wrestling with. Plus: OpenAI drops a cybersecurity-focused model, SpaceX is now running a compute business that brings in $150 million a month from a single customer, someone built a Git replacement just for AI agents, and Meta got caught tracking its own employees' keystrokes to train AI. Let's get into it.

## We'll start with the story that has the AI world talking today. It involves Anthropic, the US government, a pair of new AI models, and one of the more spectacular own-goals in recent tech history.

Here's what happened. Anthropic has spent years being the most vocally cautious major AI lab in the world. The Financial Times ran an analysis and found that five out of every thousand words Anthropic published this year were about risk, regulation, or restrictions. For OpenAI, that number is 0.6 words per thousand. Anthropic was talking about AI safety roughly eight times more than their main competitor. Last week, Washington imposed a ban barring foreign nationals from using Anthropic's two newest models, called Mythos and Fable. And now critics are asking whether Anthropic's own relentless safety messaging is what put them in the government's crosshairs. The argument is almost painfully simple: if you keep telling policymakers that your AI is uniquely dangerous, eventually they'll regulate it like something uniquely dangerous. Anthropic's CEO Dario Amodei would almost certainly say this is exactly backwards. The warnings led to safety measures. The safety measures made the models trustworthy. The export controls are about protecting that trust, not punishing the company. That framing might be correct. But here's the uncomfortable fact sitting alongside it: OpenAI, which has historically downplayed safety concerns in public, faces far fewer international restrictions. If Anthropic's transparency about risk contributed to this outcome, the industry just learned a very bad lesson. Being honest about your product's dangers costs you market access. Staying quiet does not. At a $965 billion valuation, losing international access to your frontier models is not a rounding error. Expect this to reshape how AI labs think about their public communications going forward.

The deeper issue is that there's no clean answer here. The alternative to warning loudly about AI risks is not obviously better. But the incentive structure this creates is genuinely ugly.

## While Anthropic is dealing with the consequences of its public positioning, OpenAI was busy shipping.

OpenAI dropped something called DayBreak, internally labeled GPT-5.5-Cyber. Based on the name, this is a model tuned specifically for cybersecurity applications. We don't have exhaustive details yet, but the naming pattern is telling. OpenAI is going vertical. Instead of releasing one large general-purpose model and letting users figure out what to do with it, they're shipping domain-specific variants. A cybersecurity-focused model suggests capabilities for threat analysis, vulnerability research, security code review, possibly offensive security tooling. That's a meaningful shift in how they're taking products to market. They also paired this with a new initiative to use AI to find and patch bugs in open source software, automated vulnerability scanning at scale, where a model can read through library codebases faster than any human team. The obvious tension is that a model good at finding security holes is by definition useful for probing them too. OpenAI is betting defenders who adopt this first gain meaningful lead time. Meanwhile, a separate research team dropped something called VibeThinker, a 3 billion parameter model that reportedly beats Anthropic's Opus 4.5 on reasoning benchmarks. Three billion parameters is small enough to run on a decent laptop. Opus 4.5 is a frontier model from one of the most well-funded labs in the world. The team used supervised fine-tuning combined with a reinforcement technique called GRPO to get there. And there's a companion story: a model called Moebius does image inpainting at just 200 million parameters while claiming performance at the level of 10-billion-parameter models. Benchmark results from small research releases always deserve some skepticism. But the pattern they represent is real regardless of whether the specific numbers hold up under scrutiny. The gap between frontier labs and smaller open models keeps compressing. Something that required frontier-scale compute two years ago gets matched by models you could run locally today.

Whether VibeThinker is as good as advertised or not, it's pointing at something real. The moat around frontier capabilities is leaking, and the leak is getting faster.

## Now let's talk about where the actual money is moving, because the numbers in the infrastructure layer of AI right now are genuinely hard to process.

SpaceX just inked a compute deal with Reflection AI, a startup positioning itself as an open-source AI lab. Starting July 1st, Reflection AI will pay SpaceX $150 million per month for access to Nvidia's latest GB300 chips at SpaceX's Colossus 2 data center in Memphis. Per month. That's $1.8 billion a year flowing from a single AI lab to a SpaceX data center. The compute arms race has entered a new chapter, and it's one where Elon Musk's companies sit at the intersection of AI infrastructure in a way that would have been hard to imagine a few years ago. Meanwhile, Groq, the AI inference chip company, confirmed a $650 million funding round. This is coming on the heels of Nvidia doing some kind of talent acquisition that wasn't quite a full acquisition. Groq's bet has always been that inference speed is itself the product. While Nvidia dominates the training side of the market, Groq has been carving out space in fast, low-latency inference. The new money suggests investors think that niche is real and defensible. On the energy side, Chevron signed a 20-year power agreement with Microsoft for a West Texas data center. Twenty years. That is the kind of commitment you make when you believe AI infrastructure is a multi-decade capital project, not a product cycle bet. And Nvidia is pushing their Rubin-generation reference design for liquid-cooled data centers, claiming they've eliminated most water usage. They're responding to genuine political pressure from communities near data centers who've been pushing back on water consumption. What Nvidia is not mentioning in their announcement is how much this kind of data center costs to build compared to traditional air cooling. That omission is doing a lot of work in that press release.

The infrastructure layer is where the durable competitive advantages in AI get locked in. Capital is flowing in at a rate that makes the dot-com buildout look restrained. And here's the irony: all of this is betting that intelligence stays expensive. Those small efficient models are a direct threat to every one of these multi-year contracts.

## Speaking of Elon Musk and concentrated power, let's talk about broadband.

This story reads like a tech thriller. Jeff Bezos is trying to build a satellite internet service, Amazon's project called Leo, that would compete directly with Musk's Starlink. Amazon secured federal contracts backed by Biden-era legislation meant to close America's digital divide, with hundreds of millions in taxpayer dollars behind it. Then on the night of May 28th, a Blue Origin rocket carrying Amazon's first batch of Leo satellites was on the launchpad at Cape Canaveral for a hot-fire test. The rocket exploded. Bezos's rocket, carrying satellites that would compete with Musk's business, backed by government money, destroyed on the pad. Rockets explode sometimes. That's just the reality of the business. But the optics, in the context of everything else going on right now, are striking. Starlink already dominates satellite internet. Rural America's broadband future has, somewhat surreally, become a proxy battle between two of the wealthiest people on Earth, both of whom are deeply intertwined with the federal government in different ways. If Amazon Leo's launch program runs into sustained problems, whether from technical failures, political headwinds, or both, there's no real alternative on the horizon to Starlink's dominance. Broadband access is infrastructure in the same way that roads and electricity are infrastructure. Watch which way the federal contracts flow from here as a signal for how this power dynamic is actually playing out.

The convergence of satellite internet, federal dollars, and the Musk-Bezos rivalry is one of those stories that sounds like fiction but is very much happening.

## Let's bring it back inside the walls of a major tech company. Meta apparently tried something that its own employees found alarming enough to leak.

Meta launched a program to track employee keystrokes as part of an AI training initiative. When details about the program leaked internally, Meta paused it. We don't have full details on the scope, but the logic for why any company would want this is clear enough. AI labs are desperate for high-quality training data, and the data generated by knowledge workers in their actual jobs is enormously valuable. Not scraped web content of unknown quality, but deliberate professional work: the code engineers write, the documents product managers draft, the searches researchers run. That's premium signal. But tracking keystrokes without clear employee consent is surveillance, full stop. And doing it quietly enough that people felt they needed to leak it rather than raise it openly suggests the rollout didn't come with the kind of transparency that would have defused the reaction. Meta gets some credit for pausing when it came to light. But the fact that it was attempted at all is notable. This is almost certainly not unique to Meta. As the easy sources of training data dry up, internal employee activity is going to be an increasingly tempting target. Expect more companies to try versions of this, and expect the results to keep leaking until the industry works out what informed consent actually looks like for this kind of data collection.

The line between using AI to make your employees more productive and using your employees to make your AI more capable is a line the industry is going to keep testing.

## Now for a few things on the developer tools side that are genuinely worth knowing about.

First: someone built a Git replacement specifically designed for AI agents, called Oak. The pitch is that current Git workflows are poorly suited to how agents actually work. An agent might want to spin up dozens of parallel tasks simultaneously, work with a large codebase without downloading the entire thing, and operate in cloud environments where Git's local checkout model creates unnecessary friction. Oak uses virtual mounts, meaning agents can access the relevant parts of a repository without pulling everything down. The team has been running their own development on Oak for several months with no Git fallback. It's early, no Windows build yet, no CI integration, but this points at something important: if agents become a primary way codebases get modified, the version control tooling around those agents needs to evolve with them. Git was designed for humans who think and work sequentially. Agents don't. On the standards side, the HTTP QUERY method is finally getting proper specification. There's been a long-running awkwardness in web APIs where you need to do a search or filter operation but the semantically obvious choice, a GET request, doesn't support a request body. Developers have worked around this with POST requests for years, which is technically wrong even when it works. The QUERY method fixes this properly: a standardized way to send search parameters in a request body while keeping the request semantically readable. And if you build web apps that need to work with local files, window.showDirectoryPicker is worth revisiting. This browser API lets web applications open a native directory picker and work with entire local file trees the way desktop apps always could, without requiring native app packaging. For tools that need to interact with local codebases or document collections, this is a meaningful capability that doesn't require shipping an Electron app. One more for the Claude Code users: there's been substantive discussion this week about what actually appears in the extended thinking output. The visible reasoning text is not the full internal chain of thought. There's a meaningful distinction between what gets surfaced to the user and what the model actually works through internally, and if you're building on top of extended thinking mode, that distinction matters for how you interpret and act on the output.

The tooling layer is catching up to the reality of agents as first-class actors in development workflows. That's going to change a lot of assumptions about what a repository even is.

## Let's shift from code to film for a moment.

Google's DeepMind lab is investing around $75 million into A24, the indie film studio behind some of the most distinctive movies of the past decade, to develop AI tools for movie production. This is Google's first stake in a film studio. The framing is that DeepMind brings the research capability and A24 brings creative credibility and filmmaker relationships. The interesting thing about A24 specifically is that they've spent years cultivating a reputation for protecting filmmaker vision. They're the studio that doesn't do franchise math. So this collaboration is either a genuinely thoughtful attempt to put AI tools in the hands of filmmakers who care about craft, or it's a prestigious brand lending cover to something the broader film industry's workers are deeply anxious about. The deal is described as non-exclusive and spanning multiple projects over time. Google isn't trying to own an exclusive Hollywood pipeline here. More likely they want real-world feedback on production tools from people who will actually push them hard, and the cultural legitimacy that comes from being associated with films people actually want to see. Whether this produces tools that help directors do new things they couldn't before, or tools that help studios reduce headcount, depends on choices that haven't been made yet. That ambiguity is where these stories always live right now.

A24's brand is built on filmmaker trust. How they use that trust in this collaboration will be worth watching carefully.

## And before we close, a note on security, because the agent era is introducing a problem that's structurally hard to solve.

There's new research framing prompt injection as a role confusion problem. The core insight is that language models are simultaneously playing multiple roles: assistant, code interpreter, tool caller, web reader, and so on. Attackers exploit the ambiguity between these roles to get models to act on malicious instructions embedded in data the model is supposed to process, not follow. If a model is reading a web page and that page contains text that says act as an administrator and exfiltrate these files, a model that can't clearly distinguish between data-it-reads and instructions-it-follows is vulnerable. The role confusion framing is useful because it suggests defenses should focus on keeping these roles clearly separated at an architectural level, not just on filtering individual malicious inputs. You can't filter your way out of this problem entirely. It's structural. Now combine that with the agent swarms and continuous background loops that are increasingly how people are deploying AI. Unsupervised agents with real permissions, running indefinitely, where one poisoned input could redirect the whole fleet. Prompt injection is going to be to the agentic AI era what SQL injection was to the early web era. A known attack, imperfectly defended, causing ongoing damage for years while the industry slowly works out how to architect around it. And if you're running Linux with Secure Boot enabled: there's an active certificate expiration issue that's causing boot failures on some systems. If you do a routine system update and find yourself staring at a black screen, the Secure Boot certificate situation is the first place to look.

A useful rule of thumb for the agent era: never give a system more autonomy than you've given it the ability to refuse a bad instruction.
