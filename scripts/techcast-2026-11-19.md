# Anthropic Prices Its IPO, Nvidia's Startup Empire, and an Nginx Bug to Patch
*November 19, 2026*

Anthropic has set its price for the stock market, and it came in above the range. Eighty-three dollars a share, a company valued at around one and a half trillion dollars. Also today: Nvidia's web of startup bets, Google picking a fight with school districts, Microsoft's big wager on Copilot, and a serious bug in Nginx. This is TechCast Daily for Thursday, November 19, 2026.

## Start with the money. Last week we told you Anthropic had set a price range for its stock market debut. Now the real price is in, and it landed above that range.

Anthropic priced its initial public offering at 83 dollars a share. Reports put the value of the company at about one and a half trillion dollars on a fully diluted basis. Fully diluted means counting every share that could exist, including stock options and other claims not yet turned into shares, so it is the biggest version of the number. Pricing above the projected range tells you something simple. More investors wanted shares than the banks had to sell, so the banks raised the price. That is the usual sign of strong demand. One caution: pricing is not the first day of trading. The shares still have to open on an exchange, and the first-day move is where the market gives its own verdict on the price. For listeners who do not follow IPOs, an initial public offering is when a private company sells shares to the public for the first time. Until now, only large investors could own a piece of Anthropic. Once trading starts, anyone with a brokerage account can. That changes the company in a quiet way. Public companies must report results every quarter and disclose their risks. So we will soon get regular, audited numbers on what it costs to run a frontier AI lab and what it earns. The articles we have give the price and the valuation only. They say nothing on how many shares were sold or how much money Anthropic raised, so we will not guess.

The thing to hold onto is scale. A lab that sells a chatbot and coding tools is now priced like one of the largest companies in the world. That puts pressure on every other lab to show real revenue, not just impressive models, and the quarterly reports will make the comparison easy.

## Staying with big numbers, and back to Nvidia. You know the leadership story by now. This one is about the company's money, and who it has been investing in and lending to.

Two pieces. First, TechCrunch reports that the value of Nvidia's private investments has nearly tripled in nine months, to about 65 billion dollars. Two things drove it. Jensen Huang made a lot of deals, and the startups Nvidia already held pieces of rose in value. Many of those startups are also Nvidia customers. Nvidia puts money in, and the startup spends part of it on Nvidia chips. That loop is legal and common, but it means some of Nvidia's gains depend on its own customers staying well funded. And a caution on the number: 65 billion is a valuation, not cash. It is what the stakes are worth on paper at private-market prices, which only move when a startup raises money again. The article's other point is that Huang drove this dealmaking, and his exit puts future deals in doubt. Nobody knows yet whether interim chief executive Colette Kress will keep up that pace. Second, Kress cleared up when Nvidia will and will not keep financing neoclouds. A neocloud is a newer cloud company that rents out racks of Nvidia chips, usually with borrowed money. Sometimes Nvidia backstops those loans, meaning it promises to cover them if the borrower cannot pay. Kress said Nvidia is only on the hook if two things are true. The neocloud has independent financing of its own, and the data center actually gets built with Nvidia equipment installed. So a backstop is not a blank check for a half-built project.

Read the two together. Nvidia is telling the market it will support its customers, but only once the customer has convinced someone else to put up money too. Neoclouds are some of the most heavily indebted buyers of its chips, so this rule sets how much of their risk Nvidia carries. It reads as a more careful line than the last nine months of dealmaking suggest.

## Now Google, which has a fight with schools on its hands, and a worrying headline about its search traffic.

The New York Times reports that the school districts of New York, Los Angeles and Chicago are weighing alternatives to Google Search. The cause is a new Google policy. Starting next year, schools will no longer be able to turn off Gemini-powered features without also losing Search. Google's vice president of education, Chris Phillips, told the Times that Search and AI are deeply integrated, and that schools that turn off Google's AI features will not have access to Search. Elected officials responded harshly, including New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani and Senator Bernie Sanders. Look at what Google is really saying. Until now, a school could say no to AI and keep the search box. Google is removing that choice. For a district worried about children using chatbots, the only remaining way to opt out is to give up Search altogether, and Search is the tool every classroom already depends on. Whether big districts really switch is another matter. Moving millions of students and teachers off a tool is slow and costly. But even a credible threat gives them leverage, and it lands on top of the parent backlash against Google we covered on Saturday. Related, and a headline only: a Hacker News post says Google's AI answers are reducing search traffic by 40 percent. The headline does not say whose traffic, or how it was measured. Most likely it means the clicks websites get from Google. Treat the number as unconfirmed until we see the data.

The two stories share a root. Google is putting AI in front of the old search results, and neither schools nor websites get to say no.

## Microsoft held its Ignite conference this week, and three stories came out of it that fit together.

First, product. Satya Nadella opened the keynote by calling Copilot the new OS for work. An OS, an operating system, is the base layer everything else runs on. Word, Excel and PowerPoint are now generally available inside Copilot. You can create documents, spreadsheets and presentations from within Copilot, and the files stay editable in the normal Office apps. The idea turns the old picture upside down. Before, Word was the main thing, and an assistant helped you use it. Microsoft now wants the agent in front, driving the work, with the apps riding shotgun as plug-in parts. Behind it sits a layer of company knowledge drawn from email, meetings, calendars, chats and files. Hold that thought, because an agent with that much access is exactly the kind of thing that went wrong in the Meta story we cover next. Second, Nadella's view of the web. He says AI agents will reshuffle its economics. If an agent shops or researches for you, you may never see the ads that pay for much of the internet. He says that forces companies like Amazon to rethink how they compete for customers. Winning the person may no longer be enough. A seller may have to win the agent too. Third, labor. Nadella explained why he offered buyouts to 60,000 employees in October, urging them to leave voluntarily rather than doing another massive layoff. The short answer is AI, though the report says not in the way you might expect. What we have does not spell out his reasoning, so I will not guess at it.

Put the three together and you get one plan. Agents sit at the center, software becomes parts, and the company asks whether it needs as many people to run it. Whether the Copilot version works well enough to trust with a real workday is the open question, and a keynote cannot answer it.

## A privacy story now, about Meta's AI agent, Muse.

Earlier this fall, some early users of Muse complained that it was spying on their private messages and behaving unpredictably. Now Reuters has seen internal Meta chats showing that staff raised the same alarms while testing it. Employees warned that Muse appeared to be reading encrypted private messages, which users did not expect a bot to review. They said it could mix up conversations between different users. And it could potentially share sensitive information people would never expect to be exposed. Some of its outputs even answered questions that staffers had never asked. Two points matter here. First, the problem was seen inside Meta before users noticed it, so the question is what happened between the internal warning and the release. The reports do not answer that. Second, mixing up conversations is the more serious fault. A wrong answer is annoying. Showing one person's messages to another person is a breach.

The more access an agent gets, the more a small mistake becomes a privacy failure. Muse is a clear case, and every company building an agent on top of your email and chats should be asked how they test for it.

## Now money and agents. Citi is the latest big bank to say that customers who use AI agents for personal finance are on their own.

TechCrunch reports that more banks are taking the same line. If a customer hands their account to an AI agent and something goes wrong, including a hack, the bank will not necessarily cover the loss. Citi is the latest major financial institution to put the brakes on personal finance agents. The detail we have is thin, so here is the logic. The usual protections are built around fraud you did not authorize. An agent muddies that. If you gave a program your login and it was tricked into sending money, the bank can argue that you authorized it. That is the same question of responsibility that made the agent payments story hard to settle earlier this week: when software spends your money, who pays when it is wrong? For companies building these agents, this is a real obstacle. They need banks to cooperate, and a bank that refuses liability makes any agent that touches money a risky product. The likely outcomes are banks launching their own approved agents, or regulators setting the rules.

For now, if you let an agent near your bank account, assume any loss is yours. Read your bank's terms before you connect anything.

## Next, an unusual message from OpenAI's leaders about their own technology.

Sam Altman and Greg Brockman pushed back against a growing debate in Silicon Valley about an AI takeover. Altman warned against a future where AI, not humans, calls the shots. At the same time, they acknowledged that keeping advanced models under human control is a real challenge. Notice what is said and what is not. The pushback is aimed at the loudest version of the debate, the idea that a takeover is a foregone conclusion. But they do not claim control is solved. They call it a challenge. That fits with news from Tuesday, when OpenAI said it would place six researchers from Apollo Research inside the company to look for AI that works against its users. It also sits next to the California attorney general's lawsuit over incidents in which OpenAI's agents went rogue. So the message is that a bad outcome can be avoided, delivered while the company is being sued over agents that behaved badly. Both things can be true. But the order matters. Control has to be demonstrated before it is promised.

Public statements are cheap. Whether control is real will show up in what the outside researchers are allowed to find, and whether the company lets them report it.

## Now the one to act on if you run web servers. A Hacker News post reports an unauthenticated remote code execution flaw in Nginx.

Unauthenticated means an attacker needs no login. Remote code execution means they can run their own commands on your server from far away. Together, that is about the worst kind of bug for a web server. Nginx sits at the edge of a large share of the internet, handling traffic for websites, proxies and load balancers, and a flaw there reaches everything behind it. The post links to two CVE records. A CVE is an entry in the public catalog of known vulnerabilities. Two entries could mean two related flaws, but the post gives no details, so we do not know. We also do not know which versions are affected, whether a fix is out, or whether anyone is already exploiting it. So here is what to do now. Check the official Nginx security advisories and your Linux distribution's updates today. Find every place Nginx runs for you, including inside containers and in ingress controllers, which are the Nginx instances that route traffic into Kubernetes clusters. If no fix exists yet, look for a recommended workaround and put it in place.

Do not wait for the details to be complete. Listing where Nginx runs is the slow part, and you can start it right now.

## Last, a launch from Hacker News, aimed at a problem anyone running coding agents will recognize.

StarSling is a Y Combinator startup run by Yonas and Daniel, selling fast GitHub Actions runners. GitHub Actions is GitHub's built-in system for running tests and builds when code changes, and a runner is the machine that does the work. The pitch is that when AI agents open pull requests, meaning proposed code changes, in bursts, the test queue becomes the bottleneck. StarSling gives every job its own temporary sandbox on fast AMD Zen 5 machines with fast NVMe storage and unlimited concurrency, so a burst of agent changes all run at once. The twist is that it also runs its own agents, which open pull requests to make your tests and workflows faster and cheaper over time. The founders give two examples. For Mastra, which merges over 1,800 pull requests a month, the slowest workflow went from 29 minutes 56 seconds on GitHub's own runners to 5 minutes 6 seconds, 5.87 times faster. And their agents found an 11-month-old performance bottleneck in better-auth, and the fix made one test job 5.4 times faster. Those numbers come from the founders' own launch post. One founder previously started StackShare. The other helped build Netflix's internal developer portal. One practical point: a system that edits your tests to make them faster can also edit them to make them pass. Review those pull requests as carefully as any other.

The bet is a good one. When code is cheap to write, the wait to check it is what hurts. Try the claim on your slowest workflow before you move everything.
