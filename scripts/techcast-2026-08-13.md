# Forty Minutes in March: The LiteLLM Key Leak, Three Frontier Models in One Morning, and a Bug That Waited Sixteen Years
*August 13, 2026*

Somebody spent forty minutes inside a Python package back in March. Today we found out what they walked away with: terabytes of live credentials belonging to Microsoft, Amazon, Cisco, Samsung, Salesforce, and roughly twenty-five hundred other organizations. Also on the show: three frontier-scale models shipped before lunch, a two-billion-dollar bet that the money in AI is not in selling software, and a SQLite bug that sat undisturbed for sixteen years. This is TechCast Daily for Thursday, August thirteenth.

## Let's start with the one that should make you open your cloud console. LiteLLM is an open source proxy that a lot of teams put in front of their AI stack. You point your code at LiteLLM, and it routes your requests to OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, whoever. It is a convenience layer. And by design, a convenience layer like that holds every key you have.

Two security firms, CloudSEK and Hudson Rock, published findings this week on a supply-chain attack against LiteLLM. Malicious versions of the package were sitting in the Python Package Index, the official place everyone installs from. The window was about forty minutes in March. That was enough. What came out was not just AI provider keys. It was cloud keys, repository tokens, SSH keys, Kubernetes secrets, package publishing credentials, and raw environment variables, from more than twenty-five hundred organizations. Hudson Rock says it made the discovery by analyzing a single file weighing one hundred ninety-five terabytes. Neither firm will say where that file came from. Three things make this worse than a normal breach. First, the gap: forty minutes in March, disclosed in August. That's five months of somebody holding valid keys to Microsoft and Amazon infrastructure. Second, package publishing credentials were in the haul, which means the attackers could push poisoned versions of other people's libraries. This breach can spawn the next breach. Third, environment variables are where teams keep the secrets they never got around to putting in a vault, so the blast radius is whatever each victim was sloppiest about.

The practical takeaway is boring and non-optional. If you installed LiteLLM from PyPI in March, every credential that process could see is compromised, not just the AI ones. Rotate them, and check for access from the moment of install forward, not from this week forward. And the structural lesson: a proxy that centralizes all your keys concentrates your risk in exactly the same place it concentrates your convenience.

## Staying with things happening quietly underneath your workflow. Anthropic says it will start watermarking model output. That part is expected. The part worth your attention is the word processed.

In a support article, Anthropic said it's rolling out machine-readable watermarks to comply with the European Union's AI Act, which requires providers to mark AI-generated or AI-manipulated audio, images, text, and video. The law covers any model released after August second, with a grace period until December of twenty twenty-six for older models. Anthropic says all new models globally, not just in Europe, will mark generated content from day one. Text gets embedded watermarks you can't see. Other file types get digitally signed provenance metadata where the format supports it. Here's the interesting choice. The EU carves out exceptions. If the AI is doing standard assistive editing, the guidance's own example is grammar correction, or if it doesn't substantially change your text or its meaning, you don't have to mark it. Anthropic is skipping the carve-out and marking everything it touches. Ashley Belanger at Ars Technica called it a nuke-it-from-orbit approach, which is fair. Ask a model to fix a typo in a paragraph you wrote yourself, and the paragraph comes back marked.

Two honest problems here. Text watermarks are fragile: paraphrase it, translate it, chop it in half, and the signal degrades or vanishes, so the detector will miss real AI text. Meanwhile it will flag human text that got spellchecked. That's a system that produces both kinds of error at once. And the distinction that actually matters to a teacher or an editor, did a machine write this or did a machine touch this, is precisely the distinction this design erases. The watermark is invisible today. The tooling to read it won't stay private forever.

## Now, a genuinely strange morning: three frontier model releases landed at roughly the same time. DeepSeek shipped V4 Pro, tagged zero-eight-one-three, meaning today. xAI shipped Grok four point six. And Qwen dropped a model card whose filename tells you more than most press releases.

Start with the Qwen name, because it's the most informative artifact of the day: Qwen three point eight, two point four T, A ninety-five B, FP eight. Decoded, that's two point four trillion total parameters, with about ninety-five billion of them active for any given token, released in eight-bit precision. The gap between two point four trillion and ninety-five billion is the mixture-of-experts design: the model is enormous, but only a slice of it fires per token, so the cost of running it scales with the small number, not the big one. What it does not scale with is memory. Nobody is running this on a laptop, or on one server. On DeepSeek and Grok, be skeptical of anyone quoting you a leaderboard position today. The API docs and third-party benchmark pages are live, the community numbers are hours old, and the first day of scores for any model is the noisiest data you will ever see about it. The numbers that will still matter in a month are price per million tokens and whether the long-context behavior holds up past the marketing number.

The real story isn't which of the three wins. It's that two of them ship open weights at a scale where open weights used to be a compromise. The argument about open models has quietly moved from can they compete on capability to who can afford the hardware to serve them. Those are very different arguments, and the second one has different winners.

## Which is a good handoff, because at the Ai4 conference this week, three people who rarely agree on anything agreed on that exact point.

Geoffrey Hinton, Fei-Fei Li, and Andrew Ng sat down to argue about regulation and open access, and came out making the case for staying open. That is worth sitting with, because Hinton is the person who left Google specifically to warn people about this technology. He is not an accelerationist. The argument for openness from someone genuinely worried about risk goes roughly like this: safety research requires access to the thing you're studying, and if the only people who can inspect frontier models are the four companies that own them, external oversight becomes a press release. Fei-Fei Li and Andrew Ng come at it from the competition side. If American labs close everything and Chinese labs publish weights, the global default stack ends up being the open one, and it won't be American. Today's releases are the empirical version of that argument. Two of three frontier drops were open weights, and both came out of Chinese labs.

The uncomfortable synthesis: openness is no longer mainly a values question, it's a market-share question, and the side arguing for it now includes the person most famous for saying this technology might be dangerous. When the safety camp and the competition camp reach the same conclusion by different roads, the policy usually follows.

## Money next, and it moved in one direction today. Lovable confirmed a four hundred million dollar Series C at a thirteen point three billion dollar valuation. Cognition is reportedly in talks at forty billion. And a smaller name, Blacksmith, saw its valuation multiply by nearly ten in under a year.

Take the numbers seriously for a second. Lovable told TechCrunch it hit five hundred million dollars in annualized run rate in June. At thirteen point three billion, that's a multiple of roughly twenty-six times revenue. High, but not unhinged by twenty twenty-six standards. The number that would tell you whether it's justified is the one nobody publishes: net revenue retention. App-builder products convert curiosity into signups extremely well and convert signups into year-two customers much less well. Cognition is the more aggressive story. They raised one billion at twenty-six billion a few months ago, and are already reportedly talking at forty billion. That's not a company raising because it needs cash. That's a company raising because the window is open. Blacksmith is the one I'd point to as the healthy shape of the trend: the valuation went up roughly ten times, and revenue also went up more than ten times. The multiple didn't expand. The business did.

Here's the tell to watch across all three. In this cycle, revenue growth and valuation growth have decoupled for most AI companies, and Blacksmith is the exception where they moved together. When the correction comes, and it comes to every category eventually, that's the difference between a down round and a footnote.

## Which brings up the argument that ran hot on Hacker News all day, under a title that's really a question: is AI removing the middle class of software engineering?

The thesis goes like this. Junior roles survive because somebody has to be trained. Senior and staff roles survive because judgment, system design, and knowing which failure modes matter don't come out of a model. The squeeze is on the middle: the engineer whose job was reliably turning a well-specified ticket into working code. That is exactly the task the tools got good at first. I think the thesis is half right, and the wrong half matters. What's actually being removed isn't a seniority band, it's a task type. Well-specified work is now cheap. Ambiguous work is not. Plenty of nominally senior engineers spend their week on well-specified work, and plenty of mid-level engineers spend theirs untangling what the requirement really was. The people getting squeezed are the ones whose job was the translation step from clear spec to code, at any level. And note the second-order effect the thesis usually skips: if you stop hiring the middle, you've broken the pipeline that produces your seniors. That bill arrives in about six years, on someone else's watch.

My honest read: the tooling didn't eliminate a class of engineer, it eliminated the part of the job that was easiest to describe. Which unfortunately was also the part that was easiest to hire for, easiest to evaluate, and easiest to learn on.

## One more funding item, and it's structurally different from the rest. Thrive Holdings raised two billion dollars at a twelve billion dollar valuation, from SoftBank, D1 Capital Partners, and Altimeter Capital, with OpenAI as a backer.

The detail worth noticing is the word holdings. Every other company on today's list sells a product to businesses and books the revenue as software. A holding company buys the businesses. If that's the shape of this, then the bet isn't that enterprises will pay for AI tools. It's that enterprises won't adopt them fast enough on their own, and the margin sits with whoever is willing to own the operation and do the integration themselves. Details are still thin, so hold that reading loosely. But the raise math is clear either way: two billion at twelve billion means they sold about a sixth of the company in one round, which is a lot of dilution for a firm that isn't burning cash on training runs. That money is meant to be deployed into acquisitions or operations, not into GPUs.

If this works, it reframes where AI value lands. Not with the labs, and not with the tool vendors, but with whoever ends up owning the boring companies whose costs the technology actually cuts. That's a much less exciting story and possibly a much better business.

## Now my favorite item of the day, and a palate cleanser after all that money. Two posts landed about the same corner of SQLite: a bug in the write-ahead log reset path that had been in there for sixteen years.

Quick definition, because the mechanism is the whole story. A write-ahead log, or WAL, is how a database stays crash-safe. Instead of editing the main data file directly, the database appends every change to a separate log file first. Periodically it folds those changes into the main file, a step called a checkpoint, and then resets the log back to the beginning to reuse the space. That reset is the delicate moment. Readers may still be looking at the old log while the writer is rewinding it. Get the bookkeeping slightly wrong and a reader can see a mix of old and new state, which is the one thing a database exists to prevent. What makes this notable isn't severity, it's location. SQLite is arguably the most tested piece of software on the planet, with a test suite that has more lines than the database itself, running in literally billions of devices. And this sat there for sixteen years. It survived because reproducing it needs a specific interleaving of timing that ordinary tests never happen to produce.

That's the real lesson, and it generalizes past databases. Coverage measures which lines you ran, not which orderings you ran them in. Any bug that only appears under a rare interleaving is invisible to a test suite that measures coverage, no matter how thorough. If your system has concurrency and you're grading yourself on coverage percentage, you're grading the wrong exam.

## Related in spirit: a piece asking what sort of maths language models are actually good at. Not whether they're good at maths, which is a useless question, but which kinds.

The shape of the answer is clearer than the headlines suggest. Models are strong at maths that has a recognizable form: competition-style problems with a known trick, symbolic manipulation that follows familiar templates, translating a word problem into equations, and sketching the outline of a proof. They are weak where the work is long chains with no redundancy, arithmetic over many steps, and anywhere a single dropped sign silently poisons everything downstream. And there's an asymmetry people consistently underrate. Generating a plausible proof is much easier for these models than checking whether one is correct. A wrong proof and a right proof look almost identical in the register they're written in, and the model is optimizing for that register. Which is why the verification step keeps needing a human or a symbolic engine, and why maths benchmarks with final-answer-only grading flatter models badly: a right answer from a broken argument scores the same as a right answer from a sound one.

The useful frame is that these models are good at the parts of maths that resemble language and bad at the parts that resemble bookkeeping. Which suggests you shouldn't be asking one to compute. You should be asking it to call something that computes.

## And by remarkable coincidence, somebody shipped exactly that thing today. It's called Woxi, an open source reimplementation of the Wolfram Language, written in Rust.

The Wolfram Language is what runs inside Mathematica: symbolic algebra, exact arithmetic, the ability to actually solve an integral instead of approximating one. Woxi is a free, open source interpreter for it. It ships with a Mathematica-like desktop interface, and you can also use it as a command line tool, a Jupyter kernel, a Python package, an npm package, or a WebAssembly module that runs in a browser. The differentiator the author leads with is startup time, and it's the right thing to lead with. The Wolfram kernel takes seconds to start. Woxi takes milliseconds. Seconds-to-start means the engine is something you open. Milliseconds-to-start means it's something you call, from a shell one-liner, from a script, or from inside another program a few hundred times. That's a different category of tool. On the credibility question: roughly twenty-six thousand unit tests and about nine hundred script-level snapshot tests, and the author is explicitly asking Wolfram Language users to report where compatibility breaks.

Put the last two stories together and you get a fairly obvious product. A model that's good at turning a messy question into a formal one, handing the formal one to an engine that can start in a millisecond and cannot be talked into a wrong answer. The interesting AI maths work isn't a bigger model. It's that handoff.

## Two quick ones from the smaller end of the front page, both about doing more with less.

First, a project called Hax: a minimalist, terminal-native coding agent written in C. C is an unusual choice in a space dominated by Python and TypeScript, and the payoff is a single small binary with no runtime to install and nothing to reconcile with your system's package manager. But the more interesting implication is what it reveals about these tools. If a working coding agent fits in a small C program, then the agent loop, read files, call a model, apply an edit, run the tests, repeat, is not where the value lives. The value lives in the model, and the harness around it is commodity. That's worth remembering next time someone raises a large round on the harness. Second, from the other direction: a build of Automatic1111, the long-running interface for Stable Diffusion, tuned for Apple's Metal graphics framework, reporting a forty percent speedup on Stable Diffusion one point five. Yes, one point five is an old model. It's also the one that fits comfortably in the memory a Mac actually has, and a forty percent gain is the difference between a tool you wait on and a tool you iterate with.

Both stories point the same way. Not every improvement comes from more compute. Some come from someone finally sitting down with the profiler.

## Let's finish with the most physical story of the day, and the one with the longest time horizon. A Y Combinator company called Discovered Materials launched, building AI agents to find new materials for the semiconductor industry. Stay with me, because the numbers they open with are the best summary of AI's real constraint I've seen this week.

Their framing is heat. The H100, released in twenty twenty-two, dissipates seven hundred watts. Blackwell, in twenty twenty-four, puts out one point two kilowatts. Rubin, this year, is at two point three kilowatts. That's a doubling roughly every chip generation, and it's a large part of why data centers consume the power and the water they do. Most of that draw isn't computing. It's removing heat. Then they give a concrete example of what's blocked. You could cut the energy needed to move a bit between memory and logic by ten to fifty times by stacking memory directly on top of the logic chip, instead of placing it alongside on a flat board. Nobody does it, because the insulating material inside high-bandwidth memory, silicon dioxide, is a poor conductor of heat. Stack them and you trap the heat between the two hottest things in the system. So a fifty-fold efficiency win is sitting there, gated by a material. Same story for thermal interface materials and for substrates.

That's the part the compute conversation keeps missing. The ceiling on AI infrastructure right now isn't only how many chips get fabricated, it's how fast you can pull heat out of the ones you have. It's unglamorous, it's slow, and a real answer would unlock more efficiency than the next several rounds of model optimization combined.

That's the show. If you take one action item away today, make it the first story: if LiteLLM came off PyPI onto your machines in March, rotate everything that process could see, and rotate it today. And the thread running through the rest of it, three frontier models in a morning, a bug that hid for sixteen years, and a fifty-fold efficiency win locked behind a lump of silicon dioxide, is that the fast-moving layer and the slow-moving layer are drifting further apart. The models ship weekly. The physics does not. Thanks for listening to TechCast Daily. Back tomorrow.
