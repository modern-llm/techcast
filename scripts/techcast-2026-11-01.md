# Backdoored From the Official Account, and AI Money Gets Harder to Raise
*November 01, 2026*

This is TechCast Daily for Sunday, November first. An official Claude extension was backdoored for almost twelve hours on Friday, shipped from Anthropic's own account to as many as five thousand developers. After that, the money side of AI, where a credit rating forecast, a smaller funding target and a chipmaker offering to cosign a loan all point the same way. And DoorDash tells us how many orders AI agents have placed. It is a very small number.

## Start with the security story, because if you write code in Cursor, you may have something to do today. Cursor is an AI code editor built on Visual Studio Code, and it runs its own marketplace for extensions. One of those extensions is the official Claude Code plugin, published under Anthropic's account.

On Friday, Cursor said a malicious version of that extension had been posted to its marketplace: version two point one point three thirteen. Cursor says it reached five thousand users at most. The worst detail is where it came from. The independent researcher Kevin Beaumont and Luke Roberts, the chief technology officer of the security firm Phorian, both say the bad version went out through Anthropic's official account. This was not a fake with a similar name. Roberts calls it a supply chain compromise. That means the attacker got into the real distribution channel, so the poisoned version simply replaced the good one before it. Cursor updates extensions automatically unless you turn that off. So most of the people hit did nothing wrong. They opened their editor and got the new version. Anthropic pushed a clean version, ending in three fourteen, hours later. Reports put the exposure at close to twelve hours. This kind of extension is an attractive target. An AI coding tool is built to read your files and run commands in your terminal, so it already has access to most of what is on a developer's machine, including the keys to the company's live systems. According to Ars Technica, the malware went looking for cloud credentials, the keys that let a program act on your Amazon, Google or Microsoft cloud account. As of Friday, neither Cursor nor Anthropic had said how the attacker got control of the account. Anthropic had not warned its customers itself. If you use Cursor, check which version of the Claude Code extension you have and when it last updated. If you ever ran the one ending in three thirteen, assume any cloud keys or access tokens on that machine were taken, and replace them. Deleting the extension is not enough.

Two points beyond your own machine. Automatic updates are usually the safe choice, because they deliver fixes fast. Here they delivered the attack just as fast. And the open question is the account itself: was it a stolen password, a stolen publishing token, or something inside Cursor's marketplace? Until one of the two companies answers that, nobody can say whether it could happen again tomorrow.

## Now money. Three stories this week each look small on their own. Put together, they say the people paying for the AI build-out are getting more careful. Start with the big picture from TechCrunch.

Big Tech is spending hundreds of billions of dollars on data centers, chips and power. TechCrunch looks at how that is paid for. More of it is borrowed. Some of the companies building data centers now spend more cash than their business brings in, which is what negative cash flow means. And investors are asking harder questions about when the spending pays back. You heard one version of that on Friday's show, when Google's stock fell despite strong revenue. The next story shows how this reaches smaller players. Nscale, a British company that rents out AI computing, is building a data center in West Virginia. Nvidia is considering cosigning the loan for it. That means if Nscale cannot pay, Nvidia would. For the lenders, that makes the loan much safer. But look at who is cosigning. Nvidia sells the chips the loan will pay for. So Nvidia would be partly guaranteeing its own sales. Nvidia has already invested in several of its own customers during this boom. A loan guarantee goes a step further. It suggests lenders want more than Nscale's own promise to pay.

None of this means the spending stops. It means the money for it is getting harder to raise, and the next two stories show that at OpenAI and Anthropic.

## OpenAI first, where the news is a number going down.

According to a report covered by TechCrunch, OpenAI has lowered the target for its latest funding round. The coverage we have does not give the old or new figure, so we will not guess. What matters is the direction. For three years OpenAI has raised more money with each round, often more than it first asked for. A company that cuts its target is telling you investors offered less than it hoped. This lands while the market is asking whether AI labs can earn back what they spend, and while Anthropic, OpenAI's closest rival, is preparing to sell shares to the public.

A lower target is not a failed round. OpenAI will still raise an enormous amount. But for now, it is not the one setting the price.

## Now Anthropic. We have covered its IPO filing twice this week, so only the new part.

The Financial Times reports that Blackstone, the investment firm, expects the credit rating agencies to give Anthropic a high junk rating once it is public. A credit rating is a grade on how likely a company is to pay back what it borrows. Junk means below investment grade. Many pension funds and insurers can buy little or none of that kind of debt. High junk is the best grade on the wrong side of the line. In plain terms, that rating would say Anthropic is a real business, but a risky one to lend to. Why that matters: Anthropic's plan depends on spending huge sums on computing, and some of it will be borrowed. A junk rating means higher interest on every dollar, and fewer lenders able to take part. Two caveats. This is Blackstone's forecast, not an actual rating. And Netflix, Tesla and Uber were all rated junk for years while they grew.

Put the three together. OpenAI is lowering its ask, Anthropic may borrow at junk rates, and Nvidia may cosign loans for its own customers. The money is still flowing. It just costs more, and comes with more conditions, than it did a year ago.

## Staying with Anthropic, two items from Hacker News about what it will not let Claude do. We only have the headlines, so here is what they say and what we can fairly add.

The first says Anthropic is taking steps to block Claude from reverse engineering video games. Reverse engineering means taking a finished program apart to work out how it was built. In games, that is how people make mods, fan patches and preservation projects that keep old games playable. It is also how people make cheats and break copy protection. Game publishers care a lot about the second group, and a model that can read machine code quickly would make that work much easier. The second headline is blunter: Anthropic blocks users for anything touching training larger models. Anthropic's terms have long banned using Claude to build a competing AI model. That rule exists because one cheap way to build a model is distillation, meaning training it on a stronger model's answers. The complaint here, from the title at least, is that the enforcement is catching more than that, and that people doing ordinary machine learning work are getting cut off. We cannot check from a headline how many people were affected or why.

The pattern is the same in both. Claude is now good enough at these jobs that Anthropic is drawing lines around them, and any rule enforced at this scale will catch some legitimate users. What Anthropic owes them is a clear rule and a way to appeal.

## Two more security stories. Again we have only the headlines, so these are short.

First, a report of malware that uses a language model to rank its victims by how much they are worth. Criminals often break into many machines at once. Then someone has to sort through them and decide which ones are worth the effort. A model can do that sorting for thousands of break-ins without the gang hiring anyone, so the attackers spend their own time only on the richest targets. For a defender, that means the second stage of an attack may arrive sooner, and aimed more precisely. Second, a security write-up titled Do the math: How we took over ChatGPT accounts. Taking over an account means getting into someone else's ChatGPT without their password. That matters because people put a lot into those chats. The headline does not tell us the method or whether OpenAI has fixed it. Researchers usually publish after the company has fixed the flaw, but we have not confirmed that here.

Both stories point the same way. Attackers are using AI to save labor, and AI accounts are now valuable enough to attack. Now, something lighter, about dinner.

## For two years, the tech industry has said AI agents will shop for us. An agent here means an AI that takes actions for you, like filling a cart and paying, not just answering questions. DoorDash just gave a real number on how that is going.

On its earnings call Thursday, an analyst asked about it. Chief executive Tony Xu said AI agents have generated several hundred orders on DoorDash, in total, across all the time they have been running. Not per day. In total. For scale, DoorDash handled just under one billion orders in the third quarter alone. Xu called it a very low use case, which is putting it mildly. Xu's explanation is worth hearing. Xu thinks agents will be useful for what Xu called long consideration purchases, big decisions where the agent does work for you in the background, like comparing options over days. Ordering food is not that. You know what you want, the app already remembers your address and your usual order, and two taps get it done. Asking a chatbot to do it adds a step. DoorDash also said it is open to working with outside agent makers. The demand is what is missing. This matters beyond food. OpenAI, Google and others have built shopping and checkout features into their chatbots, and much of the case for agents rests on people handing over everyday errands.

This number is useful because real numbers like it are rare. Most claims about agent adoption are forecasts. Here is a big company reporting actual use, and it rounds to zero. Agents will likely win first where a task is long and annoying, like booking travel or comparing insurance, not where the existing app is already fast.

## Apple's new chief executive has defended where Apple stands on AI.

The line TechCrunch picked out: Apple does not want to ship AI for AI's sake. Apple's argument has long been that it adds AI where it makes a product better, and does not bolt a chatbot onto everything because rivals did. There is something to that. Most people do not buy an iPhone for its AI. But it is also the answer a company gives when it is behind. Apple promised a much smarter Siri in 2024, then delayed it, and turned to Google's Gemini models to help close the gap. A new chief executive repeating the old line tells you the strategy did not change with the person at the top.

The test is not the slogan. It is whether Siri gets good enough that nobody asks this question anymore.

## Back to data centers, and two unusual ideas about where and how to build them.

Google's first data center satellite is online. It carries a TPU, Google's own AI chip, and it is running Gemma 4, one of Google DeepMind's open models. Its first public job is writing poetry, which TechCrunch calls cheesy. The poetry is a stunt. The point is that the chip works in orbit. Google has been talking for about a year about putting AI computing in space, where solar panels get sunlight almost all the time and there is no fight over land or local power. The hard problems are still ahead. In a vacuum there is no air to carry heat away, so cooling is difficult. Cosmic rays damage chips over time. And launches are expensive. One working chip proves little about a whole data center, but every plan like this has to start with exactly this step. The other idea is on the ground. A startup called Runware is building a factory to turn out ready-made AI data centers on an assembly line, the way cars are made. Today most data centers are built on site, one at a time, which takes years. Building them in standard pieces in a factory could cut that time. TechCrunch's caveat is the right one: the real test is whether Runware can deliver at that scale.

Both ideas come from the same pressure. Companies can buy chips faster than anyone can build places to put them. Whoever shortens that wait, in orbit or in a factory, gets paid.

## A short one for people who pay for ChatGPT.

A Hacker News post reports that OpenAI is merging the usage limits for ChatGPT and Codex, its coding agent. Until now, as the headline implies, each had its own allowance. A single pool cuts both ways. If you mostly chat and code a little, you may get more coding time than before. If you lean hard on Codex, a long coding session could now use up the same allowance you need for everyday questions. We only have the headline, not the new numbers, so check your plan's page before assuming either.

Anthropic already does the same, with the Claude app and Claude Code drawing on one limit, so this is becoming the norm.

## Now some research, starting with a claim about small models working as a team.

A project called Memdoor says a team of five copies of Qwen 3.8, a 27 billion parameter model from Alibaba, beats Anthropic's Fable 5 on LiveCodeBench Hard. LiveCodeBench is a coding test built from recent programming contest problems, chosen so a model cannot have memorized the answers during training. The hard set is the toughest part of it. Parameters, also called weights, are the numbers a model learns. Twenty-seven billion is small enough to run on a single strong graphics card, and Qwen's weights are open, so anyone can download and run it. Fable 5 is a frontier model you can only reach through Anthropic. If the result holds, it means organizing several models to work together, for example splitting a problem or checking each other's work, can make up much of the gap in raw ability. Three things to check before believing it. How much computing the team of five used compared with a single Fable 5 call. Whether the result holds on other tests. And whether anyone outside the project can reproduce it.

My guess is the cost answer matters most. Beating a frontier model by spending more total computing is a much weaker claim than beating it cheaply.

## Next, a paper on one of the oldest problems in machine learning.

It is called the Sleep framework, and it is about continual learning. That means teaching a model new things after its main training is done, without it forgetting what it already knew. Today, if you keep training a model on new material, it tends to lose older skills. Researchers call this catastrophic forgetting. That is one reason models have a knowledge cutoff date and get replaced by whole new versions, instead of learning as they go. We have only the title and a link to the paper, but the name points at the idea it borrows. In animals, sleep is when the brain is thought to replay the day and move it into long-term memory. A method named after it most likely alternates between taking in new material and a separate phase that merges it with what the model already knows.

Continual learning is one of the few problems whose solution would change how models are built and sold. Many papers have claimed progress. Few have held up at the scale of a real frontier model. This one deserves a look, not a celebration yet.

## Two last items about how software gets written now.

First, an interview with Leo de Moura, who created Lean. Lean is a programming language that also checks proofs. You write a mathematical proof, or a claim about what a program does, and Lean checks every step mechanically. If Lean accepts it, the proof is correct, as long as the claim itself was written down correctly. That is formal verification. The AI connection is simple. Language models produce proofs and code quickly, but they make mistakes and state them with confidence. Lean is slow to write by hand, but it does not accept a wrong step. Let a model do the writing and Lean do the checking, and you get code that is proved correct, not just tested. That is why mathematicians and several AI labs have moved toward Lean over the past two years. Second, a post titled Building an AI-native engineering org, where we have only the headline. The phrase usually means a software team designed from the start around AI agents doing much of the coding, instead of adding AI tools to an old way of working. Teams like that tend to spend less time writing code and more time reviewing and testing it.

Those two fit together. Once agents write most of the code, the scarce work is checking it. Lean is the strictest form of checking we have. And as today's first story showed, checking matters for the tools themselves, not just the code they write.
