# TechCast Daily, October 02, 2026: Google's Gemini 4 Argon, Microsoft's Copilot Rethink, and Cheaper Coding Agents
*October 02, 2026*

Google says it now has the best AI model for writing code, and the first big job it gave that model was rewriting Google's own software. Today: Gemini 4 Argon, Microsoft rebuilding Copilot around your job, a judge throwing out two lawsuits against Google's AI answers, and two tools that cut the cost of running a coding agent. This is TechCast Daily for Friday, October second.

## Start with Google, because for the first time in months it is claiming the top spot instead of chasing it. In June, Google promised a model called Gemini 3.5 Pro. That model never arrived. Google spent the summer shipping its smaller, cheaper Flash models instead. Now it has skipped ahead a full number and announced Gemini 4 Argon.

Google says Argon leads the industry at three things: writing code, office-style knowledge work, and cybersecurity. The headline number comes from DeepSWE version 1.1, a software engineering test. Argon scored seventy-seven point nine percent. Google says that beats OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra and Anthropic's Fable 5.1 and Opus 5.5. It also claims the top score on the Vals Index, a test of economic analysis work, and says Argon holds up on long tasks that take many steps. Can you use it? The reports disagree. TechCrunch says Google has released Argon. Ars Technica says Google announced it, but that you aren't allowed to use it yet. Meanwhile an outside analysis of Argon's High setting, covering intelligence, speed and price, is already on Hacker News, which suggests somebody outside Google has been able to run it. My best guess is a limited early release, but I can't confirm that. If you're planning around it, wait for Google to say who gets access and when. Inside Google, Argon is already at work, and this is the part I find most convincing. Google says Argon used performance data reported by its entire fleet of servers to help save three hundred tebibytes of memory across its data centers. A tebibyte is about a trillion bytes. Argon agents have also been rewriting old C and C plus plus code in Rust, a newer language that rules out a whole class of memory bugs. That matters because memory bugs are the largest single source of serious security flaws in big C and C plus plus programs. Google and Microsoft have each said they make up about seventy percent of the severe security bugs in their own code. The rewrites include thousands of lines in RE2, Google's library for matching text patterns, and in its decoder for AV1 video. They also include more than eight hundred thousand lines in Zircon, the kernel, meaning the core program, of Google's Fuchsia operating system, which runs on some of Google's Nest smart displays.

My take: a benchmark lead lasts until the next release, often a few weeks, and Google picked which benchmarks to publish, as every company does. Eight hundred thousand lines of kernel code moved to Rust is a different kind of claim. If those changes hold up in review and in use, it's the strongest evidence yet that AI agents can do the maintenance work companies have put off for years. For now, only Google can check it.

## From Google selling a model to Microsoft selling a workplace. Last episode, OpenAI pitched ChatGPT as the place your working day happens. Microsoft wants Copilot to be that place, and it made its pitch quietly.

Last week, Satya Nadella hosted a small, invite-only event for leaders from some of Microsoft's most important business customers. No big stage and no press event. According to Tom Warren at The Verge, Nadella described the new Copilot as the OS for work, meaning an operating system for your job. There are three concrete changes. Copilot gets coding abilities built in. It gets agents, meaning AI that carries out tasks with many steps on its own instead of only answering questions. And for the first time, Microsoft is bringing the full power of Office into Copilot. Nadella's bet is that AI will change how people work the way Office did in the eighties and nineties. The audience is the story. Microsoft didn't pitch consumers. It pitched the companies that sign multi-year contracts covering thousands of employees. That fits an argument from Russell Brandom in a piece called The ugly economics of consumer AI. The piece argues that the frontier labs, the companies building the most advanced models, have grown wary of selling AI to consumers, and the reason isn't that the technology falls short. The reason is money. Here is the basic arithmetic, which is no secret in the industry: every answer a model gives costs computing power, while a consumer plan is a flat monthly fee, or free. A heavy user can cost more than they pay. A business pays per employee, signs for years, and its usage is easier to plan for.

So when OpenAI and Microsoft go after the workplace within days of each other, part of the reason is where the profit is. There's an awkward detail here. Microsoft is one of OpenAI's biggest investors, and the two are now selling the same promise to the same customers. Microsoft starts ahead on one count: your company's documents are already stored in its software. OpenAI has to convince companies to move them.

## Now the companies that say Google's AI answers are taking their readers. On Wednesday they lost in court.

Judge Amit Mehta of the US District Court dismissed two antitrust lawsuits against Google, meaning suits under the laws against abusing a monopoly. One came from Chegg, the homework help company, whose business has shrunk as students turned to AI. The other came from Penske Media, the owner of Rolling Stone. Both made the same argument. Google uses its monopoly in search to force publishers to supply their content for AI Overviews, the AI-written summaries at the top of search results, without paying for it. A publisher that refuses risks disappearing from search. And when the summary answers the question, fewer people click through to the publisher's site. Mehta ruled that those claims don't stand up under antitrust law. The judge's name matters. Mehta is the same judge who ruled in 2024 that Google holds an illegal monopoly in search. So this ruling isn't saying Google lacks market power. It says these two complaints don't make an antitrust case. The reports so far don't spell out the reasoning in detail, and the companies can still appeal.

For publishers, antitrust was the strongest-sounding route, because it doesn't require proving that any one article was copied. That route just got much harder. What's left are copyright suits, licensing deals, or blocking Google and losing search traffic. None of those is a good option for a mid-size publisher, and that imbalance is what the lawsuits were about.

## One short OpenAI item, and it's a new one.

The Wall Street Journal reports that OpenAI has parted ways with three safety researchers. According to the report, an internal investigation found they had mishandled sensitive company information. That is nearly all that's public. The reporting I've seen doesn't name the researchers, doesn't say what the information was, and doesn't say whether it left the company. This has happened before. In 2024, OpenAI fired two researchers from a safety team over an alleged leak, and one of them later gave a different account of why.

Two readings are possible, and the facts don't settle it yet. It could be ordinary enforcement of confidentiality rules, which every company has. Or it could be another clash between OpenAI's safety staff and its leadership over what stays private. OpenAI has lost safety people in public disputes before, so many people will assume the second. I'd wait for names and details.

## Now for developers. Two launches on Hacker News go after the same problem. Running a coding agent is expensive, and most of what it does doesn't need the most expensive model.

First, the Weave Router, which is open source. A router, here, is a model whose only job is to decide which big model handles each step. It plugs into a coding agent like Claude Code or Codex. Hard debugging and system design go to a top model like OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra. Simple frontend edits go to something cheap like DeepSeek v4 Flash. The team just released Weave Router 2.0 and tested it against Astra alone on two standard coding tests, Terminal Bench 4.0 and SWE Atlas. On both, the router passed the same share of tasks as Astra. On Terminal Bench, it cost fifty-two percent as much and finished tasks two point two times faster. On SWE Atlas, it cost fifty-four percent as much and ran two and a half times faster. The team credits a new design for the routing model and more training data. These are the team's own numbers, so treat them as a claim until someone else reproduces them. The team set out to beat every single model by combining several. What it has shown is a tie on quality at about half the price. For most teams, that's the more useful result. Second, Magnitude, from a Y Combinator startup built by two engineers, Anders and Tom. They had built an open-source browser agent with more than four thousand stars on GitHub, wanted to run it on local models, and found no engine that fit. Magnitude is an inference engine, the program that actually runs a model on your machine. They say it is up to twice as fast as llama.cpp, a widely used engine for running models at home, and it works on Mac, Linux and Windows. Their complaint about existing engines is specific. Engines like vLLM and SGLang are built for data centers serving many users at once, and they give up speed for any one session. Engines like llama.cpp and Ollama run on almost anything but aren't tuned for your particular chip. Specialized engines are fast on certain hardware but missing features. Agents need something else: long sessions, several running at once, while you keep using the computer for other work. Magnitude's answer is to tune itself to your device. Its kernels, the small pieces of code that do the math on the chip, have adjustable settings, and it tests those settings on your actual hardware before the model runs. The trade-off: the team only writes kernels for the most popular open-weight model families, meaning models whose files anyone can download and run.

Put the two together and you can see where agent costs are heading. The expensive model gets called for the hard part, and everything else runs on cheap models or on the laptop you already own. If that holds, the price fights between the big labs matter less to developers than the router and the engine they put in front of them.

## Two quick developer notes. Both come from headlines with little detail behind them so far, so I'll keep them short.

First, Figma now lets only approved apps use its MCP server. MCP, the Model Context Protocol, is the standard way AI tools connect to other software. Figma's MCP server lets an AI agent read your designs, for example to turn them into code. Now only clients on Figma's list can connect. The headline names one that was left off: an agent called Pi. We don't know yet how Figma picks the list, or whether smaller tools can apply to join it. Figma's reason isn't in the headline. Security is a plausible one, since an MCP connection can read a company's private design files. Second, a write-up on edge functions, which are small pieces of code that run on servers close to the user. The team behind it says its edge functions got five times faster when it moved them from V8 isolates to Firecracker microVMs. A V8 isolate is a lightweight sandbox, meaning a space where code runs cut off from everything else, built on the JavaScript engine inside Chrome, and many of them share one process. A Firecracker microVM is a tiny virtual machine, the technology Amazon built for its Lambda service, and each one acts like its own small computer. Isolates are usually sold as the faster, lighter option, so a five-times gain in the other direction is a surprise. The headline doesn't say faster at what, starting up or running, and that changes what the number means.

Back to Figma, because it's the bigger story. MCP was sold as an open standard any agent could use. If every big app keeps its own list of approved agents, the standard stays open but access doesn't, and the small tools are the ones left out.

## Two money stories: one about the chips AI runs on, and one about a company that sells AI voices.

Micron's chief executive says memory supply will be much tighter in 2027 and 2028 than it is this year. Micron is one of the three big makers of memory chips, along with Samsung and SK Hynix. Memory is already scarce because AI data centers buy huge amounts of it, especially the high-bandwidth memory that sits next to AI chips, and prices for ordinary computer memory have climbed as factories shifted to serve them. The headline is all I have, so I can't tell you what is behind the forecast. But when a maker says the next two years will be worse than this one, anyone buying laptops, phones or servers should expect higher prices or fewer choices. New memory factories take years to build and equip, so supply can't catch up quickly. It also makes Google's three hundred tebibyte saving from earlier worth real money. ElevenLabs, which makes AI voices for audiobooks, dubbing and customer service, is now valued at twenty-two billion dollars, double its last valuation. The deal is a three hundred million dollar employee tender, co-led by the investment firms Wellington and T. Rowe Price. In a tender, employees sell some of their shares to outside investors. The company itself raises no new money. Two things stand out. First, it lets employees turn shares into cash without waiting for a stock market listing, which helps a startup keep its people. Second, Wellington and T. Rowe Price are big investors in public companies. When firms like that buy into a private one, it's often a step toward going public. That's my reading, not anything ElevenLabs has announced.

ElevenLabs is also a useful contrast with the consumer AI story from earlier. It charges by usage, so its heaviest customers pay the most. That is exactly the problem the chatbot makers with flat monthly plans haven't solved.

## Two Apple stories about privacy, and they point in opposite directions.

First, Apple is reportedly building a home security camera that doesn't give you video. Mark Gurman of Bloomberg said on the first episode of the Power On podcast that the camera belongs to a new Apple smart home lineup, alongside the smart home display Apple is rumored to be making. The camera project is code-named J450, a companion to the display, which is J490. These are small cameras you place around the house, running at very low frame rates. Instead of footage, you get text: written descriptions of what happened. Gurman also says Apple is building home security products of its own and products made with other companies. Why would anyone want a security camera with no video? Because the video is the privacy risk. A clip of your living room stored on a server can leak, or be handed to police under a court order, or be watched by someone who shouldn't see it. A line of text saying a person came to the front door at three fifteen gives away much less. It fits how Apple sells itself. The cost is plain too: if something does happen, you have no footage to show anyone. Second, a report that police can get around one of Apple's privacy features. Since iOS 18.1, an iPhone that stays locked for three days restarts itself. After a restart, the data on the phone stays fully encrypted until someone enters the passcode, which makes it much harder for forensic tools, the software police use to pull data off phones, to get anything. The headline says police can now get past that automatic restart. The headline is all I have, so I can't tell you how, which phones or iOS versions are affected, or whether Apple has fixed it.

Put together, these two stories show where Apple's privacy approach is strong and where it isn't. A camera that only ever hands over text can't leak footage. A feature that protects data after the fact, like the automatic restart, can be worked around, and then it's a race between Apple's patches and the next workaround.

## Last, two projects that use AI for jobs nobody would have handed it a couple of years ago.

The first is called Bez, and its headline says it generates a browser engine from specs and tests. A browser engine is the part of a browser that turns a web page's code into what you see on screen. It is some of the hardest software there is, and the big ones are the product of decades of work. The web has very detailed written standards, the specs, and a huge shared test suite that browsers run against. The idea, going by the title, is to use the specs as instructions and the tests as the check, and have AI write the engine. The headline doesn't say how far it got, and that's the question that matters. Projects like this test whether AI can build a large system from a clear definition, not just patch small pieces of one. The second: someone used Anthropic's Opus 5.5 to find a new eyewitness account of the dodo. The dodo died out in the late sixteen hundreds, so an eyewitness account means a document written by someone who saw a living bird, one historians hadn't known about. The post's title is all I have, so I can't tell you what the record says or how the model found it. But the use is easy to see. Historians have far more old documents than time to read them, and a model that can scan them for a mention can point a historian to the right page.

Set these next to Argon's Rust rewrite from the top of the show. In each case the work can be checked: by a test suite, by a compiler, or by a historian reading the original document. That's where AI is doing its most believable work right now.
