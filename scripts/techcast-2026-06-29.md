# China Takes the Crown, and AI Meets Its Limits
*June 29, 2026*

Today, China just reclaimed the world's fastest supercomputer for the first time since 2018, and it did it without a single GPU. We'll dig into what that really means for the chip-restriction strategy, why central bankers are now openly worried an AI crash could take the global economy with it, and a Ford executive admitting out loud that the robots couldn't replace the gray-haired engineers after all. Grab your coffee. Let's get into it.

## Let's start with the story everyone in the chip world is chewing on. The new TOP500 supercomputer rankings dropped at ISC, and there's a new number one. It's called LineShine, it lives at the National Supercomputing Center in Shenzhen, and it just knocked the American machine El Capitan off the top spot.

Here's why this is a bigger deal than the usual bragging rights. The United States has spent years trying to choke off China's access to the high-end hardware that modern computing runs on. The whole theory of the export controls is that without Nvidia's top GPUs, China simply can't build frontier-class machines. And yet LineShine took the crown, and the kicker is that it reportedly doesn't use any GPUs at all. GPUs are the backbone of almost every modern supercomputer and certainly of AI training. So China didn't just match the West with smuggled chips. It found a different architecture entirely to get to the top. That's not a workaround, that's a statement. Now pair that with the model side of the story. Zhipu AI, which goes by Z dot AI, released an open-weight model called GLM 5.2. And the claims around it are striking. Some researchers say it matches the leading Western frontier models in specific bug-finding and cybersecurity tasks. There's even a benchmark write-up making the rounds claiming GLM 5.2 beats Claude on certain evaluations. Be skeptical of any single benchmark, especially a vendor-friendly one, because models can be tuned to win the test you're being shown. But the broad signal is consistent across multiple sources this week. GLM still lags behind the top American models on general reasoning, but the gap has narrowed dramatically, and it's open weight, meaning anyone can download it and run it.

So put the two together. The hardware controls were supposed to buy the U.S. a durable lead, and instead we're watching China top the supercomputer list with a non-GPU design and ship open models that are closing in on the frontier. The lesson isn't that export controls did nothing. It's that they create pressure, and pressure creates ingenuity. When you can't buy your way to the answer, you engineer your way there. Washington should treat this as the message Beijing clearly intends it to be.

## Speaking of pressure, let's talk about money, because the people whose entire job is to worry about the financial system are now worried about AI specifically.

Central bankers, the folks at institutions like the Bank for International Settlements, have started warning that the AI boom carries real risk of triggering a broader financial crash. Strip away the diplomatic language and the concern is concrete. An enormous share of recent market gains, and an enormous share of capital spending, is concentrated in a handful of AI and chip companies. Valuations are pricing in a future where AI pays off massively and soon. Hundreds of billions are being poured into data centers, often financed with debt, on the assumption that demand will keep compounding. The danger isn't that AI is fake. It's the concentration. When this much of the market's value and this much borrowed money rests on one narrative, a disappointment doesn't stay contained. If returns come in slower than the spending assumed, the correction ripples out through everyone exposed, which by now is almost everyone, because these are the largest companies in the major indexes. That's the precise mechanism that turns a sector slump into a systemic event. Central bankers calling it out by name is notable, because they're usually the last people to say the quiet part loud.

I'd put it this way. The technology can be transformative and the financing around it can still be a bubble. Those two things are not in conflict, they're how almost every important technology gets funded. The railroads were real and the railway mania still wrecked a lot of investors. The question isn't whether AI matters. It's whether the spending got ahead of the payoff, and the people who watch the plumbing are telling you they think it might have.

## There's also a sharp little move in the AI business world that tells you how the competitive dynamics are hardening. Google is putting limits on how Meta can use its Gemini models.

The short version is that Google has restricted Meta's access to its Gemini AI. And the reason this matters goes beyond two giants squabbling. For a while the AI ecosystem ran on a kind of mutual convenience. Everyone used everyone's APIs, models flowed across company lines, and the assumption was that frontier capability was a commodity you could rent. What we're seeing now is the opposite. The leading labs increasingly view their best models as strategic weapons they don't want sharpening a direct competitor. Meta builds its own models, the Llama family, and is a clear rival in the race. So Google curbing Meta's Gemini access is a signal that the era of casual cross-pollination is closing. Access is becoming a lever you pull to advantage yourself and disadvantage the other guy.

This is the natural endgame when a capability goes from nice-to-have to core competitive moat. Expect more of it, and expect it to hit smaller builders hardest. When the giants start fencing off their best models from each other, the startups renting those same APIs should ask how secure their supply really is.

## Now let's get to the reality-check segment, because a few stories this week land on the same uncomfortable truth. AI is genuinely useful in engineering, and it is also being oversold, and the bill for the overselling is starting to arrive.

Start with Ford. A Ford executive said, and I'm quoting the spirit of it, that they had mistakenly thought introducing artificial intelligence would by itself produce a high-quality product. It didn't. So Ford has been rehiring its gray beard engineers, the veterans with decades of hands-on judgment, because the AI tooling couldn't replace what those people actually know. That's a remarkable admission from a company that, like everyone, was told AI would let it do more with fewer experienced humans. Then there's a thoughtful piece making the rounds called Reflections on Software Engineering in the Age of AI. The honest takeaway from that genre of writing is converging. AI coding tools are spectacular at producing code and mediocre at producing correct, maintainable systems. They accelerate the typing, not the thinking. The hard parts of software, deciding what to build, understanding the existing system, owning the consequences when it breaks, those still sit with experienced engineers. And finally there's a developer-tools piece called Model Training as Code, which points at the constructive direction. Instead of treating model training as a pile of ad hoc scripts and notebooks, you treat it like real software, with version control, reproducibility, and engineering discipline. That's the maturity curve. The first wave was magic demos. The second wave is the boring infrastructure that makes the magic reliable.

The thread connecting all three is the same. AI raises the floor on output and does nothing for the ceiling on judgment. Ford learned it expensively, the engineers writing reflections learned it daily, and the Model Training as Code crowd is building the discipline that lets you actually trust the output. The companies that win the next two years aren't the ones that fired their experts to buy a copilot. They're the ones that gave their experts a copilot and kept the experts.

## Staying with engineering craft, two pieces this week dig into something AI tends to make worse, not better, which is the cognitive load of the code we leave behind.

The first is titled The Cost YAGNI Was Never About. YAGNI is the old principle You Aren't Gonna Need It, the advice to not build features and abstractions you don't yet require. The usual argument against premature building is that it wastes effort. But this piece makes a sharper point. The real cost was never the wasted hours of writing the speculative code. The real cost is the permanent tax it puts on everyone who reads the codebase afterward. Every abstraction you added for a future that never arrived is something the next developer has to understand, work around, and be afraid to delete. The expense isn't in the writing, it's in the lifelong reading. The companion piece is called Engineering for Bounded Cognition, and it states the underlying constraint plainly. Human working memory is small and fixed. You can only hold so much in your head at once. So the highest-value thing good engineering can do is respect that limit, keep the amount you must understand at any one moment small, and stop pretending that smart people can just absorb arbitrary complexity. They can't. Nobody can.

Why am I pairing these with the AI theme? Because AI coding assistants are complexity machines. They'll happily generate that speculative abstraction, that extra layer, that clever generality you didn't ask for, in seconds, at no apparent cost. But the cost is real and it lands later, on a human with limited working memory trying to figure out why any of it exists. YAGNI and bounded cognition aren't outdated rules from a slower era. In the age of effortless code generation, they matter more than ever, because the thing that's now scarce isn't writing code, it's understanding it.

## Let's nerd out for a minute on how these models actually get built and run, because two items here are quietly important under the hood.

The first has a great title, Tokenmaxxing Is Dead, Long Live Tokenmaxxing. Tokens are the chunks of text models read and generate, and tokenmaxxing is the obsessive optimization around them, squeezing more meaning into fewer tokens, managing context windows, getting the most out of every unit you pay for. The argument is that the old version of this game, hand-tuning prompts to save tokens, is fading, because context windows got huge and token costs dropped. But a new version is rising, where the optimization moves to a different layer, smarter retrieval, better caching, and architectural choices about what the model even sees. The discipline didn't die, it relocated. The second piece is a 2024 paper getting fresh attention on Knowledge Distillation of Black-Box Large Language Models. Distillation is how you take a giant expensive model and train a smaller cheaper one to imitate it. Black-box distillation means doing this when you can only see the big model's outputs, not its internals, which is exactly the situation when you're learning from a model you access only through an API. And that connects right back to our China story. If you can't get the hardware to train a frontier model from scratch, distilling capability out of one you can query is a very attractive shortcut.

These sound like deep-in-the-weeds research topics, but they're actually the economics of the whole field in disguise. Distillation and token efficiency are how capability leaks downhill, from the few labs that can afford to train the biggest models to everyone else. That diffusion is exactly what the export controls are trying to slow, and exactly what techniques like these are designed to accelerate.

## Now a cluster of stories about accountability, about what happens when AI systems become witnesses, accomplices, and leak vectors all at once. This one's a little dark, so bear with me.

Start with a genuinely chilling legal first. In the trial over the Palisades fire, one of the deadliest wildfires in Los Angeles history, prosecutors used a defendant's ChatGPT logs as evidence. The man faced arson charges, and alongside the usual phone location data and security footage, prosecutors presented his chatbot history. They said he'd asked ChatGPT to generate images of fire, asked it why he was so angry all the time, ranted to it about the wealthy, and in a screen recording asked whether someone could be blamed for a fire if it was lit by their actions. Sit with that. The private conversations people have with an AI, the questions they'd never type into a public search, are discoverable evidence. People treat these chatbots like a diary or a therapist, and they are neither. They're a logged service owned by a company that can be subpoenaed. Second story, a professor at Brown publicly denounced what he called mass AI fraud on an exam, students using AI to cheat at scale. This is the trust crisis arriving in education, where the assessment methods we've used for a century assume a human did the work unaided, an assumption that's now just false. And third, a smaller but telling one, there's an open issue for OpenAI's Codex about giving users a way to exclude sensitive files. Right now a coding agent can sweep up secrets, credentials, private data, and there's no clean way to fence it off. The fix is still pending.

The common thread is that we deployed these tools faster than we built the guardrails around them. Your chat logs are evidence, your exams are no longer trustworthy, and your coding agent might be reading your secrets. None of these are reasons to abandon the tools. They're reasons to treat them with the seriousness we extend to any powerful system that keeps records, makes mistakes, and answers to someone other than you.

## On the lighter and frankly more hopeful side of AI in the real world, here's a story that captures both the promise and the peril in one anecdote.

Someone used Claude Code, which is an AI coding assistant, to get a second opinion on their MRI. They fed in their medical imaging and used the AI to interpret and cross-check what the radiologist had told them. And the reason this resonated with so many people is that it points at a real democratization. Expert medical interpretation has always been scarce, expensive, and gated. The idea that you can get a competent second look, instantly, for the cost of an API call, is genuinely powerful, especially for people without easy access to specialists. But the obvious caution writes itself. An AI second opinion is a prompt for better questions, not a diagnosis. These models are confident even when they're wrong, they don't carry malpractice liability, and they don't know your full history. The right use is to walk back into your doctor's office better informed, not to overrule the doctor from your couch.

I love this use case precisely because it sits on the line we keep circling back to today. AI is an extraordinary amplifier of access and a terrible substitute for accountable expertise. Use it to ask sharper questions of the professionals. Don't use it to replace them. The MRI story and the Ford story are the same lesson told in two very different rooms.

## Let's close with the hacker-delight corner, three stories that have nothing to do with the AI gold rush and everything to do with why people fell in love with computers in the first place.

First, someone brought Swift, Apple's modern programming language, to the Apple Two. Yes, the 1977 machine with a few kilobytes of memory. There is zero practical reason to run a contemporary language on a forty-year-old eight-bit computer, and that's exactly the point. It's a feat of engineering done for the pure joy of seeing whether it can be done, and the answer is delightful. Second, a project called Librepods, billed as AirPods liberated. Apple deliberately locks its best AirPods features, like seamless switching and certain controls, to its own devices. Librepods reverse-engineers the protocol so those features work on Android and elsewhere. It's the open-source community refusing to accept that your hardware should only fully function inside one company's walled garden. And third, a deep technical write-up dissecting Apple's Sparse Image Format, ASIF, the disk image format Apple uses where the file only takes up space for the data actually written, not its full declared size. Someone reverse-engineered how it works under the hood, the kind of careful spelunking that keeps the platform understandable to people who don't work at Apple.

I always like ending here because these projects are the immune system of computing. While the giants fence off models and lock down features, there's a steady undercurrent of people who reverse-engineer, port, and liberate, just to keep things open and to prove it can be done. The AI headlines will keep getting louder. But somebody putting Swift on an Apple Two for no reason at all is a reminder of why a lot of us got into this in the first place.
