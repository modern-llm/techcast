# Huang Exits Nvidia, Microsoft's Long Outage, and AI Agents Get a Credit Card
*November 14, 2026*

Good morning, it's Saturday, November 14th, and this is TechCast Daily. Our biggest story: Jensen Huang is out at Nvidia, days after the board opened an investigation into him, and the open source AI world is asking what happens next. Also today: a Microsoft outage that has now run more than a day, OpenAI giving its shopping agents a wallet, and a new Gemini model with a very short name.

## We start with Nvidia, where the story has moved on. A quick reminder: on Sunday the board opened an investigation into Jensen Huang. Now the headlines say he has resigned.

Two TechCrunch pieces cover the fallout, and both focus on open source AI. We only have short summaries, so here is what they say. Nvidia has put a lot behind open source under Huang. It agreed to buy Hugging Face, the site where most open models are shared, for 12.9 billion dollars. It has released its own open-weight models, which means models whose trained numbers anyone can download and run. And it has argued in public against restrictions on open source AI. Now the company is in a leadership transition, and the question is whether those commitments belonged to Nvidia or to the man who made them. Hugging Face's chief executive has already answered one half of that. He says he will keep fighting for open source AI. That is a reassuring sentence, but notice who said it. It is a pledge from the company that was bought, not from the company that did the buying. What the summaries do not tell us is who replaces Huang, or on what terms he left.

Open source is easy to support when the boss is a fan. The real test comes at the first budget meeting after he is gone.

## Staying with Nvidia, a second story that will be read alongside the first.

TechCrunch reports new research saying the most advanced AI models from China were trained on Nvidia chips that US export rules had banned. We have only the headline and one sentence, so we do not know which models, which chips, or how the chips got there. But the claim matters even without the details. US export controls rest on one bet: that advanced chips are the choke point, and that if China cannot buy them, its best models fall behind. If the strongest Chinese models were trained on banned hardware, then one of two things happened. Either chips are still getting through, or they were bought and stockpiled before the rules tightened. In both cases the controls are doing less than their supporters said. For Nvidia, it lands at the worst moment. The company is already under scrutiny over who its customers are, and it has just lost the executive who spent years lobbying on exactly this issue.

Treat this as a claim to check, not a finding. But expect it to be quoted in Washington within the week.

## One more Nvidia item, and this one is a headline only, so we will keep it short.

A Hacker News post says Nvidia allegedly engineered scarcity by funding AI startups to buy its GPUs. The post has no text behind it that we can read, so we do not know who is alleging this or what evidence they have. Here is what the claim means if it holds up. Nvidia invests money in a young AI company. That company then spends much of the money on Nvidia chips. Nvidia books the sales, and the shortage of chips looks bigger, because demand now includes buyers Nvidia itself paid for. Critics call this circular financing. Nvidia has put money into many AI startups, so the pattern is plausible. What matters is the word allegedly. Investing in your customers is legal and common. Using it to fake demand is a different thing, and nobody has shown that here.

Until someone produces numbers, this is a question, not a scandal.

## Now to an outage. If your email has been strange since yesterday, this is why.

A global outage of Microsoft 365 began midday Thursday and was still going Friday morning. It hit Microsoft's email, cloud and AI services. Microsoft has published the full list of affected services. The most useful detail so far is what the company did to protect your mail. It extended the email timeout to 48 hours. Normally, when a sending server cannot deliver a message, it gives up after a short time and bounces it back to the sender. With a 48 hour timeout, the sending servers keep retrying for two days, so messages should arrive late instead of being lost. Microsoft says recovery actions are ongoing. One outside expert says this outage may turn out worse than the big Amazon outage or the CrowdStrike failure, which grounded flights and shut down hospitals. We do not yet have a cause, and the expert's comparison is a judgment, not a measurement. Duration matters here. A day-plus outage on email is a long time for businesses that run on it.

If you rely on Microsoft 365, tell your customers to resend anything urgent by another route, and do not assume silence means a message was seen.

## Now AI agents that spend money. An agent, again, is an AI that takes actions for you, not just answers questions.

OpenAI launched ChatGPT Wallet today. It works with Dots, the agent platform OpenAI launched earlier this year. The rollout is narrow. It is for ChatGPT Pro subscribers in the US, aged 18 or older, with a US billing address and an eligible US-issued American Express, Chase, Mastercard or Visa card. Once you add a card, your Dots agents can buy things when you ask them to. The safety design is the interesting part. The agent checks with you before every checkout. And OpenAI's support page says every purchase needs a security check with biometrics, meaning Face ID or a fingerprint. So the agent can find the item, fill the forms and get to the final step, but a human body has to approve the payment each time. TechCrunch calls it a limited rollout. This is the cautious version of agent shopping: the machine does the legwork, you hold the pen.

The harder question is what happens when people get tired of tapping approve. Every one of those prompts is a chore the agent was meant to remove.

## The other side of that deal is how agents pay, and a headline from Hacker News touches it.

Brave Search API now accepts agentic payments via the x402 protocol. The post has no text we can read, so this is what the title means. Brave sells search results to developers through an API, a way for programs to ask for searches. Normally you sign up, get a key and are billed monthly. x402 is a payment standard built on the web's old Payment Required error code. An agent asks for a search, the server replies that it costs a small amount, the agent pays, and the answer comes back. No account, no human, no invoice. For an AI agent that needs a few searches and nothing else, that is a lot simpler than a signup form. What we do not know is the price, or how many agents actually use it.

Small payments between machines have been promised for years. Agents may finally give them a reason to exist.

## Now Amazon, and a plan that is really about who controls the product list.

Reuters reports that Amazon wants to build a vault of data on every product sold anywhere on the internet, including products Amazon does not sell. The project is called the World Product Graph, and Amazon would make the information available through its own site. The reasoning is plain. Today, many shoppers research a purchase on Reddit or Google and then buy somewhere else. If Amazon holds the best product data, shoppers have less reason to leave, and they may end up buying on Amazon even when they started out looking at another store's item. The second reason is agents. When an AI shops for you, it needs a trusted list of products, prices and specs. If Amazon's index is the biggest, agents may lean on it by default, and then even people shopping through bots end up on Amazon. This is the same fight as search, moved to the agent era. Whoever owns the catalog shapes where the money goes.

Notice what Amazon is offering here: free reference data in exchange for being the place everything starts.

## Now a model release. Google has launched Gemini 4 Hydrogen.

Google keeps naming its models after elements, after the recent Argon and Carbon releases, and Hydrogen is the cheap, fast one. It replaces Gemini 3.8 Flash across Google's products, and unlike the last two releases it is available to everyone right away. Argon and Carbon were staged, starting with cybersecurity researchers and then reaching only the most expensive subscribers. Google's claims, which are its own numbers: Hydrogen has four thinking levels, low, medium, high and a new one called xhigh. Even on high it runs 2.5 times faster than 3.8 Flash. It cuts fabricated claims, meaning made-up facts, by more than five times. Its score on Terminal-Bench, a test of working in a command line, is more than triple the old Flash model. And on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, a third-party ranking, it scores 54 at xhigh, against 50 for Argon. A small model beating a larger sibling on one index is worth noting, but it is one index.

The practical news is access. Most people use the cheap model, so this is the model most people will meet.

## Now a backlash against Google, this time from parents.

Families protested in seven US cities on Wednesday against Google's push to put its AI in schools. In Chicago, dozens marched to Google's downtown office in cold rain, chanting that they do not want Gemini. The trigger was a Google statement last week. Google said it would turn off Search, Gemini and Gemini Notebook by default for schools, which it described as more choice, not less. But it confirmed that students will have no way to use Google Search without AI. Parents point out what that leaves. The only way to avoid AI answers is to add the minus-ai trick to every query, and few children will remember to. Protesters are calling for moratoriums, meaning pauses, on AI in schools. Notice the gap in Google's message. Gemini can be switched off, but the AI in search cannot, and search is the tool students use most.

Google gave schools a switch for everything except the thing that matters most. Parents noticed.

## Next, robots. OpenAI has disbanded its robotics team.

Robotics teams usually build special software for one machine. If so, robotics becomes one more thing the main model does. That fits a pattern. Google DeepMind's robot software, which we covered earlier this week, also builds on a general model. Disbanding a team can mean success or retreat, and a headline cannot tell us which.

## Now coding agents. Anthropic has upgraded Claude Code.

The headline says Anthropic upgrades Claude Code Projects to run parallel AI workers. Again there is no article behind it, so I will only explain the idea. Until now, a coding agent mostly works through a task one step after another. Parallel workers means several copies of the agent work on different parts of a project at the same time, like several engineers each taking a ticket. The speedup can be large for work that splits cleanly, such as updating many files or running separate checks. The cost goes up too, because each worker uses its own share of tokens, the units that AI usage is billed in. And there is a new problem: workers can change the same file and collide. What we do not know is the limit on how many run at once, who gets access, or how it is priced.

Faster is only useful if someone still reads what comes out. Ten workers produce ten times the code to review.

## A good question next, from a well-known game developer.

Jonathan Blow, who made Braid and The Witness, has a video titled: If AI solved coding, why is my software still the same? The post is just the link, so I cannot tell you what he argues in it. But the question stands by itself, and it is fair. We have heard for two years that AI writes most of the code. If that were changing what software is like, you would expect apps to get noticeably better, faster or cheaper. Blow's point, from the title, is that the software on his machine feels the same as before. Possible answers are that code was never the slow part, that AI output needs as much checking as writing, or that the gains are real but have gone into the hands of companies who ship the same product with fewer people. Blow is known for being blunt about software quality, so expect him to lean toward the first.

Watch the video before you take my guess. The question is better than any answer I can give from a title.

## Now a funding story from the agent tooling world.

LangChain, the startup behind a widely used framework for building AI agents, has reached a three billion dollar valuation. The company says it now has 7,000 customers for LangSmith, its tool for managing agents. LangSmith is described as model and platform agnostic, which means it works with models from any company and runs anywhere. That matters because most big AI labs want you inside their own stack. A neutral tool sits above all of them. Think of it like a control room for agents. When an agent fails, someone must trace what it did and why, and LangSmith is built for that job. Seven thousand customers is the headline number, but notice we do not know how many pay, or how much. The risk for LangChain is that the labs add the same features to their own platforms for free.

Three billion dollars says investors believe agents will break often enough that someone needs to watch them.

## Now the darkest story of the day, from a person who knows the field well.

Paul Christiano, who sits on the board of the OpenAI Foundation and runs the Alignment Research Center, gave the first of three lectures this week in Toronto. His line to the audience: for most people in the room, the most likely reason they will die is an AI accident. He defines an AI takeover as a situation where AI systems that pursue goals are the ones calling the shots on Earth, in the way humans do today, and where any human trying to stop them would be as ineffective as one person trying to stop the US military. He said that would likely mean mass death and perhaps an environment humans cannot live in. The headline in Ars Technica says a takeover could be months away. That is the outlet's framing. The article text we have does not give his timeline, so I will not put a date on it. What it does show is that a board member of the nonprofit that oversees OpenAI is saying this in public.

You can disagree with the odds. It is still worth noticing who is speaking.

## Now power, which keeps deciding how fast AI can grow.

SoftBank lost more than three billion dollars preordering natural gas turbines to power AI data centers. TechCrunch asks what the loss means for power markets, and its answer is that it may put a cap on AI's appetite for natural gas. The background: turbines are in short supply, with waits of years, so companies reserve them early. SoftBank placed big orders for the AI buildout and then lost money on them. We only have the summary, not the full piece, so the reason for the loss is not clear to me. But the signal is useful. If one of the loudest spenders on AI infrastructure is taking a hit on its gas bets, other developers will think harder before ordering. That would cool demand for turbines, and lower the odds that gas supplies most of the new data center power. It would also push builders toward other sources, including solar, batteries and nuclear.

Every AI forecast quietly assumes the power arrives on time. This is a reminder that someone has to pay for it first.

## Three quick headlines to finish, starting with math.

AI unlocks Gödel's notebooks, said to contain hundreds of proved theorems. Kurt Gödel was the logician who proved that any consistent math system has true statements it cannot prove. He wrote in an old German shorthand that few people can read. The title suggests AI has now transcribed these notebooks. There is no text behind the post, so we do not know who did it, which model was used, or who has checked the claim of hundreds of proved theorems. That is the question we have asked all week: who checks the answer? A transcription can be wrong in small ways that change a proof. The word said is carrying a lot in that title.

If it holds up, historians get a new source. Until a human expert signs off, treat the count as unverified.

## Next, a research write-up from Hacker News.

After 600 experiments, here's what I discovered about JEPA world models. JEPA stands for Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture, an approach pushed by Yann LeCun. Instead of predicting every pixel of what happens next, the model predicts a compressed summary of it, and the idea is that this teaches the model how the world works. The post has no text we can read, so I cannot tell you what the author found. But six hundred experiments is a serious number for one person, and independent results on JEPA are rare. Most of what we hear comes from the labs that built it. We will watch for whether others reproduce the findings.

Hands-on results from outside the big labs are where new methods get tested.

## Last, a small milestone for running AI on your own machine.

Ggml version 1.0.0 has been released. Ggml is the library underneath llama dot cpp and many other tools that let you run AI models on a laptop or phone, without a cloud service. Reaching 1.0 usually means the maker promises the interfaces will be stable, so developers can build on it without code breaking every few weeks. That is a real change for a project that has moved fast. The post has no text, so we do not know what else is in this release. After Nvidia's open source commitments went into question today, a tool that runs open models anywhere on ordinary hardware looks like a useful backstop.

Boring version numbers are often the sign that a tool has grown up.
