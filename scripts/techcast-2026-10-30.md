# Google's AI Search Earns Less, Azure Shows Its Number, and an AI Investor Buys ADT
*October 30, 2026*

This is TechCast Daily for Friday, October 30, 2026. Google just had one of the worst days after an earnings report in its history, and the reason is something publishers have been saying for a year: AI search makes less money than the old kind. Also today, Microsoft finally says what its cloud earns, two cybersecurity models with opposite ideas about who gets to use them, and an AI investor buying a home security company for fifteen billion dollars.

## Start with Google. On Wednesday its revenue went up by a quarter, and its stock still fell almost twelve percent. Here is why.

Here are the numbers. Alphabet, Google's parent company, took in about 127.6 billion dollars in the third quarter, July through September. That is up 25 percent on a year ago. Net income was a little over 26 billion dollars, which sounds fine until you see it is almost 9 billion lower than the same quarter last year. Earnings per share came in at 2 dollars 12 cents, against the 3 dollars 2 cents analysts expected. The stock fell about 12 percent, at one point 13, one of the largest drops after an earnings report in the company's history. The miss was part of it. Two things in the report did the rest. First, Google said AI search earns less than ordinary search. When you get an AI answer instead of a page of links, there are fewer places to put an ad and fewer reasons to click one. Second, Google expects to spend even more on AI next year. So the product it is pushing hardest earns less per search and costs more to run. Markets punish that combination. Google's answer is more ads. Alongside the results, it announced that ads inside AI search are expanding worldwide. That means ads in AI Overviews, the summary box above ordinary results, and in AI Mode, the chat-style search tab. TechCrunch notes this comes as growth in Google's core ad business slows. Put plainly: the old ad business is growing more slowly, the new product pays less per use, so Google is putting ads into the new product everywhere at once. One part of the report was clearly good. Google Cloud beat expectations, crossing 30 billion dollars in quarterly revenue, about a billion more than analysts guessed. Keep that number in mind for the next story. Now the other side of the same shift. Reddit also reported this week, and its chief executive, Steve Huffman, was blunt. Reddit said its search traffic in October dropped by more than a million referrals a week on average, and Huffman blamed part of that on AI Mode and AI Overviews. In his words: The reality is, AI Mode does not drive traffic. Not for us, and not to anybody, as far as I know. He called it a fundamental shift in how Google works. There is an irony here. In early 2024 Google began paying Reddit to use its posts to train AI, and Google search started showing Reddit threads far more often, which sent Reddit's traffic up sharply. Now those same posts help fill AI answers that keep people from clicking through.

Put the two reports side by side and you see one problem from both ends. Google's AI answers send fewer people to sites like Reddit, and they earn Google less than the old links did. Google's fix is to sell ads inside the answer. That may repair Google's numbers. Reddit's call suggests it won't repair anyone else's.

## Microsoft reported the same day, and for once it put a dollar figure on a number it has kept vague for years.

Microsoft has always reported how fast Azure, its cloud business, was growing, in percent, while folding the dollars into a larger group. This quarter it gave the figure for the first time: 33.2 billion dollars in the three months to September 30. TechCrunch's reading is that Microsoft is showing it now because Azure is getting close to Amazon Web Services, the largest cloud provider. Companies tend to start publishing a number once the number looks good. For comparison, that puts Azure roughly three billion dollars ahead of the Google Cloud figure we just heard. The two lines are not drawn around exactly the same products, but the order is clear: Amazon first, Microsoft closing in, Google third. The second number is about OpenAI. Microsoft said it made 10 billion dollars in revenue in the quarter from its commercial arrangements with OpenAI, including revenue-sharing payments. That is ten billion dollars in three months from one partner. Microsoft also owns a large stake in OpenAI, so it gains twice when OpenAI grows: as its cloud supplier and as a part owner. That cuts both ways. It shows how much Microsoft earns from the relationship. It also shows how much of Microsoft's AI business still rests on one company it does not control, and one that has spent the past year signing computing deals with Microsoft's rivals.

And this came the same week Microsoft said it will offer buyouts to about a quarter of its workforce. Rising cloud revenue and fewer staff in the same week tells you where the money is going: into data centers, not people.

## A short follow-up to Wednesday's episode on Anthropic's IPO filing.

Anthropic has launched tools that show business customers what their spending on its models is buying. TechCrunch frames it as the company lifting the curtain on how customers spend money on its models, just ahead of the planned listing. The report has few details about the tools themselves, so we won't guess at them. The timing is the point. Large companies are now asking a hard question about their AI budgets: what did we get for this money? A vendor that answers with its own usage reports has an easier renewal conversation, and an easier time telling investors its revenue will last.

It is the filing's worry about keeping customers, turned into a product.

## Now two frontier models built around the same job, cybersecurity, with opposite answers to who should get to use them.

First, Google. Two weeks after rolling out Gemini 4 Argon, it has announced Gemini 4 Carbon. This is not the smaller, faster Flash model people were waiting for. It is an improved Argon. And like Argon, you cannot use it yet. Google is releasing it in stages, which could take a while, because it says the model is that capable. The claims are mostly about security. Google says Carbon makes a significant leap in finding, confirming and fixing vulnerabilities, meaning security holes in software. It says Carbon is twice as likely as Argon to write code with no vulnerabilities. The Chrome team claims Carbon is ten times better at writing high-quality patches than models from other companies. And on WebDev Arena, a public leaderboard where people vote between models on web-building tasks, Carbon gained 89 points and now ranks fourth. Note who is making these claims. Apart from the leaderboard, every number comes from Google, and the claim about rival models comes from Google's own Chrome team. The leaderboard result deserves a second look. A model described as too capable to release ranks fourth on a public leaderboard. Both can be true, since a model can be excellent at finding exploitable bugs without being the best at building websites. But the tone of Ars Technica's write-up, stop me if you've heard this one before, says what a lot of people now think: too dangerous to release has become a launch line as much as a safety decision. Mistral, the French lab, went the other way. It has released the weights of Mistral Large 4, a model with about a trillion parameters. Parameters are the numbers a model learns during training, and the weights are the file that holds them, the thing you need to run the model yourself. A trillion puts it among the largest models anyone has released openly. Mistral is pitching it at the same job, cybersecurity, and its argument is control. With a closed model, your defenses run on someone else's servers, under someone else's rules, and you send them your code and your logs. With open weights you run the model on your own hardware, keep sensitive data in house, and adapt it to your systems. It fits the case IBM's chief executive made, which we covered on Monday: open models are now good enough to run on your own premises. The case against is the one Google is making without saying it. The skill that finds a hole for a defender finds it for an attacker too, and once weights are public, nobody can take them back. Mistral is betting defenders gain more than attackers do. Why is everyone suddenly selling security models? TechCrunch spoke with the chief technology officer of Wiz, the cloud security company, who says a Vulnpocalypse, his word for a flood of vulnerabilities, is coming for the internet's aging code. The backdrop is last week. Two critical bugs turned up in PHP, the open source language behind roughly 70 percent of websites. Organizations scrambled to patch, and some took their sites offline until they could. The story has left people's feeds. The cleanup has not finished. Put that next to today's two launches and the connection is plain: there is a vast amount of old code running the web, and AI is getting good at reading code for holes. Whoever reads it first, defender or attacker, decides how this goes.

Both strategies have a fair case. Google says its best bug finder should stay locked up for now. Mistral says defenders need one they own. Neither changes the size of the pile of old code, and that pile is the real story.

## Staying with Google for a smaller, more practical change.

Gemini can now create, preview and edit files inside the chat. That covers PDFs, Google Docs, Google Sheets, slide decks, Markdown and plain text files, and code. Earlier this year Gemini learned to generate files you could download. Now you can change them without leaving the app. You can open a file full screen, highlight a passage, and ask Gemini about it or tell it what to rewrite. It is available to all Gemini users starting today. One catch: PDFs work on the web and on phones, but every other format is web only for now. OpenAI and Anthropic have had similar side-by-side editing windows in ChatGPT and Claude for a while. What Google adds is its own formats: a real Doc or Sheet, not a copy you paste somewhere else.

Features like this change habits quietly. If the draft, the edits and the questions all happen in one window, fewer people open Docs directly, and the chat becomes the way into Google's office apps.

## Now money changing hands, three deals in one day.

The biggest is also the strangest. Thrive Holdings is buying ADT, the home security company, for 15.3 billion dollars. Thrive Holdings is the arm of the venture firm Thrive Capital that buys ordinary, established businesses and tries to rebuild them around AI, and OpenAI has a stake in it. The stated plan is to bring AI to home security. This is the clearest test yet of a theory many AI investors hold: the fastest way to make money from AI is not to sell software to old businesses, but to buy the old businesses and change how they run. ADT has millions of customers, monitoring centers where people watch alarms around the clock, and a large crew of installers and technicians. That is exactly the kind of labor-heavy operation AI is supposed to make cheaper. We don't have the details of the plan. The obvious place to start is false alarms. A large share of alarm calls turn out to be nothing, and every one costs a monitoring agent's time and sometimes a police visit. Software that checks the camera before a person picks up the phone is easy to picture. But this is also a business where a mistake means a missed break-in or a missed fire, which raises the cost of getting the AI wrong. Second, and here we only have a headline: DoorDash is to acquire Instacart. These are two of the biggest delivery companies in the United States, DoorDash best known for restaurant meals, Instacart for groceries. DoorDash has been buying a lot lately, including Deliveroo in Britain last year. We don't have the price or the terms. The obvious question is whether competition regulators will let two of the biggest names in on-demand delivery combine. Third, a smaller deal that matters to developers. Deepgram, which makes speech recognition and voice AI, is acquiring Daily. Daily runs real-time audio and video infrastructure, and it makes Pipecat, a popular open source framework for building voice agents, the kind of AI you talk to out loud. With it, Deepgram gets the plumbing that carries a live conversation, not just the models that listen and speak. Voice agents are one of the busiest corners of AI right now: customer service lines, appointment booking, drive-through orders. Owning the connection layer makes Deepgram harder to swap out.

If you build on Pipecat, watch whether it stays neutral, or slowly starts to favor Deepgram's own speech models.

## Apple's new Macs were on Wednesday's show. Today, the first hands-on.

Samuel Axon at Ars Technica got about five minutes with the new touchscreen MacBook Pro, on a very crowded showroom floor in Los Angeles. His first impression is that it is less of a departure from the old design than he expected. The first thing he noticed was the weight. He uses a 14-inch MacBook Pro with the M1 Max chip every day, and the new one felt noticeably lighter as soon as he picked it up. Not a different class of laptop, but a real difference. What stole the show was the screen. It is OLED, the display type where each pixel makes its own light, so black areas are truly black, and he calls it outstanding.

Five minutes in a crowd is not a review. The real question, whether touching a laptop screen is actually useful, needs weeks of daily use to answer. But the display is the upgrade every owner will notice every day.

## Two stories about getting more out of hardware, one very large and one very small.

The large one is a Hacker News post where we only have the headline: Grace Blackwell needs a new scheduler, and with one you get 27 percent more usable GPUs from the same racks. Grace Blackwell is Nvidia's rack-scale system, where dozens of GPUs in one rack are wired together with a very fast link and work almost like a single machine. A scheduler is the software that decides which job runs on which GPUs. Twenty-seven percent is not a rounding error: for every hundred GPUs a company could actually use before, it could use about a hundred and twenty-seven after. We can guess at the reason for the waste: a scheduler that does not understand how a rack is wired can leave GPUs stranded, sitting idle because no waiting job fits the gaps. That is our reading, not the author's, and we can't check the method from a headline. But if a gain like that holds up, it is worth a lot of money, given what these racks cost. The small one is a startup launch. Simantic, from Y Combinator's fall 2026 batch, was started by two former firmware engineers from Neuralink and Tesla, who worked on early prototypes of the Cybertruck and the Roadster. Firmware is the low-level code that runs on the chips inside devices. Their problem: an AI agent can write firmware in seconds, but testing it means flashing it onto a physical board and running tests by hand with expensive tools whose software is a decade old. So the agent writes fast, then waits for a person. Simantic offers virtual development boards you can program and run with one API call from your terminal, so an agent can test its own firmware and decide what to do next from the results. It also offers an AI code reviewer for firmware.

The open question is how faithful a virtual board can be. Firmware bugs often hide in timing and in how a real chip behaves at its limits, which is exactly where simulators fall short. If Simantic gets close enough, it removes the slowest step left for coding agents. Both of these stories say the same thing: writing code is cheap now, and running it on real hardware is where the time and money go.

## To finish, three more Hacker News items where all we have is the headline, so we'll keep each one short.

First, Stack Overflow, the question and answer site a generation of programmers learned from, now gets fewer than a thousand new questions a month. At its peak it took well over a hundred thousand. Most of that drop is developers asking chatbots instead. The catch is that those chatbots learned to answer by reading Stack Overflow. If nobody posts new questions, there is less fresh, public writing about new tools and new bugs for the next models to learn from. Stack Overflow's owners have already sold access to its archive to OpenAI and Google for AI training. Those deals pay for the past. They do not create new answers. Second, a piece titled Zombie stats: where the numbers AI quotes come from. A zombie statistic is a number that keeps getting repeated long after its source has vanished, or one that never had a real source at all. Chatbots repeat what is common on the web, so they repeat these too, and they say them with total confidence. Next time a model hands you a neat percentage, ask it where the number came from, then check. Third, the strangest. Someone has built what they call the Qwenputer: a working, deterministic risk five processor inside Qwen three point five, Alibaba's open model. Risk five, spelled R I S C V, is an open design for processor chips. Deterministic means the same input always gives the same output, something language models do not normally promise. So, going by the title, the model's own machinery has been made to behave as a real CPU. Useful? Probably not. A striking demonstration of what a neural network can be made to compute? Very much so.

That Stack Overflow number is the one to remember. It shows how fast the habit of millions of programmers changed.
