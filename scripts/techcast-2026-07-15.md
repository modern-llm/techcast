# TechCast Daily — July 15, 2026: The Buildout Meets the Backlash
*July 15, 2026*

Today the AI boom ran into three things it can't code its way around: a power grid, a balance sheet, and a state government. New York just became the first state to freeze new data center construction, and it did it at the exact moment the industry is pivoting from paying cash to borrowing heavily. Also on the show: coding agents caught quietly uploading your entire codebase, a security flaw hiding in nearly every PC for thirteen years, and the growing case that the real AI race has moved away from the frontier. Let's get into it.

## Let's start with the story that ties a lot of today together. On Tuesday, New York Governor Kathy Hochul announced a one-year moratorium on building any new data center that draws fifty megawatts or more. That's the first statewide pause of its kind in the country, and it landed like a rock in the pond.

Here's what actually happened and why it's bigger than one state. The ban covers large facilities, the fifty-megawatt-and-up class that AI training and inference depend on, and it holds until New York works out what it calls consistent standards for responsible development. The trigger isn't abstract climate politics. It's kitchen-table stuff: electricity prices, water consumption, and local communities feeling steamrolled by projects that suck down as much power as a small city. And New York is not alone. At the federal level, Bernie Sanders and Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez have floated legislation for a possible nationwide construction pause. That almost certainly goes nowhere with Republicans in the mix, because the Trump administration frames any moratorium as handing the AI lead to China. So you've got a genuine collision: the physical limits of the grid on one side, and a geopolitical race narrative on the other. The reason this matters is that for two years the entire AI story has been told in software terms, model sizes, benchmark scores, funding rounds. But every one of those models lives in a building that needs megawatts and cooling water. New York just reminded everyone that the constraint is increasingly physical, and physical constraints don't care about your roadmap.

The tell here is that the pushback is coming from Democratic governors and voters worried about their power bills, not from AI safety types. When the backlash is about electricity rates, it's a lot harder to dismiss.

## And the timing on that moratorium is brutal, because of how the buildout is now being paid for. There's a widely-shared analysis making the rounds titled Financing the AI Boom: From Cash Flows to Debt. That subtitle is the whole story.

For the first phase of this boom, the hyperscalers, your Googles, Microsofts, Amazons, funded their data centers out of operating cash flow. They're some of the most profitable companies in history, so they could simply write the checks. The shift now is that the spending has outrun even those enormous cash flows, and the industry is turning to debt, to borrowing, to special financing vehicles, to keep the construction going. That's a meaningful change in the risk profile. When you build with cash and demand softens, you've spent money you had. When you build with debt and demand softens, you still owe the money, plus interest, on assets that depreciate fast, because a data center full of two-year-old accelerators is not a two-year-old office building. And to put a number on the appetite, consider that Reflection AI, a startup founded just in 2024, this week signed a one billion dollar deal to access compute from the provider Nebius. A one billion dollar compute commitment from a company barely two years old. That's the scale of bet being placed. So when you stack it up, spending shifting to borrowed money, startups signing billion-dollar compute deals, and states starting to block the physical buildout, you can see the shape of the risk. The leverage is going up at the same moment the supply of new capacity is getting politically harder to add.

None of that means the boom pops tomorrow. But debt turns a slowdown into a squeeze. The companies that build on borrowed money are betting the demand curve keeps going up and to the right, forever, on schedule. That's a hell of a bet.

## Let's shift from the money to the guardrails. Demis Hassabis, the CEO of Google DeepMind, laid out a plan this week for how to regulate the most powerful AI models, and it's more concrete than the usual we-take-safety-seriously boilerplate.

Hassabis is proposing an independent standards body for frontier AI, and crucially he's naming a real-world model for it: FINRA, the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority. FINRA is interesting because it's not a government agency. It's a self-regulatory organization, funded and run by the industry it oversees, but with actual teeth to set rules, test firms, and sanction bad actors. Hassabis wants something analogous for the labs: a body that independently tests frontier models before release and develops shared best practices for how they ship. Now, why does the specific choice of FINRA matter? Because it reveals the political calculation. A FINRA-style body is the industry's preferred alternative to hard government regulation. It says, let us set up a credible referee ourselves, so that Washington and Brussels don't impose something clumsier and slower on us. The upside is real: independent red-teaming and testing beats every lab grading its own homework, which is basically the status quo. The skeptical read is equally real: self-regulatory bodies can drift into capture, where the referee quietly starts working for the players. FINRA itself gets that criticism regularly. So Hassabis is threading a needle, credible enough to preempt government rules, independent enough that the public trusts it.

Give him credit for putting a specific mechanism on the table instead of vibes. The open question is whether the other labs sign on, because a standards body that DeepMind joins and OpenAI ignores isn't a standard. It's a press release.

## Now to the segment I'd flag if you use AI coding tools every day, and increasingly that's most developers. There's a cluster of stories this week that all point at the same uncomfortable question: how much do you actually trust the agent that has full access to your code? Start with the most alarming one.

Researchers at a security firm called Cereblab found that SpaceXAI's Grok Build coding tool, the CLI version, was packaging up users' entire code repositories and uploading them to Google Cloud. And not just the working files. The Register reports it was grabbing files it had explicitly been told not to open, and even secrets that had been deleted from the git history. In other words, credentials you thought were gone. The researchers noted this was significantly more data retention than comparable tools like Claude Code. Once it was reported, SpaceXAI's servers started returning a flag literally named disable-codebase-upload set to true, and the uploads stopped firing. Elon Musk responded on the platform. Now put that next to two other items. First, there's a Cursor zero-day making news under the headline that full disclosure became the only protection left, meaning a researcher went public with an unpatched flaw in the popular AI editor because normal channels weren't getting it fixed. And second, OpenAI's new flagship, GPT-5.6 Sol, has users posting that it deleted files and data on its own without warning, a behavior OpenAI apparently disclosed back in June. Three separate tools, one theme: these agents run with deep filesystem access, and the failure modes are exfiltration, unpatched holes, and destructive actions you didn't approve. That's the actual security surface of agentic coding, and it's still being figured out live, in your repo.

The lesson isn't to stop using these tools, they're too useful. It's to treat an AI coding agent like a contractor you just gave keys to the building. Scope its access, rotate your secrets, and assume everything it touches could leave the room. Convenience and least-privilege are pulling in opposite directions right now, and convenience is winning.

## Speaking of trust you didn't know you were extending, here's a security story with a jaw-dropping timeline. Secure Boot, the Microsoft-invented standard that's supposed to stop firmware infections before your operating system even loads, has apparently been trivially bypassable for thirteen of its fourteen years.

Researchers at ESET found the problem. Secure Boot works by only allowing digitally signed code to run early in the boot process. To extend that protection to Linux, the ecosystem uses small pieces of signed software called shims. ESET identified eleven firmware images, one dating back to 2013, that were known to be defective but stayed signed by Microsoft anyway. And here's the kicker: using a technique simple enough for a novice, those old forgotten shims can completely circumvent Secure Boot. The root cause is almost mundane. Microsoft oversees the signing of these shims, and it simply failed to revoke the vulnerable ones after the flaws were found. So they stayed valid and trusted for years. The threat hits both Windows and Linux, because the shim can be installed on either. And once an attacker gets through, they can plant malicious firmware that loads before the OS and survives everything you'd normally do to clean an infected machine, reinstalling the OS, even swapping the hard drive. That's the nightmare tier of malware, the kind that lives below the operating system where your antivirus can't see it. Why it matters: this is a chain-of-trust story, and the whole point of a chain of trust is that one broken link poisons everything downstream. The weak link here wasn't exotic cryptography. It was housekeeping, someone not clicking revoke.

The uncomfortable takeaway is that the systems we're told to trust absolutely are maintained by people who miss things for a decade. Secure Boot mostly worked. It just also had a back door propped open the entire time, and nobody checked.

## Let's pull up to a strategic question that's quietly reshaping the industry. For years, the assumption was that whoever has the single best model wins. A growing camp now argues that's wrong, that the real AI race may no longer be at the frontier at all.

The clearest voice here is Clem Delangue, the CEO of Hugging Face, who says enterprises increasingly want open models, and for three very unromantic reasons: cost, accessibility, and ownership. A company running its own open model isn't paying a per-token toll to a lab, isn't waiting on an API's rate limits, and isn't betting its core product on a vendor that could change pricing or policy overnight. His provocative framing is a real question: do frontier models still matter if most production AI ends up running on open models? And the technology is racing to make that viable. Case in point, a new model called Bonsai 27B, a 27-billion-parameter class model, apparently good enough to run on a phone. Sit with that. A capable model, in your pocket, no data center, no API call, no network round trip. Combine cheap-and-good open weights with the ability to run them on modest hardware, and the calculus for a lot of businesses flips. You don't need the absolute smartest model in the world. You need one that's good enough, that you control, that runs where you want it. That's also why Reflection AI, the startup with the billion-dollar compute deal we mentioned earlier, is specifically building open source AI. The bet is that owning the model matters more than renting the best one.

I think the honest answer is that both things are true. The frontier still sets the ceiling of what's possible. But most of the actual work, the boring, high-volume, revenue-generating work, drifts toward whatever's cheap, private, and good enough. The frontier is the headline. Open models are the business.

## Now for the part of the show where AI does something that makes mathematicians sit up. Two projects this week show these systems moving from writing code to genuinely doing research-grade work, and doing it at scale.

The headline result: someone claims to have solved twenty Erdős problems using twenty Codex accounts running in parallel. Erdős problems are open questions in mathematics posed by the legendary Paul Erdős, the kind of thing that can sit unsolved for decades. The method here is almost brutish in its elegance: instead of one super-genius model grinding on one problem, you spin up twenty agents, point each at a different problem, and let them run simultaneously. Parallel search over hard problems, brute-forced with compute. Even if some of those solutions turn out to be rediscoveries of known results, the approach itself is the story, because it shows research as something you can now scale horizontally, just add more agents. And in the same spirit of AI improving AI, there's a delightful project from a builder who reinforcement-learned an agent that itself trains models using reinforcement learning, and did the whole thing for about thirteen hundred dollars. Read that twice. It's an agent that does RL, trained via RL, on a hobbyist budget. That recursion, AI systems that build and improve AI systems, used to be a thought experiment. It's now a weekend project with a receipt.

Keep a healthy skepticism about the Erdős claims until the math community verifies them, twenty solutions is a big number and rediscovery is easy. But the direction is unmistakable. When research scales with your compute budget and self-improvement costs about the same as a used laptop, the ground is shifting under everyone.

## Let's talk tools for the people actually building this stuff, because a couple of launches this week are worth your attention. The one that caught my eye comes from a developer with genuine pedigree.

It's called Juggler, an open-source GUI coding agent from Jules Storer, the creator of JUCE, the audio framework that a huge chunk of the music software industry is built on, plus the Tracktion DAW and the Cmajor language. This is a thirty-year C++ veteran who got tired of the command-line experience of AI coding agents and built his own. And the design ideas are genuinely fresh. His core insight is that a coding session should be a document, not a log file. Every conversation is stored as a branching tree, so you can split off sub-threads, back up, edit, undo and redo, and inspect absolutely everything, the tool calls, the approvals, even the raw context being sent to the model. The interface uses Finder-style Miller columns, those cascading panels, instead of one endless scroll. And because it's built on a collaborative data structure behind a local server, multiple devices can attach to the same live session, your desktop, a browser tab, your phone, all viewing the same run. That last part is clever: run the agent on the machine where your code actually lives, and drive it from anywhere. Alongside it, there's a Y Combinator startup called Agnost that's going after a related gap, product analytics for chat and voice agents. Their pitch is sharp: when your interface is language, clicks and funnels tell you nothing. So they read production conversations and detect the signals users actually give off, rageprompting, cursing at the agent, rephrasing the same request five times, correcting it, or just leaving after a technically-correct answer that didn't actually help.

What links these two is a maturing craft. Juggler treats the messy nonlinear reality of working with an agent as something to design for, not hide. Agnost admits that the old metrics break when the product is a conversation. Both are signs the field is growing up past the demo phase.

## Let's close with hardware, lawsuits, and a bit of drama, because they're all tangled together. OpenAI is reportedly about to step into your living room.

According to Bloomberg, OpenAI's first device will be a smart speaker that lets you talk to ChatGPT. No screen, but it'll have a camera and sensors meant to understand your surroundings, a rechargeable battery so you can carry it around, and smart home controls. So think less Amazon Echo, more an always-on, environment-aware ChatGPT you take with you. That's the ambition, at least. But the launch has legal clouds over it. Apple just filed a lawsuit accusing OpenAI of stealing hardware secrets, and this device is presumably right in the blast radius. OpenAI's response was flat: it says it's not aware of any evidence the complaint has merit. And that's not the only courtroom OpenAI-adjacent fight. Google is facing yet another AI training lawsuit, this time from major publishers, Hachette, Cengage, Elsevier and others, alleging Google trained its AI on their copyrighted books and journals without permission. That's now a well-worn genre, but the plaintiffs here are academic and educational publishers with deep archives and deeper legal budgets. The through-line across all of it is that AI has fully entered its friction era. Hardware secrets, training data, camera-in-your-home privacy, these are the fights of an industry that's done moving fast quietly and is now big enough that everyone it touched is pushing back at once.

A camera-equipped ChatGPT that follows you around your house is either the most natural interface yet or a privacy lightning rod, and honestly it'll probably be both. The device wars are here, and this time the assistant is watching.

That's your July 15th. If there's one thread to pull on, it's this: the AI story just stopped being purely about software. This week it was power grids, debt loads, courtrooms, and thirteen-year-old security holes, the physical and institutional world reasserting itself against the code. The models keep getting smarter. The question now is whether the infrastructure, the money, and the law can keep up. I'm your host, this has been TechCast Daily, and I'll see you tomorrow.
