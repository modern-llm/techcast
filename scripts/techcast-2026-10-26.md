# AI Gets Cheap, the Payoff Doesn't, and the iPhone Duo Cracks
*October 26, 2026*

This is TechCast Daily for Monday, October 26th. OpenAI and Anthropic have cut what they charge for their models by as much as 80 percent in a few months, and the head of IBM says models anyone can download are now good enough to run in your own building. AI is getting cheap, fast. The awkward part: a Wall Street economist says it still is not paying off for most companies. Let's get into it.

## Start with price. Over the last few months, OpenAI and Anthropic have cut what they charge developers for their models by up to 80 percent. A post that hit Hacker News this weekend asks why, and it is a better question than it sounds.

A quick note on how these companies charge. Developers who build on a model pay by the token. A token is a chunk of text, roughly three quarters of a word. Prices are quoted per million tokens, with one rate for what you send in and a higher one for what comes back. When that price falls by 80 percent, a feature that cost a dollar a day per user now costs twenty cents. That is the difference between something a startup cannot afford and something it gives away for free. So why would the two leading labs cut this hard? There are three obvious answers. First, competition from below. Open weight models, meaning models whose files anyone can download and run, have closed much of the gap. Several of the strongest come from Chinese labs such as DeepSeek and Alibaba's Qwen team, and they cost a fraction of the American ones. Second, the cost of serving each answer keeps falling. Newer chips, and tricks such as reusing work when many requests start with the same text, mean the same answer takes less hardware. Third, market share. Whoever becomes the default model inside a company's software is hard to dislodge, so a price cut now is a bet on being locked in later. My guess is that all three are true, and the first one forced the timing.

Cheaper is good news for anyone building with these models. It also tells you where the pressure is coming from, and that brings us to IBM.

## IBM's chief executive says open weight models are now good enough, and cheap enough, to run on premises.

On premises means on a company's own servers, in its own building or its own data center, instead of sending every request to OpenAI or Anthropic over the internet. For banks, hospitals and governments, which are IBM's core customers, that matters for more than cost. Their data never leaves their hands. No outside company can swap the model under them, or raise the price next quarter. Two years ago, running your own model was mostly a privacy choice, and you accepted a worse model in exchange. The claim now is that the trade-off has largely gone. IBM is not a neutral voice here. It makes its own open models, called Granite, and it sells the hardware and the consulting to run them. So take this as a sales pitch as well as a forecast. But it fits the price cuts. If a big customer can get a good enough answer from a model on its own machines, a frontier lab has to charge less to keep that customer.

So the price of AI is being pushed down from two directions at once. The next question is whether anyone outside tech is making money from it.

## Apollo is one of the biggest investment firms on Wall Street, and its chief economist has put the case bluntly.

His words: despite massive investments and widespread hype around artificial intelligence, the technology has delivered zero financial payoff to corporate America outside of tech. Outside of tech is the key phrase. Nobody disputes that Nvidia, Microsoft and the cloud companies are making money from AI. They sell the chips, the servers and the models. The question is whether the companies buying all of it, the insurers, retailers, manufacturers and airlines, see it in their profits. His answer is that it does not show up in their numbers yet. There are two ways to read that. The gloomy one is that AI makes a great demo and a weak business tool, and companies are spending because they are afraid of being left behind. The patient one comes from history. Electric power and, later, personal computers both took a decade or more to show up in productivity figures, because firms first had to reorganize how work was done around them. Bolting a chatbot onto an old process rarely changes the bottom line. Redesigning the process can, and that is slow. Either reading is a problem for the people selling AI. The data centers being built right now assume customers will pay a lot, for a long time. If those customers cannot point to a return, the price cuts we just covered look less like generosity and more like necessity.

Hold that thought about who pays for AI. The next story is about who has been paying for it without being asked.

## The Verge has a long look at what Google's AI changes to search have done to online publishing. Here is the short version.

For about twenty years the deal was simple. Publishers let Google read their pages. Google showed links. People clicked, and the publisher earned money from ads or subscriptions on its own site. A whole industry, search engine optimization, grew up around getting to the top of that list. Then Google started answering the question itself. AI Overviews, the generated summary at the top of the results, and the chat-style AI Mode give you an answer built from publishers' pages, and many people never click through. Publishers have reported steep drops in search traffic as these features spread. Some have sued. Penske Media, which owns Rolling Stone and Variety, took Google to court last year. Google's line has been that AI search sends better quality visits, and that people search more than ever. Publishers answer that more searches do not help them if fewer of those searches end on their pages. The deeper problem is that the bargain has no exit. Google reads pages for its AI answers with the same crawler it uses for ordinary search. So a site that blocks the AI risks vanishing from search altogether. Most sites have no real choice but to take whatever traffic is left.

If the open web runs on traffic and the traffic stops, the summaries eventually have less to summarize. That is Google's problem too, even if it does not feel like one yet.

## Now a study that made two chatbots take sides in American elections.

Researchers asked xAI's Grok and OpenAI's GPT to pick a candidate in nearly 500 election races, and made each model choose. Forced to pick, Grok overwhelmingly recommended Republicans. GPT favored Democrats. The two disagreed in more than 90 percent of the races. Three points on this. First, forcing a choice is an artificial test. Most people do not ask a chatbot who to vote for, and the companies train their models to decline. But the test shows which way a model leans when its guardrails are pushed aside, and that lean leaks into softer answers, such as which candidate's record sounds more reasonable when you ask for a summary. Second, the results match what the owners have said they want. Elon Musk has complained for years that other chatbots are too liberal, and has said Grok exists to correct that. OpenAI says it aims for neutrality. This result says it is not there. Third, and most important: when two of the biggest models disagree 90 percent of the time, there is no neutral answer to measure either one against. Which chatbot you use is now also a choice about whose judgment you borrow.

Worth remembering the next time a chatbot gives you a confident summary of anything political.

## Next, a medical comparison with a surprising winner.

The finding: a convolutional neural network about ten years old beats today's frontier language models at diagnosing skin cancer from images. A convolutional neural network, or CNN, is the kind of image model that ruled computer vision before chatbots. It does one job. You feed it thousands of labeled photos of moles and lesions, some cancerous and some not, and it learns to tell them apart. Models like this made headlines around 2017 for matching dermatologists on photos of skin lesions. A frontier model like GPT or Gemini can also look at a photo, and it will tell you a lot about it. But it was trained on everything, not on a large set of carefully labeled skin images. On a narrow task with good training data, the specialist still wins. This matters because people already upload photos of a worrying mole to a chatbot. It is free and it is right there. The answer comes back fluent and confident, whether it is right or not. The best thing a chatbot can say about that photo is: see a dermatologist. There is a lesson here for anyone building AI products too. The biggest model is not automatically the best tool. A small, old, well trained model can be cheaper, faster and more accurate at the one thing you need.

From medicine to the classroom, where the question is not whether AI is accurate, but whether anyone is still doing the work.

## A math professor at UC Berkeley says that because students use AI, there is no point in grading homework anymore.

Think about what homework grades were for. In a math course, homework is where you learn. You get stuck, you work it out, and the grade is a nudge to keep you doing it. It also gave the professor a rough signal of who understood the material. Both of those break when any problem set can be pasted into a chatbot and solved in seconds, with tidy working shown. The grade then measures who is willing to use the tool, not who understands the math. Grading also costs hours, often from teaching assistants, that could go to helping students instead. So the honest move is to stop pretending the score means something. What carries the weight instead is the old answer: the exam, in a room, without a phone. That puts everything on a few high pressure hours, which is hard on students who test badly. Homework still works for the student who does it alone. The difference is that nobody checks. Some students will skip the practice and find out at the exam.

Every course built on take-home work faces the same question, from essays to code. Math got there first because chatbots got good at it quickly.

## To Apple, whose first foldable, the iPhone Duo, has a problem people are calling crackgate.

Owners on social media and on the iPhone Duo subreddit report the phone failing fast. Some say they had it for only a few hours before the hinge seemed to catch on something, made an audible crack, and broke the screen. The telling detail is that the cracks appear in the same place each time. A YouTuber called CuriousHagi managed to reproduce the crack during durability testing, then took the phone apart. The hinge turns out to be complex: a gear-like central hinge, plus two different types of linking hinges. He thinks the fault is in that assembly. One teardown is not proof. But cracks in the same spot across many phones point to a design or manufacturing fault, not to careless owners.

iFixit has opened one up as well, and its teardown adds two useful details.

## iFixit, the repair guide site, has done its traditional teardown.

First, the crease. Every folding phone has a line where the screen bends, and Apple's is unusually hard to see. Part of the reason is the matte nano-texture cover on the inner screen. It makes the display easier to read in sunlight, and it scatters reflections, which masks the crease. Under a microscope, iFixit also found oval-shaped subpixels, the tiny red, green and blue dots that make up the picture, set at various angles. iFixit believes that is a deliberate design choice that also helps hide the fold. Second, repair. iFixit gave the Duo 5 out of 10. That sounds poor, but it is the best score the site has given any foldable yet. Given crackgate, a lot of owners may soon learn how much that score matters.

Apple holds its event in Los Angeles on Wednesday. The hinge is the question it least wants to answer there.

## Developers now, and a fight in one of Reddit's biggest open source communities.

The moderators of r/opensource posted new rules on Saturday evening. The first line reads: if your project uses AI, is made by AI, or is intended for AI, it's not welcome on r/opensource. AI, as it exists today, is incongruent to this Open Source community. The Verge's headline says the rules also shut out non-English speakers, among other things, but the AI ban is what set off the fight. The comments exploded, and a rival community, r/trueopensource, has already appeared. One moderator, who goes by Wolvereness, has been in the comments saying there is nuance and room for selective enforcement, for example software that incidentally has accessibility features that use AI. The trouble is the wording. Made by AI covers a large share of open source today, since many maintainers use coding assistants for at least part of their work. Intended for AI covers tools for running models locally, which many open source fans see as the best answer to the big AI companies. A rule that broad gets enforced by mood, and selective enforcement is the moderator's own phrase. There is a real grievance underneath. Maintainers are buried in low effort, AI generated bug reports and code submissions. But banning every project that touches AI does not target that problem.

Expect more communities to try rules like this, and to find the line harder to draw than the rule makes it look.

## Next, engineering advice that keeps getting louder: stop splitting your monitoring into metrics, logs and traces, and use wide events instead.

Some quick definitions. Metrics are numbers tracked over time, like requests per second or error rate. Logs are lines of text a program writes as it runs. Traces follow one request as it passes through several services, with a timing for each step. Most teams run separate tools for each, and when something breaks, they jump between them trying to line things up. A wide event is one structured record per request, per service, with as many fields as you like: the user, the endpoint, how long it took, whether the cache hit, which feature flags were on, the app version, how many database queries ran. Dozens or hundreds of fields in one row. The payoff is that you can ask questions you did not plan for. Metrics are added up in advance, so if you never tracked errors by app version, that detail is gone. With wide events you keep the raw rows and slice them afterwards: show me slow requests, only for customers on the new version, only in one region. Charity Majors and the team at Honeycomb have pushed this idea for years. The cost is storage, and you need a database built for fast queries across many columns. But storage is cheap next to an engineer spending an afternoon matching timestamps across three tools.

If you run anything in production, it is worth an hour of your time.

## A short one that will annoy some web developers.

A blogger removed Next.js from their site and got a perfect 100 in every Lighthouse category. Next.js is a popular framework from the company Vercel for building websites with React. Lighthouse is Google's free tool that scores a page out of 100 on speed, accessibility, best practices and search friendliness. The point is not that Next.js is bad. It is built for complex web apps, with logins, dashboards and pages that change as you use them. A blog is text and pictures. It can be plain HTML files that load almost instantly. Wrapping it in a JavaScript app makes the reader's browser download and run code just to show words that could have been sent as words. Many developers reach for the big framework out of habit, because it is what they use at work. For a personal site, the simpler tool wins on everything Lighthouse measures.

Pick the tool for the job, not for your CV.

## To finish, an essay that pushes back on the mood around AI.

It is called Cognitive Mercantilism, and it argues that today's AI panic resembles fears from early capitalism. Mercantilism was the leading economic idea in Europe from roughly the sixteenth to the eighteenth century. It held that a country's wealth was a fixed pile of gold and silver, so trade was a contest. Buy from abroad, gold leaves, and you are poorer. Adam Smith and others answered that wealth is what people make, and trade lets both sides make more. Applied to AI, the mistake is treating thinking as a fixed stock: if a machine does some of it, people are left with less of it, less work, less purpose. History mostly sides with the essay. Cheaper printing, cheaper calculation and cheaper computing each led to more jobs that involve thinking, not fewer. The analogy has a limit, though. Gains from trade spread slowly and unevenly, and some towns never recovered. The gains from AI could pool in a few companies for years. That is exactly what the Apollo economist's numbers would look like early on.

So that is the picture today. AI is getting cheaper by the month, and the cost and the payoff are landing in different places.
