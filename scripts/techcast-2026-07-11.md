# Apple Sues, GPT-5.6 Proves a Theorem, and Everyone Ships an Agent
*July 11, 2026*

Apple just did something it almost never does: sue a Silicon Valley peer by name, accusing OpenAI of stealing hardware secrets through its own former employees. That's today's biggest story, but don't check out after it, because OpenAI also shipped GPT-5.6 this week, and one variant of it reportedly produced a proof for an honest-to-god open conjecture in graph theory. We've also got Elon Musk pledging loyalty to Anthropic, a fifteen-year-old security hole hiding in every Linux distribution on Earth, and a story about Boko Haram using frontier AI that's worth sitting with. Let's get into it.

## Start with the lawsuit, because it has everything: espionage allegations, a Jony Ive connection, and named individuals who used to work at Apple.

Apple has filed suit against OpenAI, alleging what its complaint calls a pattern of theft of Apple's trade secrets by OpenAI employees who used to work at Apple. The suit doesn't stop at OpenAI as a company. It also names IO Products, the hardware startup Jony Ive built and sold to OpenAI back in 2025, plus two specific people: Tang Tan, who's now OpenAI's chief hardware officer, and Chang Liu, who left Apple for OpenAI back in January. Reporting on the complaint says the alleged misconduct was directed by OpenAI's senior leadership, including a longtime former Apple employee. This is squarely about hardware, not chatbots. Apple builds its entire business on tightly controlled silicon and industrial design, and here comes a company assembling a hardware division out of Apple's own alumni, led by a former Apple executive, working on some kind of AI device. Apple almost never sues this aggressively or names individuals like this. That alone tells you how seriously Cupertino is taking the threat.

This isn't a trademark spat over an app icon. Apple is treating OpenAI's hardware ambitions as an existential threat to the product category it invented, and it's using its legal department to say so out loud.

## While Apple's lawyers were filing paperwork, OpenAI was busy shipping. GPT-5.6 landed this week, and one part of the launch is stranger than the usual benchmark chart.

OpenAI's new model family promises improvements across the board, including specifically in cybersecurity, alongside a deployment safety report published for the release. Standard stuff for a frontier launch. What's not standard is what came out of the top-tier variant, apparently called Sol Ultra: a produced proof of the Cycle Double Cover Conjecture, a real, decades-old open problem in graph theory, not a repackaged competition question. That's a different kind of claim than acing a math benchmark. Benchmarks test whether a model can solve problems humans already know the answers to. An open conjecture has no answer key. If this proof holds up, it means a model engaged with genuinely unsolved research mathematics and produced something new, which is a meaningfully bigger claim than what's been said about every previous model release.

Big if true, and that if is doing a lot of work. A proof like this needs actual mathematicians to comb through it line by line before anyone should treat it as settled. But the fact that this headline is even plausible in July 2026 tells you how far the ground has shifted under a field that used to move in decades, not launch cycles.

## From proving theorems to just doing your job — OpenAI and Meta both pushed hard this week on agents that stick around.

OpenAI launched ChatGPT Work, pitched as a fix for the biggest complaint about last year's Agent Mode in Atlas: that it fell apart after a few minutes on anything complicated. OpenAI now says the new tool can stay with a project for hours, turning a stated goal into finished work, and it's testing that claim by challenging users to hand it something they already know well, like a budget analysis or meeting prep, so they can judge the output against their own expertise. It also folds in Scheduled Tasks, essentially cron jobs for your AI, that can run on a timer or in response to an event and be checked on from your phone, while still pausing for your approval before anything consequential happens. Meanwhile Meta jumped into the same fight with Muse Spark 1.1, aimed squarely at enterprise coding: large agentic workloads, bug fixes, and the kind of big, tedious code migrations that companies are increasingly happy to hand off rather than staff.

The interesting fight here isn't whose model is smarter, it's whose approval workflow you actually trust enough to let it run unattended for hours. 'It stopped after five minutes' was last year's punchline. If these products can't clear that same bar honestly, expect it to become this year's punchline too.

## Zoom out from the products to the money underneath them, because the numbers keep getting less believable in both directions.

There's a sharp piece making the rounds arguing Nvidia is now a victim of the compute marketplace it created: having proven just how valuable raw compute is, it finds itself at the center of a market that every hyperscaler and every well-funded competitor now wants a piece of, while less glamorous suppliers get rich quietly on the sidelines building the boring stuff nobody's fighting over. That sits right next to a bigger question making the rounds: the AI ROI debate is back, with even bigger numbers attached, and correspondingly bigger consequences if the spending doesn't pan out. And underneath both of those, there's a quieter, more uncomfortable story: it's genuinely unclear how the government decided OpenAI's latest frontier model was safe enough to release. Reporting says the actual dialogue between regulators and companies like Anthropic and OpenAI on that call is opaque, essentially invisible to outside scrutiny.

So the industry is simultaneously making trillion-dollar bets on whether this pays off, and gating the safety of the models those bets depend on through a process nobody outside a small room can actually see. You don't have to be a doomer to find that combination unsettling.

## Speaking of trust, here's a stranger story: Elon Musk is publicly pledging loyalty to a company he's clashed with before.

Musk praised Mythos, also known as Fable, and went out of his way to promise he won't cut off Anthropic. The unstated context is that Anthropic's compute arrangements put roughly forty billion dollars of its revenue within reach of whatever infrastructure Musk controls, which is exactly the kind of leverage Musk has shown before he's willing to use, whether in the OpenAI fight or in his other companies. So the actual question isn't whether Musk said something reassuring this week. It's whether a company with that much money on the line should be resting any part of its business on a promise from someone whose entire public track record is playing infrastructure control as a bargaining chip.

Nice words are cheap. The real test is what happens the next time Musk wants something from Anthropic and has this exact lever sitting right there.

## Quick funding roundup, and both stories share a number: a hundred million dollars, at a stage where that used to sound absurd.

Paris-based AI voice startup Gradium raised a hundred million dollar seed round backed by Nvidia, and it's using the cash to open a Bay Area office specifically to compete for talent in the world's densest AI labor market. That's Nvidia's now-familiar playbook: fund the companies building on top of your chips, then help them plant a flag where the engineers are. Separately, enterprise agent startup Lyzr had its own AI agent run its hundred million dollar fundraising process, presumably handling outreach and coordination, as a live demonstration that the product actually works.

A hundred million as a seed round, twice, in one week. Somebody's agent running its own fundraise is a great demo. It's also exactly the kind of stunt you'd want to run if you needed one, so take the 'proof it works' framing with a grain of salt.

## Switching to builders for a minute. Three stories here, and together they're a snapshot of infrastructure trying to catch up with where the industry actually is.

Mitchell Hashimoto, who built Terraform and Vagrant at HashiCorp, gave a wide-ranging interview about Ghostty, his terminal emulator, and Zig, the systems language it's written in. Ghostty has become one of the strongest real-world arguments that Zig is production-ready outside toy projects, since it's a widely used tool, not a demo. Meanwhile, a genuinely nasty piece of news: GhostLock, a stack use-after-free vulnerability, has apparently existed in every Linux distribution for fifteen years before anyone caught it. Fifteen years across essentially the entire Linux ecosystem is a long time for something like this to hide, and anyone running Linux infrastructure should be checking patch status today, not next week. And on the lighter end, a Show HN caught attention for a browser-based agent that watches how a web app calls its own internal APIs while a human uses it, then automatically turns those calls into agent tools, effectively a self-updating MCP server that never touches the underlying source code. They demoed it against Jira, Spotify, and even Hacker News itself.

One language maturing through real use, one ancient bug finally surfacing, and one clever hack for the fact that most software still doesn't expose clean APIs for agents. Different stories, same underlying theme: the tooling is scrambling to catch up with what we're actually trying to build.

## Back to Apple for a lighter beat, and it pairs nicely with that lawsuit from the top of the show.

An Apple Silicon executive laid out why Mac Mini demand has been climbing and what it says about Apple's on-device AI strategy. The short version: unified memory makes the Mac Mini a surprisingly good deal for running large models locally, and Apple's whole AI pitch is built around doing inference on your own hardware rather than shipping your data to someone else's cloud.

Put this next to the OpenAI lawsuit and the contrast is almost too neat. Apple's bet is private, on-device, your-silicon-your-data. OpenAI, with Jony Ive's old team in tow, is building some kind of standalone AI hardware of its own. No wonder Apple's lawyers are nervous — this isn't a philosophical disagreement anymore, it's two device strategies about to collide in the same market.

## Last story, and it's a serious one to close on rather than a fun one.

There's a report out on how the terrorist group Boko Haram has been using frontier AI. I won't dress this up or find a clever angle on it. The point of including it isn't shock value, it's a plain reminder: every capability we've talked about today, models that write production code, agents that run unattended for hours, systems that engage with open research problems, doesn't stay confined to the labs and the startups celebrating it. Degraded, repurposed, or just used exactly as intended, that same capability curve is available to people doing real harm with it, on the same timeline as everyone else.

Safety teams are triaging misuse cases in the same season the industry is bragging about proof-of-theorem headlines. Worth remembering both are true at once.

So: Apple's lawyers are drawing a hard line around its hardware future, OpenAI shipped a model that might have proven real math and definitely wants to run your workday unattended, and the money and the misuse are both scaling right alongside the capability. That's the shape of this industry right now — try to hold all three of those at the same time. That's TechCast Daily for July 11th. Back tomorrow.
