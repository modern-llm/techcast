# The Hard Problem and the Big Number
*August 12, 2026*

An unreleased Anthropic model went after the Riemann hypothesis, a problem that has outlasted every mathematician who ever tried it, and while it did not solve it, it got further than it had any right to. On the same day, both Gemini and ChatGPT confirmed they have crossed a billion users, a number that sounds like a verdict and mostly is not. Today is Wednesday, August twelfth, twenty twenty-six. This is TechCast Daily, and today is a good day to talk about which numbers you can actually check.

## Start with the math, because it is the most interesting thing that happened this week and the least understood. The Riemann hypothesis was written down in eighteen fifty-nine. It is one of the seven Millennium Prize problems, meaning there is a million dollars sitting there for whoever settles it, and that money has gone unclaimed for twenty-six years. Here is the plain version. Prime numbers look scattered, but they thin out at a predictable rate. Riemann wrote a formula for that rate, and the hypothesis claims the error in that formula is always as small as it could possibly be. Prove it, and hundreds of results that currently begin with the words assuming the Riemann hypothesis suddenly become true without the assumption.

Anthropic says one of its unreleased models made real progress on it. Not a solution. Progress. And that word is carrying an enormous amount of weight, so let me be careful about what is and is not being claimed. Partial results around Riemann are their own small industry. Mathematicians have spent a century tightening bounds, proving that a certain fraction of the relevant values sit where they should, checking the first several trillion cases by computer. Improving one of those bounds is genuine work. It is also not the hypothesis. What makes this worth your attention is not the headline. It is the domain. Mathematics is one of the very few places where you can check a machine's output without trusting the machine. A proof either holds or it does not, and a proof checker can verify it line by line in a way no benchmark score can. Every argument we have about whether these models are really reasoning or merely pattern matching just evaporates here, because the question is decidable. If the argument holds, it holds, and it does not matter what was happening inside the thing that produced it. That is why the frontier labs keep pointing their newest systems at open math problems. It is the one arena where a win cannot be explained away afterwards.

My caution is simple. An unreleased model, described by the company that built it, is a press release until the argument is in front of referees. What I would actually watch is the acknowledgments section of math papers. Models are already showing up there. When they start moving up into the author line, and a working mathematician defends that choice in public, the argument is over. I think that is a couple of years away, not decades.

## Now the big number. Sundar Pichai announced that Gemini has passed a billion monthly active users, making it the fastest-growing product Google has ever shipped and the fourteenth Google product to reach a billion people. ChatGPT got there a few weeks earlier, though OpenAI buried the milestone in the middle of an otherwise unremarkable blog post on August sixth. Outside estimates suggest ChatGPT actually crossed the line back in June.

Before anyone declares a winner, look at what a monthly active user means here. Google is counting people who opened the Gemini app or the Gemini website at least once in a month. Once. It does not include the Gemini that organizes your email, or the one writing AI summaries at the top of your search results, which is the version most people actually meet. So this is a real number, honestly reported, using the loosest available definition. The more useful observation is that these two billions are made of different material. Google's billion is a distribution billion. Google can put a product in front of a billion people because it owns Android, the search box, and the default slot on the home screen, and it has now done exactly that fourteen times. OpenAI's billion is a demand billion. Nobody preinstalled ChatGPT. People typed the name in. Both are enormous assets. They are not the same asset. The statistic that genuinely surprised me is further down the page. Sixty-three percent of Gemini users are talking to it out loud rather than typing, and Gemini is generating more than a hundred and fifty million images every day. If most of your users are speaking, your product problem changes completely. You need very low latency, you need to handle being interrupted mid-sentence, and you have to be wrong out loud, in a confident voice, with no page of results to hide behind. You also cannot put ten blue links into a spoken answer, which means the economics of search do not port over at all.

So the headline everyone will run is that the race is tied. It is not one race. Google is proving AI can be a feature of everything you already use. OpenAI is proving it can be a place you go on purpose. The second job is much harder, and if you win it, it is a far better business. And notice what neither company published: how many of that billion came back the next day.

## Speaking of businesses built on other people's spending. There is a piece circulating called Nvidia's Risky Business, and it is worth engaging with seriously, because the bear case on Nvidia is almost never about the chips.

The chips are fine. The chips are great. The risk is the shape of the customer list. A very large share of Nvidia's revenue comes from a very small number of buyers, and those buyers are not normal customers. They are five or six companies making capital spending decisions on multi-year horizons, and any two of them changing their minds in the same quarter is a bad quarter. There is a second wrinkle. Nvidia invests in AI companies, and those companies spend the money on Nvidia hardware. That is not fraud, and it is not unusual in a capital-intensive boom, but it does mean some slice of demand is being financed by the seller, and that slice is very hard to size from outside. Then the structural problem. Every one of those big buyers is also building its own silicon. Google has its tensor chips, Amazon has Trainium, Meta and Microsoft have their own designs. None of them displaces Nvidia for training the largest models any time soon. All of them are credible for inference, which is where the volume goes once you stop building models and start running them. Now read today's other Nvidia story in that light. Nvidia shipped a new open model in its Nemotron line, plus a serving and routing layer to go with it. Nvidia is giving away good models for free. Why would a hardware company do that? For the same reason Google gave away Android. If models are abundant, cheap, and open, then nobody in the layer above you gets to charge monopoly rents, everybody keeps needing more compute, and the value stays exactly where you happen to sit.

Nvidia is not trying to win the model race. It is trying to make sure nobody wins it decisively. That is smarter than competing, and it tells you precisely where the company thinks the danger is coming from.

## Two stories about running models on hardware you own. The project llama dot c p p is on the front page again, which happens roughly monthly and never stops being justified. And alongside it, a write-up on getting fast inference inside macOS virtual machines on Apple Silicon.

If you have not used llama dot c p p directly, you have almost certainly used it anyway. It is the C plus plus project that made running language models on your own machine a normal thing to do. It compiles to a single binary with no Python environment to break, it popularized the compressed file format that lets a large model squeeze into a laptop by storing its weights at lower precision, and it is the engine sitting underneath most of the friendly local model apps people install. It is arguably the most consequential piece of infrastructure of the last three years, and it is not a company. The virtual machine piece matters for a less obvious reason. Apple Silicon is genuinely good at this work because the processor and the graphics chip share one pool of memory, so a Mac with a lot of RAM can hold a model that would otherwise need several data center cards. Capacity is not the wall on a Mac. Memory bandwidth is. But until recently that advantage evaporated the moment you moved the work inside a virtual machine, because the guest could not properly reach the graphics hardware. Closing that gap turns a Mac from one developer's laptop into something you can rack, rent, snapshot, and run reproducible builds on.

The framing I would push back on is treating local models as a budget version of the frontier. They are not competing. There is a large class of work, code completion, transcription, searching your own documents, summarizing things you would rather not upload, where a mid-size model on your own machine is simply enough. For that work, running locally is not a compromise on privacy and cost. It is categorically better.

## Three separate language stories landed today, and read together they are really one story about what a programming language is even for now. Mojo reached one point zero. Rust shipped a new way to do floating point math faster. And someone made the case that Go is the ideal language for AI-assisted engineering.

Mojo first. It comes from Chris Lattner, who built the LLVM compiler toolkit and then Swift, so the track record is not in question. Mojo looks like Python and performs like a systems language, and its real target is the job you currently do in CUDA C plus plus: writing the low-level code that makes a model run fast on one specific accelerator. Reaching one point zero is a promise about stability, which is what serious teams wait for. But Mojo's hard problem was never speed. It was gravity. CUDA's moat is fifteen years of code that already works, and the only lever against that is portability. Write the kernel once, run it well on whatever silicon you can actually buy. Whether that holds is the entire bet. Rust's change is smaller and more elegant. Floating point addition is not associative. Add three numbers left to right, then right to left, and you can get different answers, because of rounding. That means a compiler is forbidden from reordering your arithmetic, which means it often cannot vectorize your loops, which leaves a large speedup on the floor. C and C plus plus solved this with a compiler flag that switches off the rules for your whole program, including code you did not write, and it is a famous source of bizarre bugs. Rust's version is per operation. You request the fast arithmetic on the specific line where you know it is safe, and everywhere else the strict rules still apply. Permission granted locally, in the source, where a reviewer can see it. Then the Go argument. Go has a small specification, one accepted way to format code, explicit error handling, a fast compiler, and a big standard library. The claim is that this makes it unusually good for a model to write, because there is less to get wrong and a tighter loop to check the result.

I think the Go argument is right, and it generalizes further than the author took it. For thirty years we designed languages to be pleasant to type in. Expressiveness, conciseness, clever abstractions, fewer keystrokes. When a machine writes the first draft, keystrokes cost nothing and ambiguity costs everything. Verbose, boring, and only one way to do it just became a performance feature. That inverts the value system of modern language design, and almost nobody has updated for it.

## Two pieces of security work, both about the same blind spot. Not jailbreaks. Plumbing. Somebody put GitHub Copilot behind a man-in-the-middle proxy, which is a tool that sits between your machine and the server and unscrambles the traffic so you can read what is actually being sent. And separately, researchers showed you can pull reasoning traces out of commercial model APIs.

The Copilot investigation is the kind of thing every developer assumes somebody already did and almost nobody has. Most people picture these tools sending the file you are looking at. What actually goes out is far broader: surrounding files, other open tabs, paths, repository metadata, and a steady stream of telemetry, all wrapped in a prompt template you never see. None of that is necessarily malicious. But if your company has a policy about what code may leave the building, that policy is currently based on reading documentation. Documentation is a claim. A proxy is a measurement. The second result is sharper. When a reasoning model works on a hard problem, it generates a long internal chain of thought before it answers. The major providers hide that chain from you, then bill you for the tokens it consumed. This research demonstrates the hidden reasoning leaks anyway, through the side channels around it: timing, token counts, the boundaries of the stream, the summaries you are shown. That matters three separate ways. One, you are paying for work you cannot inspect, so any way of measuring it is a consumer issue. Two, reasoning traces are the single most valuable training data in existence right now, because copying a big model's reasoning is exactly how you make a small model behave like an expensive one. If the traces leak, the moat leaks. Three, the general lesson: you cannot hide something while continuously streaming a derivative of it.

The pattern connecting these two is worth internalizing. The interesting attack surface on AI products is not the prompt. It is what gets sent, what gets billed, and what leaks sideways out of the connection. Old-fashioned network security, pointed at new-fashioned products.

## Now the story of the day on pure comedic merit. A company selling one hundred percent human-written, never AI medical research turns out to be one hundred percent AI.

Enjoy that for a second, then look at why it works as a business. Human-written has quietly become a premium product category, and there is no way to verify it. AI detection tools do not work reliably. They flag plain, well-edited prose, and they flag writing by people whose first language is not English at much higher rates. So the claim I wrote this myself is currently unfalsifiable in both directions, which makes it a perfect thing to lie about. In medical research the consequences stop being funny. Made-up citations to studies that do not exist propagate into clinical summaries, and someone downstream makes a decision on that. Now pair it with the other trust story today. OpenAI's head of ethics, Chloe Bakalar, has left after less than a year in the role. One departure is noise. The pattern across every frontier lab, where safety, policy, and ethics leads cycle through in twelve to eighteen months, is signal. And I do not think the explanation is villainy. These roles are structurally very hard. They are advisory positions inside organizations where shipping is existential and the competitive clock is set by a rival you do not control. If the role carries no veto, it is a research job with a stressful title. Note the tell in how we found out about it: a news report and an archive link, not a farewell post.

What links these two is that trust in this industry is currently a claim rather than a mechanism. And the fix for fake human writing is not better detectors, because that is an arms race the detector always loses. It is provenance. Drafts, version history, signed commits, the boring paper trail that shows the work happening. Stop certifying the vibe. Show the receipts.

## Two agent company stories. General Catalyst led a one point one billion dollar round into River AI, which is two months old. And Manus is going back to operating as an independent company.

River AI was founded by Igor Babuschkin, a co-founder of xAI, and the pitch is personal agents. Two months old means no product, no users, no revenue. So understand what that money is really buying. It is not a company budget. At current prices, a round that size is substantially a compute purchase order plus a recruiting war chest. That is the actual shape of AI-native startup finance now: the first round is a hardware order, and the thing being underwritten is one founder's ability to hire forty people who could otherwise have their pick of jobs. On the merits, personal agents are the most attempted and least delivered product category of the last three years. Apple tried. Amazon tried. Several very well-funded startups tried and are gone. It is hard for unglamorous reasons. An agent needs real access to your accounts and your money, your tolerance for an irreversible mistake is zero, and most of the value hides in a long tail of integrations nobody wants to build or maintain. Meanwhile Manus, which had one of the loudest agent debuts anyone has managed, is unwinding its arrangement with a larger owner and going independent again. Independence for a company like that means controlling which models you run on, who sees your users' data, and where you are incorporated. It also means paying full retail for inference with no parent absorbing the bill.

Both are bets on the same unproven thesis from opposite directions. One is a billion dollars saying the problem is solvable by a strong enough team starting fresh. The other is a team that already tried, buying back the freedom to try again on its own terms. I would take the second bet, honestly. Scar tissue is worth a lot in a category this full of graves.

## Two visual stories, and I want them side by side because they ask the same question from opposite ends. There is a Show HN of an iPhone app that captures from two lenses at the same time and fuses them into a single photo. And there is a project called WorldClaw doing agent-driven three D open world generation at scale.

The iPhone app is a small idea with a good reason behind it. iOS has let apps run two cameras simultaneously for years, and almost nobody uses that to produce one better image. If you shoot the wide and the telephoto at the same instant, you get two things neither lens gives you alone. You get parallax, because the lenses sit a couple of centimetres apart, which means depth information for free. And you get complementary detail, the telephoto's sharpness in the middle and the wide lens's field of view at the edges. Apple has fused multiple exposures inside its own camera app for years. Fusing across lenses, in a third-party app, is the interesting part. It is a useful reminder that a phone camera is a computation problem that happens to have a lens attached. WorldClaw is the other end of the question, generating entire navigable three D worlds with agents doing the building. The details available are thin, so let me tell you what I would check rather than what I think. Generated three D has one consistent failure mode. It looks spectacular in a fifteen second flythrough and falls apart the moment you can walk wherever you like and then walk back. So the questions are whether the world persists, whether the geometry is genuinely coherent rather than merely plausible from the demo camera angle, and whether there is anything to do once you are standing in it. The phrase at scale is doing the heaviest lifting in that title, and scale is the easiest part to fake.

One project takes two true views and fuses them into a truer one. The other invents the view outright. Both are arguments about what a pixel is supposed to be for, and that argument is nowhere near settled.

## Last one, and it is the idea I would keep if you forget everything else in this episode. Compression is prediction. That is not a metaphor. It is a theorem.

Here is the argument in plain terms. Say you want to store a piece of text in as few bits as possible. The best possible encoding gives short codes to things you expected and long codes to things that surprised you. The number of bits a symbol costs is set precisely by the probability you assigned to it before you saw it. So a perfect compressor requires a perfect predictor, and a perfect predictor hands you a perfect compressor. They are the same object seen from two sides. Now notice what follows. The loss function used to train a language model, cross entropy, is literally the number of bits that model would need to encode its training text. Training a language model is training a compressor. It always was. There is a competition, the Hutter Prize, that has treated compressing a fixed dump of Wikipedia as the definition of progress toward intelligence for twenty years, and this is exactly why. And it is not only theory. Researchers at DeepMind fed a large language model the raw bytes of images and audio, and it compressed them better than PNG compresses images and better than FLAC compresses audio, despite having been trained on text. Next-byte prediction does not care what the bytes mean. If there is structure in there, a good predictor finds it. That reframes the memorization argument too. A model that merely memorized would compress its training data well and nothing else. A model that compresses text it has never seen, meaning text that was not in its training data, has found real structure. The ratio is a measurement of how much.

The practical takeaway, if you build with these things: to find out whether a model actually knows your domain, measure how many bits it needs to predict your own documents, ones it could not possibly have trained on. That number is much harder to game than a benchmark score, it does not care about any leaderboard, and you can compute it yourself this afternoon.

There is a thread running through all of that, and it is verification. The Riemann story is exciting because proofs check themselves and no press release can change that. The billion-user number is thin because a monthly active user checks nothing. The fake human-written research firm worked as a business precisely because provenance has no mechanism behind it. And compression is prediction is worth keeping because it hands you a number you can compute yourself, without asking permission or believing anyone's chart. That is the filter I would run everything through this year. Not is it impressive, but can I check it, and would anyone know if it were false. That is the show. I am back tomorrow.
