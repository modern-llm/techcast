# TechCast Daily — August 2, 2026: AMD's Second-Source Moment, China's Lithography Clock, and Who Pays When the Answer Appears Without a Click
*August 02, 2026*

Today, AMD is having a moment, and it has nothing to do with a spec sheet — two separate signals suggest the software problem that has held it back for a decade is finally getting worked on in public. China says it has begun producing its own advanced deep-ultraviolet lithography machines, which, if it holds up, is the export-control story of the decade. And Reddit's CEO got on an earnings call and essentially accused Google of eating him alive with a product built on his own content. We've also got a soundness bug postmortem worth reading even if you'll never touch kernel code, a documentation framework you should steal, and someone put Linux on a two-dollar microcontroller. Let's get into it.

## Start with AMD, because two stories landed today that are the same story told from opposite ends. AMD's problem has never really been silicon. For years the chips have been competitive on paper and uncompetitive in practice, because CUDA is fifteen years of accumulated libraries, kernels, tutorials, Stack Overflow answers, and muscle memory. You don't beat that with FLOPs. You beat it, if at all, with an enormous amount of unglamorous documentation work.

The first story is a customer result: a writeup on running Moonshot's latest Kimi model on AMD MI355X hardware, claiming better performance per dollar than NVIDIA's B300. Read that claim precisely, because the wording carries all the weight. Not performance per chip. Performance per dollar. The MI355X and the B300 are both in roughly the same 288 gigabyte high-bandwidth memory class, which means AMD's old pitch — more memory per GPU, so your giant mixture-of-experts model fits in fewer boxes with less cross-node chatter — is largely neutralized at the top of the stack. What's left is price. And price is a market fact, not a hardware fact. AMD instances rent cheaper partly because demand for them is lower, which is a bit circular. But circular or not, if you are writing the check this quarter, the money is real. It's also worth noting why big sparse mixture-of-experts models are the right test case: at decode time you are reading enormous amounts of memory to produce one token, so you are bandwidth-bound, and bandwidth-per-dollar is exactly the axis AMD wants to compete on. The second story is the vendor doing the work that makes results like that possible. It's a detailed guide to optimizing attention decode kernels on the upcoming MI450, written in Gluon — the lower-level sibling of Triton, where you trade away automatic scheduling in exchange for explicit control over memory layouts and software pipelining. Attention decode is the memory-bound phase of inference: one token at a time, dragging the whole key-value cache through the memory system. It is precisely the kernel where hand-tuning pays, and precisely the kernel where NVIDIA has a decade of head start.

What I find notable is the sequencing. AMD is publishing a step-by-step optimization guide for hardware that isn't shipping yet. That's a company that has correctly diagnosed its own problem: the moat isn't transistors, it's that nobody knows how to write a fast kernel on your part. And AMD does not need to beat NVIDIA. It needs to be the obvious second source that any serious buyer qualifies. That's a much lower bar and a very large business.

## Staying on compute supply, but moving one layer down the stack. Reports today that China has begun producing advanced deep-ultraviolet lithography machines domestically. To understand why this is the story it is, you need the shape of the chokepoint.

One Dutch company, ASML, makes the machines that print modern chips. Extreme-ultraviolet tools — the ones you need for the leading edge without heroics — have never been exported to China at all. Deep-ultraviolet immersion tools, the 193-nanometer workhorses, were sellable until export controls tightened in 2023, and now new sales and servicing are restricted. Here's the thing people underrate: deep-ultraviolet is not a consolation prize. With multipatterning — printing a layer in several passes and stitching it together — you can reach 7 nanometers and, painfully and expensively, arguably 5. That is how SMIC produced the chip that caused so much noise a few years back. So the practical effect of the controls was never to make advanced chips impossible in China. It was to cap the ceiling at whatever tools were already inside the country, and let attrition do the rest. Domestic production changes that from a fixed stockpile to a supply line. Now the caveats, and they are not small. The phrase 'begins producing' is carrying a lot of weight. A lithography scanner is plausibly the most complex machine humanity builds. The distance between a tool that images a clean test pattern in a lab and a tool a fab will run in production is measured in years — you need overlay accuracy in single nanometers, throughput north of a hundred and fifty wafers an hour to make the economics work, uptime that doesn't wreck a fab schedule, and matched performance across a fleet of machines so wafers can move between them. And lithography isn't the only chokepoint. Metrology, etch, deposition, photoresist chemistry, and design software all have their own bottlenecks.

The right read here is neither 'China has caught up' nor 'this is propaganda.' It's that export controls were always a delay, not a wall — everyone serious said so at the time — and today we got a rough reading on the clock. The policy question was never whether the gap closes. It was whether the delay bought enough time to matter, and I have never heard anyone in a position of authority say what 'enough' was supposed to buy.

## Now to a fight about money that's really a fight about a broken bargain. Reddit reported earnings, the stock fell, and CEO Steve Huffman used the moment to question the value of Google's AI Overviews — the feature that answers your search query directly, above the links.

Reddit's position is genuinely unusual, which is what makes this instructive. Reddit is the single best-positioned content company in this fight. It has a licensing deal with Google, signed in 2024, reportedly worth tens of millions a year. It has a corpus nobody else can replicate — millions of humans arguing about which dishwasher to buy. It is one of the most-cited sources in AI-generated answers. It even benefited enormously from Google's ranking changes that pushed forum content up, plus the widespread habit of typing your question and appending the word 'reddit.' So this is the strongest possible hand. And here's the structure of the complaint underneath the corporate language: Google pays Reddit once, on a recurring but negotiated schedule, for access to the corpus. Google then builds a product that answers the user's question inline, and the user never clicks through. Reddit sold access to the raw material and Google captured the recurring attention. The licensing revenue is a fixed number. The traffic loss is an ongoing bleed. There is no term in that contract that prices the second thing, because when it was signed nobody knew how to.

In fairness to Google, AI Overviews are frequently useful, and the honest version of this is that users prefer the answer to the link. That preference isn't going away because a publisher is unhappy. But here's the part every media executive should internalize: if the company with the licensing deal, the unique corpus, and the community moat is still getting squeezed, the problem is structural, not a bad negotiation. Everyone with a weaker hand is in worse shape and hasn't noticed yet. Read that earnings transcript before you sign your own data deal.

## Related theme, much smaller scale, and it's about someone else's meter. Cursor, the AI coding editor, removed cost information from its usage page and from the CSV export.

Context matters here, because Cursor has a history on pricing. It moved from a request-based model to token-based billing, and there was a stretch where plans marketed as unlimited turned out to have throttles that users discovered the hard way. Against that history, quietly removing dollar figures reads badly regardless of intent. Think about what a usage page is actually for. If you're a solo developer, it answers 'what did that four-hour refactor cost me?' If you run a team, it's how you do chargeback to projects. If you're evaluating vendors, the CSV export is how you build a real cost model — cost per merged pull request, cost per model, whether the expensive model is actually cheaper once you count retries. Take the dollars out and none of those questions are answerable. You're left with an abstract credit balance that only the vendor can convert to money. There's a charitable read: if you've moved to a bundled credit system, per-request dollar amounts can genuinely mislead, because the marginal cost of one request isn't what you paid. And there's the uncharitable read, which is that opaque pricing is much easier to change than transparent pricing.

My rule is simple. If you're metering me, show me the meter. AWS billing is a legendary user-experience disaster, but the numbers are all there, and that's why an entire cost-optimization industry exists on top of it. Removing the numbers is a decision you make when you'd prefer customers not do arithmetic. And practically speaking, every engineer who now spends an afternoon standing up a proxy to count their own tokens is a customer you just taught how to be portable.

## Onto a training technique that keeps getting rediscovered under new names, and is worth understanding because it's the cheapest real lever in the toolkit. The framing today is explorative modeling: sample K guesses, keep the best one, train on that.

The observation this rests on is one of the more robust findings in the field. Models are dramatically better at pass-at-K than pass-at-one. Give a model twenty attempts at a hard problem and score whether any of them is right, and the number is far higher than if you take the first one. Which means the correct answer is already in the model's distribution — it just isn't ranked first. So best-of-K training isn't teaching the model anything new. It's re-ranking. You're taking capability that was latent and probabilistic and making it the default behavior. The appeal versus full reinforcement learning is practical: no reward model drifting out from under you, no policy collapse, no delicate divergence-penalty tuning. It's supervised fine-tuning on data you generated, and it's stable. Now the limits, which matter more than the technique. It is entirely hostage to your scorer. In verifiable domains — math with a checker, code with a test suite — it works beautifully, because 'best' is a fact. In fuzzy domains where a model judges the outputs, you don't amplify quality, you amplify whatever your judge is biased toward, and you do it with conviction. Second, it collapses diversity. Every round pushes probability mass onto the mode, which makes the next round of sampling less exploratory, which is why the returns diminish sharply after a couple of iterations.

The clean way to hold this in your head: best-of-K makes a model reliable, not smarter. You can only harvest capability the base model already had. That's a real and valuable thing — most of what users experience as a model being bad is actually a model being inconsistent — but it's a different thing from raising the ceiling, and the two get conflated constantly in launch posts.

## Video generation next. ByteDance released Seedance 2.5, the latest in its video model line, which has been at or near the top of the public comparisons since the first version landed.

Two things make this worth your attention beyond the demo reel. First, Chinese labs are genuinely at the front of video generation, and video is the modality where the compute economics are most punishing — you're paying for a spatial and temporal dimension simultaneously, and the inference cost per second of usable output is brutal. Being good at it is partly a research result and substantially an infrastructure result. Second, and more important: ByteDance is the only lab in this race whose distribution problem is already solved. For everyone else, a better video model means building a product, finding users, and teaching them a workflow. For ByteDance, it means adding a button to CapCut and TikTok, where hundreds of millions of people already make video every single day and already understand the editing metaphors. Model quality across the leading video systems is converging. Distribution is not converging at all. Now, the honest caveat about evaluating any of these: the demo reel is always the ceiling, never the median. Cherry-picking is not dishonest in this field, it's the norm, and everyone does it. What determines whether a video model is actually usable in production work is not peak quality — it's controllability and iteration. Can you make a small change to the prompt and get back something recognizably the same shot? Or does every generation reroll the entire universe?

So here's a concrete test if you're evaluating this yourself. Generate a clip, then change one word — the color of a jacket, the time of day — and regenerate. Measure how much of the frame survives. That single number predicts whether it fits into a real workflow better than any leaderboard, and almost nobody reports it.

## Two stories that look unrelated and are actually about the same question: what happens when a general-purpose model meets people who never signed up to be part of the experiment.

First, Sam Altman is again making the case for ChatGPT as a parenting assistant, describing it as a cool use case. And the underlying use is genuinely mundane and genuinely useful — is this rash something, how do I get a four-year-old to stay in bed, what do I pack for a flight with a toddler. Parents have been searching the web for exactly this for twenty years, and the search results were terrible. But the error profile is different in a way that deserves stating plainly. A confidently wrong answer about a database migration costs you an afternoon. A confidently wrong answer about a child's fever is a different category of mistake, and the interface offers no signal about which kind of answer you just got. It also arrives while OpenAI is still under real scrutiny about how its products behave with minors and with people in distress. Leading with family use while that's unresolved is a sequencing choice, and it's fair to notice it. Second story: a judge denied xAI's request to block Minnesota's ban on so-called nudify apps — tools that generate sexualized images of real people from ordinary photos. The ban stands while the case proceeds. The denial itself is the signal worth reading, because preliminary injunctions turn substantially on whether the party is likely to win on the merits, and the court didn't think xAI was. The legal question underneath is genuinely unsettled: is a model's output the developer's own speech, protected accordingly, and does the intermediary-liability shield that protects platforms from user content extend to content the platform's model generated? Federal law now addresses distributing non-consensual intimate imagery. States going after the tools that manufacture it is the newer front, and that's where the constitutional fight will actually happen.

I want to be careful not to flatten these into equivalence, because they aren't equivalent — helping a parent is not the same as shipping an image-abuse tool. But they sit on the same axis. AI companies consistently ask to be judged by their best use case and regulated by their average one. The parenting pitch is the best case. The nudify tools are what the market does with general capability shipped without friction. Both are downstream of the same decision about how much to constrain a general system.

## Switching to craft. Brandur has a piece on pgtestdb and its template-cloning approach to database testing, and this is the kind of thing that sounds like a small tooling detail and is actually a change in how you write code.

Every team with a Postgres test suite eventually picks one of two bad options. Option one: wrap each test in a transaction and roll it back at the end. This is fast and it's what most people do. It also means you cannot honestly test anything that manages its own transactions, which is most of your interesting business logic, and the savepoint emulation you bolt on to work around that has its own subtly different semantics. You end up testing a lie. Option two: rebuild the schema for each test. This is correct — a real, clean database every time — but you pay the full migration cost per test, and that cost only ever grows, because nobody deletes migrations. Two years in, your test suite spends most of its life replaying database history. The template approach sidesteps both. You run your migrations exactly once into a template database. Then for each test you issue create database with template, and Postgres does an essentially file-level copy of the template's directory. The cost is proportional to the size of your schema, not to the number of migrations, and in practice it's tens of milliseconds. Every test gets a real, isolated, writable database. You can test transactions, DDL, triggers, deferred constraints — all the things the rollback trick makes untestable. The mechanism isn't new; template databases have been in Postgres essentially forever. The contribution is packaging it so nobody has to think about it, including handling the awkward requirement that no other connection can be attached to the template while you clone from it.

The reason to care is compounding. A test suite that runs in ten seconds gets run constantly, mid-thought, as a form of exploration. One that takes six minutes gets run at the end, once, defensively. That gap doesn't just change your CI bill — it changes whether people write database tests at all. Most of the best developer tooling turns out to be someone noticing the database already had the feature.

## Next, a postmortem for a kernel soundness bug. Worth reading even if you will never touch kernel code, because the failure mode generalizes almost perfectly.

Quick definition, because 'soundness bug' is precise jargon and people use it loosely. A soundness bug means that safe code — code containing no unsafe blocks, code the compiler promised to vouch for — could be made to trigger undefined behavior. It's not that someone wrote a bug. It's that the guarantee itself leaked. In a kernel, where the entire argument for adopting a memory-safe language is that guarantee, this is the bug class that matters. Here's what's consistently true about these, and why the postmortem is more valuable than the patch. The language itself is almost never what breaks. What breaks is the wrapper. Kernel-level safe abstractions are a thin layer of safe-looking APIs sitting on top of an enormous body of existing C whose invariants live in maintainers' heads and, at best, in comments. Every unsafe block in that layer is a proof obligation written in prose. A soundness bug means someone's proof had a hole — and it's nearly always one of three shapes: a lifetime assumption that doesn't hold under some caller, an aliasing assumption that a second code path violates, or a 'this is only ever called from context X' that turned out to have an exception. The valuable questions in the writeup aren't what the fix was. They're: how long did it survive review, what shape of API invited it, and did the safety comment even claim the thing that turned out to be false — because very often the comment asserts something adjacent to the real requirement, and that gap is where the bug lives.

And to be clear about the direction this points: this is an argument for memory-safe languages in the kernel, not against. The bug is findable, nameable, has a bounded blast radius you can grep for, and is worth a postmortem. The equivalent in C isn't a soundness bug — it's just a bug, anywhere, forever, with no boundary to audit. Broader advice: read postmortems from projects you have nothing to do with. The specific bug will never apply to you. The failure mode almost always will.

## Two items about how technical knowledge actually gets transferred, which is a problem most engineering teams solve badly and expensively.

First, Diátaxis, a documentation framework that keeps resurfacing because it's correct. It splits documentation along two axes — whether the reader is learning or doing, and whether they need practical steps or theoretical understanding — which yields four modes. Tutorials, for learning by doing. How-to guides, for accomplishing a specific goal. Reference, for looking things up. And explanation, for understanding why the system is shaped this way. The taxonomy is not the insight. The insight is the diagnosis: almost all bad documentation is bad because a single page is trying to do two of these jobs at once. A tutorial that stops to explain the architecture loses the beginner, who wanted to reach a working state. A reference page that tries to teach you loses the expert, who wanted one flag. Neither reader is served, and the author feels productive the whole time. There's also a newer reason this matters more than it did five years ago. Your documentation is now consumed by retrieval systems and coding agents at least as much as by humans. A chunk of text with one job retrieves cleanly and answers precisely. A page that's half-tutorial and half-reference retrieves ambiguously and produces confidently wrong answers with your project's name on them. Second item, and it's the same idea in practice: the Go 1.27 interactive tour. This is the format where every new feature comes as a runnable example you can edit in the browser and immediately see change. That's a tutorial in the strict Diátaxis sense, and it's exactly the mode that official release notes are worst at — release notes are reference material pretending to be education. I'm not going to read a changelog aloud to you; go click through it, that's the entire point of the format. What I'd say about Go generally is that the cadence is boring by design, two releases a year, and with a strong compatibility promise the interesting question is never what got added. It's what got declined.

Concrete suggestion if you own docs anywhere: spend one afternoon labeling every page with exactly one of the four modes. The exercise takes less time than you think, and the pages you can't label are, with uncanny reliability, the exact pages your users complain about.

## Closing with two items for people who like knowing what's actually happening underneath.

First, The Art of 64-bit Assembly, Randall Hyde's x86-64 book, which resurfaced today. The honest case for learning assembly in 2026 is not that you're going to write it. Almost nobody writes it. The case is that you're going to read it, constantly, whether or not you're prepared to. Every time you open a profiler and stare at a hot loop, every time you check what the compiler did with your allegedly zero-cost abstraction, every time you're handed a crash dump with no symbols, or you're trying to work out why the vectorizer bailed on a loop that looks obviously vectorizable — you are reading assembly. And it loops directly back to the AMD story we opened with. Writing a fast attention kernel is fundamentally about register pressure, memory hierarchy, and instruction scheduling. Those are assembly-level concerns wearing a Python costume, and people who understand the costume but not the body underneath hit a hard ceiling. Second, Linux running on an ESP32 — a microcontroller that costs a couple of dollars, with external memory bolted on, booting a real kernel. Practically speaking, you shouldn't do this. The existing real-time operating systems for these chips are better at what these chips are for, and running Linux on a part without a proper memory management unit means giving up the memory protection that's half the reason you'd want Linux in the first place. But the demonstration is the point. A two-dollar chip now has more memory and more clock than the workstations Linux was originally written for. The line between 'microcontroller' and 'computer' stopped being about capability some time ago; it's now about which software stack you're willing to carry and pay for in power.

Both of these are unfashionable in exactly the same way, and they pay off in exactly the same way. Each one collapses the number of things you have to treat as magic. Over a career, that's not a hobby — that's the entire skill.

That's the show. Three threads worth carrying with you today. AMD is making a serious, patient run at being the qualified second source for AI compute, and it's doing it through documentation rather than benchmarks, which is the correct strategy. China's lithography clock is now partially visible, and the honest framing is that export controls bought time rather than built a wall — the open question was always how much time, and nobody in charge has defined what that time was for. And across Reddit, Cursor, and a courtroom in Minnesota, the same argument keeps surfacing in different clothes: when a model answers the question, who gets paid, who gets to see the bill, and who's responsible for what comes out. Nobody has settled any of those, and this year is when the answers start getting written down. Thanks for listening to TechCast Daily. See you tomorrow.
