# SpaceX Buys Cursor for $60 Billion, Anthropic Gets Export-Controlled, and OpenAI Is Bleeding Money
*June 18, 2026*

SpaceX just acquired an AI code editor for sixty billion dollars, two days after its own IPO. Meanwhile, Anthropic's newest models are still offline after an unprecedented government export-control order, and leaked OpenAI financials show the company spending nineteen billion on R&D against thirteen billion in revenue. The AI era is colliding with real governments, real budgets, and real physics. This is TechCast Daily for June 18th.

## Let's start with the deal that everyone is talking about. SpaceX is buying Cursor, the AI-native coding tool, for sixty billion dollars in stock. That number is worth saying slowly. Sixty. Billion. For a code editor.

Here's the context. The deal was announced today, just two days after SpaceX's blockbuster IPO, and a few months after SpaceX absorbed Elon Musk's AI lab xAI in a major restructuring. In its IPO filings, SpaceX told investors it sees a twenty-six trillion dollar addressable market in AI. Now it's making a statement about where it thinks the value lives. Cursor built its reputation as one of the first tools to treat AI as a genuine first-class collaborator in software development, not a sidebar chatbot, not an autocomplete widget, but something actually woven into how you write, edit, and reason about code. It's built on a fork of VS Code, but the philosophy was different from day one. The honest assessment, though, is that the early-mover advantage has compressed. Microsoft, GitHub, and the model labs themselves have all shipped features that roughly match what Cursor was uniquely doing eighteen months ago. Which makes the sixty billion price tag interesting. SpaceX isn't paying for a feature set. It's paying for a developer base that already lives inside Cursor every day, and for a team that has been thinking harder than almost anyone about what agentic coding infrastructure actually looks like. There's a genuine argument circulating right now that the chat-box paradigm, where you type a question and get a suggestion, is fundamentally insufficient for what agentic development needs. The real value is in building systems where an AI can plan, browse, edit, test, and iterate across a full codebase autonomously. That's a different engineering problem than autocomplete, and it's the problem SpaceX may actually need solved at scale for its own engineering org.

My read: this is SpaceX planting a flag. A newly public company with stock to spend, buying its way into the developer-tooling market that every serious AI company now wants to own. Whether sixty billion was the right number, we won't know for years. But the direction is clear: the coding environment is the new platform war.

## Now to a story that's escalated from a corporate outage to a genuine geopolitical flashpoint. The Anthropic blackout has reached the G7, and the arguments being made there are ones that are going to define AI policy for years.

Here's where things stand. Last Friday, the Trump administration ordered Anthropic to cut access to its newest models, Fable 5 and Mythos 5, for any foreign national. The scope was so broad that Anthropic's only practical path to compliance was to take the models offline entirely, for everyone, including users inside the United States and the company's own staff. Anthropic has been in talks with the White House since then and still hasn't secured an agreement to bring the models back. This is, by all accounts, the first time U.S. export controls have been used to restrict access to an AI model in this way. The administration cited national security authorities but has not publicly explained the legal basis. Now the why here is worth understanding, because it's not arbitrary. Mythos 5 has advanced capabilities for finding and potentially exploiting software vulnerabilities. Anthropic flagged this themselves. They'd released an early version privately to a vetted working group under a program called Project Glasswing, and the public version, Fable 5, shipped with specific limits on biology and cybersecurity questions. The company was not asleep at the wheel. But the administration apparently concluded that even filtered access was too risky. The security community's response is pointed: dangerous-capable AI is coming regardless of which vendor's API gets locked. You can black-box Anthropic's model and the underlying capability doesn't disappear. Meanwhile, the geopolitical signal is reverberating. At the G7 summit this week, French President Macron and Indian Prime Minister Modi both raised explicit alarms: if the world depends on American AI, America can switch it off overnight. That was previously a theoretical concern. This week it became a live demonstration. And the policy picture is muddied further by Washington simultaneously holding off on formally blacklisting DeepSeek, while flagging over a hundred other firms as security risks. The inconsistency is hard to read as strategy.

The sovereignty problem is now undeniable. American AI is the world's best AI, and every government that built on it just watched one company's flagship get switched off without warning or legal explanation. Expect this to be the single biggest accelerant for sovereign AI efforts in Europe, India, and the Middle East. Washington may have done more this week for its competitors than they've done for themselves in two years.

## Let's talk about the money, because a set of stories this week, when you line them up, tell one coherent narrative: the AI industry's financial assumptions are being stress-tested in real time.

Start with the biggest data point. Leaked OpenAI financial documents, obtained ahead of an expected IPO filing, show revenue growing from three point seven billion dollars in 2024 to thirteen billion in 2025. That's nearly a four-times increase in a single year, with monthly revenue approaching two billion by year's end. Impressive by any standard. Now the other side of the ledger. R&D expenses grew from seven point eight billion to nineteen point two billion in the same period. OpenAI spent nearly twenty billion dollars on research and development in a year when it brought in thirteen billion in revenue. The cost of staying at the frontier completely swamped the revenue growth. Inside that R&D spend, more than ten billion went to Microsoft for compute under their partnership. OpenAI is, in a meaningful sense, paying Microsoft more than it earns from most of its other customers combined just to keep training. Connect that to two other signals from this week. ChatGPT's global market share just slipped below fifty percent for the first time. It's still the dominant product, with one point one billion monthly users, against Gemini's six hundred sixty-two million and Claude's two hundred forty-five million. But the compression is real. And inside companies, the trend that had a name earlier this year, tokenmaxxing, pushing employees to use AI as aggressively as possible, has hit a wall. Uber reportedly burned through its annual AI budget in a few months. Companies have cut Claude licenses for whole parts of their organizations. Meta killed an internal usage leaderboard. And Anthropic, in a telling move, abruptly paused a pricing change that would have started billing Agent SDK usage at API rates instead of subscription limits, walking it back at the last minute after announcing it a month earlier. The unit economics of heavy AI use are still being negotiated between labs and customers, and the customers are pushing back.

The thesis for all these companies is still that revenue grows faster than costs over time. But 2025's numbers show that gap is enormous, and the easy usage growth from tokenmaxxing culture is ending. What comes next requires AI delivering ROI that's measurable, not just impressive-sounding. That's a higher bar.

## Let's get out of the spreadsheets and into the lab, because the most genuinely interesting technical story this week is about AI that teaches robots by running experiments overnight.

Researchers at NVIDIA's GEAR lab, working alongside teams from Carnegie Mellon and UC Berkeley, published work on a framework called ENPIRE, an agent harness that wraps around AI models and gives them what they need to actually operate autonomously: memory, context, feedback loops, and tool use. Then they pointed those agents at a physical robotics lab and said, essentially, train the robots. The result: robotic arms successfully learned to cut zip ties and to insert GPUs into the thin, tight sockets on a motherboard. The GPU insertion task is a useful benchmark because it requires precise force applied in exactly the right direction. Not trivial even for humans who've done it a hundred times. Jim Fan, NVIDIA's director of AI, described the operational picture directly: part of the lab now self-improves tirelessly overnight, and the team reads the reports in the morning. That's the meaningful shift. The traditional bottleneck in robotics is designing training curricula, slow, expensive, human-in-the-loop work. What ENPIRE demonstrates is that AI agents can design those curricula, run iterations, observe outcomes, and improve, all without a human in the loop. This doesn't stand alone. Alibaba has released the Qwen-Robot Suite, a family of foundation models aimed at physical-world intelligence, the same idea of general-purpose reasoning applied to machines. And the investment side reflects the conviction: a startup called Odyssey just secured a valuation of one point four five billion dollars, backed by Amazon, to build world models, AI systems that learn internal simulations of how physics works rather than predicting text.

The software-eating-the-world metaphor is getting a physical extension. Agentic AI that was built to write code is now running robotics experiments. The harness doesn't care if the tool is a file editor or a robotic arm. That generality is going to matter a lot.

## A few more stories deserve your attention even if they didn't dominate the headlines.

Pramaana Labs raised a twenty-seven million dollar seed round from Khosla Ventures to bring formal verification to AI. Formal verification is the mathematical practice of proving a system's outputs satisfy a specification. It's used in safety-critical hardware, aerospace, and compilers. Applying it to AI is genuinely hard because these models are probabilistic, not deterministic, but Pramaana is targeting the verticals where errors carry real consequences: law, drug discovery, and tax preparation. If you're an AI drafting a legal brief or calculating a drug dosage, a confident wrong answer isn't just an embarrassment, it's a liability. The pitch is that some markets will pay a real premium for proof rather than probability. Separately, a YC W25 company called Adam launched an open-source platform called CADAM for AI-generated mechanical CAD. You describe a part in plain text, or reference an image, and the system generates OpenSCAD code that renders a parametric three-dimensional model, complete with interactive sliders for adjusting dimensions. It exports to standard formats including STL and OBJ. The underlying bet is the same one that's played out in software: that design will increasingly be described in language and refined in parameters rather than drawn by hand. And on the pure engineering front, a piece worth flagging: someone made Python's ast.walk function, a core utility used by basically every Python linter and analysis tool, run two hundred twenty times faster through careful low-level optimization. No new product, no funding round, just shared infrastructure made dramatically better. That kind of work rarely gets celebrated but it compounds across every project that depends on it.

Three different scales of impact. A startup betting that reliability is the next moat in AI. An open-source tool bringing AI into physical design. And one engineer making everyone's Python tooling faster by two orders of magnitude. All worth knowing.

## Finally, Apple is raising prices and looking far into its own future at the same time.

Tim Cook told the Wall Street Journal this week that the RAM shortage has become, in his words, unsustainable, and that price increases are unavoidable. Apple has been trying to absorb rising memory costs rather than pass them on, but says it's reached the limit. No specific products or dates were named, but the evidence is already in the market: Apple dropped the Mac Studio's highest-memory configuration in March, and raised the Mac Mini's starting price from five ninety-nine to seven ninety-nine dollars. More is coming. The underlying pressure is the same story we've been following all year. The AI infrastructure buildout has created enormous demand for high-bandwidth memory. Apple's unified memory architecture means it competes for the same premium DRAM that data centers are buying to run AI workloads. The shortage isn't incidental, it's the AI boom showing up as a line item in Apple's cost of goods. On the product roadmap side, Bloomberg's Mark Gurman has new details on camera-equipped AirPods, currently on track for late 2027. The cameras would sit in the stems of the earbuds, with indicator lights showing when data is uploading. The idea is that a future, more capable Siri would gain visual context about your environment, letting your earbuds see what you're looking at and fold that into responses.

Apple's squeeze is a microcosm of the broader dynamic: the AI buildout is real and expensive enough to raise prices on consumer hardware that has nothing to do with AI. And Apple's answer to that pressure is to bet its product roadmap on becoming the ambient AI interface on your face. Both things can be true at once.

Export controls on an AI model, world leaders raising alarms, a legal basis the government still hasn't explained. We're in genuinely new territory, and the rules are being written as we go.
