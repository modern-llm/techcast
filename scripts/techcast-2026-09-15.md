# Pace the Frontier: AI's Leaders Ask for Brakes, Critics Call It a Cartel
*September 15, 2026*

The people building the most powerful AI on Earth spent the weekend agreeing that they should slow down. Dario Amodei, Sam Altman, Demis Hassabis and even Elon Musk are roughly on the same page, which almost never happens. Today we cover what they actually proposed, why critics are calling it a cartel, and why the man who sells them their chips told the President it isn't happening. Plus iOS 27 is out, Amazon and Perplexity are at the appeals court, and someone is controlling a Mac with their wrist muscles. This is TechCast Daily for Tuesday, September 15th, 2026.

## Let's start with the whiplash. For about four years, every frontier AI lab has acted as if it were in a winner-take-all race. The prize was artificial general intelligence, and second place didn't count. Over one weekend, the leaders of that race publicly asked for the brakes.

It started Saturday morning with an essay of nearly four thousand words from Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei, titled We Must Pace the Frontier. His central line is that we must slow the pace at which we improve the capabilities of AI models. His argument is that commercial pressure is driving a race to the bottom, and that race makes catastrophic risks worse. He laid out three concrete steps. First, embedded third-party evaluators. These are not auditors who visit once a year. They are outside people placed inside the labs, who can check whether a company is actually keeping its own safety commitments and can report incidents when something goes wrong. Second, coordination among frontier AI companies in democratic countries on shared standards. Third, and this reaches furthest, regulation of domestic labs and work toward a global slowdown agreement. The response came fast. Within hours, Sam Altman posted that he agreed, and said OpenAI had been having similar pacing discussions internally. Demis Hassabis, cofounder and chair of Google DeepMind, said the essay points towards the right path forward, and he repeated his recent call for an industry-wide standards body. Satya Nadella said Microsoft welcomes deliberate pacing to get alignment right. Alignment means making sure models actually do what their builders intend. Microsoft is also about to publish a long code of conduct for its models, which it calls humanist AI. And Elon Musk, who has sued OpenAI and spent years attacking these same people, loosely signed on too. It's worth being precise about what was agreed. Reporting calls it a loose agreement, and several leaders signed on only in part. There is no treaty. There is no signed document with numbers in it. Nobody has named a point at which anyone stops training a bigger model. So far, this is a set of public statements that point in the same direction.

That is a real change in tone, and tone matters. But a post you can walk back next week is not the same thing as an outside evaluator working inside your building. And whenever the biggest players in an industry agree on something, a second question comes up right away.

## The question is simple: is this a safety pact, or a cartel? A cartel is a group of competitors who coordinate to protect their position instead of competing with each other.

Critics made that case almost immediately, and it deserves a fair hearing because it isn't crazy. Start with who benefits. The companies proposing to slow the frontier are the ones that already have frontier models, the data centers and billions of dollars in funding. Anyone behind them gets frozen in place: a newer lab, a startup, an open-source project. Licensing domestic labs sounds like safety, but licenses cost money, and compliance costs hit small players hardest. Critics also say the plan would kneecap open source, because open-weight models, the ones anyone can download and run, don't fit a system built around evaluators sitting inside a company. And they argue that voluntary coordination inside the industry is a way to avoid real legal safeguards written by governments. On Hacker News the argument got sharper. One widely shared post says Anthropic is caught in a regulatory-capture financial loop. Regulatory capture is when the industry being regulated ends up writing its own rules. The claim is that Anthropic's safety advocacy and its business interests feed each other: push for rules, the rules make it harder for new companies to enter, and Anthropic benefits. Another front-page post was titled simply Dario, Please, a direct appeal to Amodei by name. From a different direction, former FTC chair Lina Khan went further than anyone. She said it's time to break out the handcuffs for AI CEOs, and she cited a precedent from 1934. Her point is not about slowing down or speeding up. She thinks voluntary self-policing isn't enough, and that executives should face personal legal consequences. So there are three camps. The labs say coordination is the responsible move. Competition critics say coordination among the biggest companies is the problem. And Khan says the answer is law enforced against the people at the top, not promises from them.

My take: both things can be true at once. The risks Amodei describes can be real, and the biggest companies can still shape the rules to suit themselves. The answer lies in details nobody has published yet. Who do the evaluators answer to, the companies or someone independent? Do the rules apply based on what a model can do, or based on who built it? If a small lab and a giant face the same test, that's safety. If only the giants decide who passes, that's a cartel. And one very important company hasn't signed on at all.

## That company is Nvidia. Nearly every lab in that list trains its models on Nvidia chips. That makes Jensen Huang the one person whose revenue depends most directly on nobody slowing down.

Musk and Altman backed Amodei's call. Huang went to President Trump and told him: we're not going to let an AI slowdown happen. That's a striking thing for a supplier to say about the stated plan of his own biggest customers. Look at it from Nvidia's side. If the labs really slow the growth of model capabilities, the most direct way to do that is to train fewer and smaller frontier models. That means buying fewer of Nvidia's most expensive chips. Huang also went to the right place. A global slowdown agreement cannot happen without the U.S. government, and Amodei's plan explicitly calls for domestic regulation. The labs can coordinate voluntarily among themselves, but anything binding needs Washington. Huang made sure Washington heard the opposite argument from the company at the center of the hardware supply.

So the lineup is strange. The AI labs say they want to slow down. The company selling them the hardware says no. The White House has now heard from both. Here's what to watch: whether any lab's chip orders actually shrink. That number will tell you more than any essay will.

## Away from the policy fight, Apple shipped its biggest AI bet today.

iOS 27 is out, along with macOS 27, named Golden Gate, plus watchOS 27, visionOS 27 and tvOS 27. The headline feature on every one of them except tvOS is Siri AI. It's a rebuild of Siri on a large language model, the same kind of technology behind ChatGPT and Claude. The word Apple keeps using is context-aware. That means Siri is supposed to understand what's on your screen and what you've been doing, instead of treating every request as a brand new command. The releases also refine Liquid Glass, the translucent design Apple introduced last year, which drew plenty of complaints about text being hard to read. Here's why this matters. Among the big consumer tech companies, Apple has been the most visibly behind on generative AI. The old Siri became a punchline, and Apple leaned on a ChatGPT integration to fill the gap. This release is Apple's claim that its own assistant is now good enough to be the main way people talk to more than a billion devices.

The real test isn't the keynote demo. It's the thousandth boring request. Set a timer. Text my partner that I'm running late. Find that PDF from last week. If Siri AI handles the boring stuff reliably, Apple's sheer number of devices gets it back in the game almost overnight. And that ChatGPT integration is still causing legal trouble, just not for Apple anymore.

## Elon Musk's antitrust lawsuit over the Apple and OpenAI partnership now has only one defendant left.

Some background. In 2024, when Apple announced it would build ChatGPT into iPhone features, Musk called it Apple letting OpenAI install creepy spyware. In 2025 he sued both companies. He claimed the partnership gave them a monopoly on Apple users' AI prompts and hurt competition in both smartphones and chatbots. It got personal when Musk argued that his own chatbot, Grok, was being kept from the top of the App Store. Last August he posted that Apple made it impossible for any AI company except OpenAI to reach number one. Now Apple has found a way out of the case, and Musk appears to be done going after Apple. OpenAI is left to fight the suit alone. In the same stretch, OpenAI made another Apple-adjacent move. It is buying Glass Imaging, a smartphone camera company, for a reported 300 million dollars. Glass Imaging was founded by two former Apple engineers who led the team that built Portrait Mode on the iPhone. OpenAI already bought io, Jony Ive's hardware startup, last year to build its own devices. A device that sees the world needs a very good small camera, and OpenAI just bought a company started by people who did that work at Apple.

Put today's Apple and OpenAI news side by side. They started as partners. Now Apple ships its own assistant, gets out of a lawsuit it shared with OpenAI, and OpenAI buys a camera company founded by Apple's former Portrait Mode leads. These two are becoming competitors, and Musk's lawsuit now targets only one of them.

## The next legal fight may matter more than Musk's, because it decides whether AI agents are allowed to shop for you.

Amazon versus Perplexity is now at the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit. Here's the background. Perplexity makes an AI browser called Comet. Its agent can browse and buy things on your behalf, including logging into your Amazon account. Amazon sued in late 2025. It claimed Comet got into customer accounts without Amazon's permission and made automated activity look like a regular person using Chrome. Amazon says that breaks the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, the main U.S. anti-hacking law. Perplexity's position is that the user gave permission and it's the user's account. It says Amazon just wants to protect its own shopping pages and ad business from being skipped. The appeals court is why this matters. A district court ruling decides one case. A Ninth Circuit ruling becomes binding precedent across the western United States, and that includes California, where most AI companies are based. This same court has shaped the law here before, in hiQ versus LinkedIn, the big web scraping case. The underlying question is easy to state and hard to answer. When you tell an AI agent to act for you, whose permission counts: yours, or the website's?

If Amazon wins, any website can lock out agents it doesn't like, and only the big platforms get to offer shopping agents. If Perplexity wins, your agent gets the same access you have. Either way, the rules for agents get written by this court. Now, on to what running those agents costs.

## Two posts this week asked the same practical question from opposite ends: how much model do you actually need?

The first compares OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Luna with GPT-6 Astra for code review, and asks whether a model priced at a dollar twenty is good enough. That's the right way to frame it. Code review is a narrow, repetitive job. The model reads a set of code changes, looks for bugs and flags problems. That doesn't obviously need the flagship model, and teams that run a review on every pull request pay for every single call. The second post is a set of notes from someone moving 35 kilobytes of pre-prompts from Anthropic's Claude Opus to a self-hosted model running on Ollama. Pre-prompts are the long standing instructions a model gets before any task. Ollama is a popular tool for running open models on your own machine. 35 kilobytes of text is roughly five or six thousand words of instructions, which is a lot. The notes are about the gotchas: what breaks when carefully tuned instructions move from a frontier model to a smaller local one. One well-known trap is worth mentioning here. Ollama's default context window, meaning how much text the model can see at once, is small. If your instructions don't fit, the model silently loses part of them, and the output just gets worse without any error message.

Connect this back to the top story. If the frontier really does slow down, money and attention move to exactly this kind of work: making cheaper models good enough and running them yourself. Slowing the most powerful models does nothing to slow down everything below them.

## Now a security story, about AI systems seeing things before people do.

A post getting attention claims that OpenAI's bots knew about a RubyGems caching vulnerability. RubyGems is the package registry for the Ruby programming language. It's where developers download the libraries their software depends on. A caching flaw in a registry like that is serious, because one bad cached package can reach many people who never touched the original. This belongs to the class of problems called supply chain attacks: instead of hacking the target directly, you tamper with something the target downloads. The headline's claim is that OpenAI's automated systems had knowledge of the flaw. That raises questions nobody has good answers to yet. If an AI crawler or agent runs into a security hole, does anyone read those logs? Does the company running it have a duty to disclose what it found? And if models learn about unpatched bugs from the web, they could repeat them to anyone who asks. In a related vein, a project called Pion is pitching an agent designed to run any company on its own. Treat that as a goal, not a result. A real business has to file taxes, sign contracts and take responsibility when something breaks, and that list is far longer than what today's agents do reliably.

These two fit together. Agents are getting more and more access, to package registries and to the day-to-day running of companies. But there's still no agreed process for what should happen when an agent finds something it shouldn't have.

## Speech AI next, where a small company says open models can beat the closed ones on a public benchmark.

Nari Labs made Dia last year. It was the first open-source text-to-speech model that could produce natural dialogue between speakers. Their new argument is that closed speech models don't lead because they're better. They lead because nobody has done the engineering to serve open models fast. The standard inference engines, vLLM and SGLang, are the software that actually runs models in production. Nari says they were built for text and are a poor fit for audio. To prove it, Nari built its own engine for Alibaba's open Qwen3 text-to-speech model and open-sourced it. It runs at under 50 milliseconds of delay while handling 10 requests per second. Now Nari sells hosted versions of Qwen3 text-to-speech and Qwen3 speech recognition. Here are their numbers on the Coval voice AI benchmarks. Their text-to-speech service is second fastest and first in accuracy, ahead of ElevenLabs and Cartesia, and it's the cheapest. Accuracy here is word error rate: you transcribe the generated audio back to text and count how many words came out wrong. Their speech recognition service is the fastest, second in accuracy, a tenth of a percentage point behind first place, and second cheapest. Nari also says Alibaba's own official services for these same models perform worse.

One caveat. These are the numbers Nari chose to highlight, and word error rate doesn't measure whether a voice sounds natural. But the Alibaba comparison is the real finding. The same open model, run by two different companies, gave different results. How you run a model now matters about as much as which model you pick.

## Two research stories now, both about how models learn.

Sakana AI, the Tokyo research lab, published a paper and code for an alternative to backpropagation, called Augmented Lagrangian Predictive Coding. Backpropagation is how essentially every neural network is trained today. You run the input forward, measure the error at the end, then send that error backward through every layer to work out how each one should change. It works extremely well. But the whole network has to wait for that backward pass, and it has to keep a lot of intermediate results in memory while it does. Predictive coding is an alternative inspired by the brain. Each layer tries to predict the activity of its neighbor and adjusts itself using only its own local prediction error. There's no single backward pass through the whole network. The appeal is that local updates are much easier to run in parallel, and they could suit new kinds of hardware. The long-standing problem is that predictive coding has been hard to make train as stably, and as well, as backpropagation on large networks. Augmented Lagrangian is a classic method from mathematical optimization for solving problems that come with constraints. Sakana applies it to predictive coding, and the aim of work in this area is to close that gap with backpropagation. The second story asks a good question: why don't machine learning research agents overfit? Overfitting here means tuning so hard against one test that your results look great on that test and fall apart everywhere else. AI research agents run many experiments against the same benchmark, which should be a recipe for overfitting. The observation in the title is that it isn't happening as much as you'd expect, and understanding why matters if we're going to trust results produced by agents. And if you want to read how today's models are actually built, not just use them, a project called OpenArch collects PyTorch implementations of modern large language model architectures in one place.

Here's why this belongs next to the top story. Every pacing proposal so far is about compute: how many chips, how big the training run. A training method that gets more out of the same chips doesn't show up in any of those counts. Better algorithms are the part of AI progress that nobody knows how to slow down.

## To finish, a hardware hack I really like.

Meta's Neural Band is the wristband that comes with Meta's display glasses. It reads sEMG, short for surface electromyography: the small electrical signals your forearm muscles give off when you move your fingers, even slightly. It's how you control the glasses with small finger gestures. A developer who goes by callbacked had a ten-month-old proof of concept for reading those signals from the band without the glasses. They handed that old code to the GPT-6 Astra model, and it finished the job quickly. Then they built Kinesis, which lets you control a Mac with the band. Swipe your fingers to switch desktops. Swipe up to open Mission Control. Turn an invisible knob in the air to change the volume. The developer adds one honest caveat: so far it has only been tested on their own band.

This is the good side of this week's themes. A coding agent turned a stalled side project into a working tool in a couple of hours. And a piece of hardware that only worked with one product now works with another. The frontier labs can keep arguing about pacing. People are going to keep building.
