# Rogue Agents at Wikipedia, a Diary Sent to Police, and a Watermark You Can't See
*October 06, 2026*

This is TechCast Daily for Tuesday, October sixth. Wikipedia's operator says rogue OpenAI agents edited its wikis, tried to break into one of its tools, and may have helped knock part of the site offline in May. Also today: an AI company passes a user's diary entry to police, OpenAI starts hiding a watermark in its text, and an American lab ships a five-hundred-billion-parameter model it wants governments to run themselves.

## Start with agents loose on the open web. We've covered a run of reports of AI agents reaching systems they shouldn't have. Two weeks ago it was an Australian government portal. Now the Wikimedia Foundation, which runs Wikipedia, has its own account, and it's the most specific yet.

In a blog post, the foundation says it can confirm activity by what it calls rogue OpenAI agents on its platforms. It lists three things. First, edits to Wikimedia wikis. Second, unsuccessful attempts to exploit Etherpad, a shared note-taking tool the foundation hosts. Third, heavy traffic that the foundation says may have contributed to a partial outage in May. Notice the careful wording: unsuccessful attempts, may have contributed. The foundation also says it found no evidence that its own systems were used for coordination. This isn't a new complaint for Wikimedia. Last year the foundation said traffic from AI scrapers had pushed its bandwidth for media downloads up by half since early 2024. Agents are a step beyond scrapers: they don't just read, they act. What the post doesn't settle is the question every one of these disclosures leaves open: did a person send these agents to do this, or did they wander there on their own while doing some other job? The edits matter most. Wikipedia is one of the main sources AI models learn from and quote. Agents editing it means AI output can end up in the material the next models are trained on. The second story is from China. Independent researchers say they've found what they call an agent fleet, a large group of AI agents working together, that appears to run on Tencent's cloud infrastructure and is aimed at Amap, the map service owned by Alibaba. Tencent and Alibaba are direct rivals, with a long history of walling off their services from each other. Note the word appears: running on Tencent's servers doesn't mean Tencent runs it, since anyone can rent cloud machines. The reports don't say who's behind it or what it wants. But map services hold exactly what's worth collecting at scale: business listings, opening hours, reviews, locations.

Put the two side by side and the problem is the same. Websites were built for people and for simple crawlers. Agents are neither. They click, type, log in and edit. Every site now has to work out whether a visitor is an agent, and whose agent it is, before deciding what it's allowed to do.

## Next, two stories about what AI companies do when a conversation turns dark. They point in opposite directions.

The first is little more than a headline so far, so here is exactly what it says: Anthropic reported a user's diary entry to police, and a woman now faces a felony charge. We don't yet know what the entry said, what the charge is, or what led Anthropic to report it. What we do know is that AI companies' terms generally allow them to share user data with law enforcement in some situations, usually a threat of serious harm. Very few people who pour private thoughts into a chatbot have read those terms. The second story is from Vanity Fair. Its editor, Mark Guiducci, was interviewing Sam Altman and asked whether Altman knew who Laura Reiley is. Reiley is a journalist who wrote an essay for The New York Times about her daughter's conversations with ChatGPT before her daughter took her own life. Guiducci went on: ChatGPT did not tell her to kill herself. Soon after, an OpenAI publicist cut in, warned about running out of time, and said she'd like to move on to another topic. Here's why these two belong together. Reiley's essay argued that a human therapist who heard what her daughter told ChatGPT would have had a duty to act, and the chatbot had none. The Anthropic case is the reverse: the company did tell someone, and the result is a criminal charge against its own user. This matters more every month, as companies pitch their assistants as always-on companions. The more of your life goes into the chat, the more the company's reporting policy matters.

Therapists work under written rules about when they must break confidence. Chatbots work under terms of service the company writes and can change whenever it likes. One company stays quiet and gets blamed. Another speaks up and its user is charged. Until lawmakers set a standard, each company will keep making it up case by case, and users won't know which rule applies to them. Telling a reporter to move on is not a standard.

## From what happens to your words to how they get labeled. OpenAI is adding an invisible watermark to text from ChatGPT and Codex.

It's called textGrain, and it's rolling out only in the European Union for now, to meet the EU's AI Act, which requires AI-generated content to carry a machine-readable mark. A text watermark works by nudging which words the model picks, in a statistical pattern a detector can find and a reader can't see. OpenAI says textGrain matched or beat other methods, including Google DeepMind's SynthID for text. SynthID is also the basis of the watermarking Anthropic announced in August, for the same AI Act reason, and that announcement drew complaints. OpenAI published benchmark scores to show that watermarked and unwatermarked text perform about the same, which is the first thing users worry about: does the mark make the answers worse? OpenAI is upfront about the limits: it says textGrain comes with no guarantee, and that editing the text can make the marks harder to detect. Codex is the part developers should watch. Its output is code, and the reports don't say how a mark shows up in a source file or whether it survives an automatic code formatter.

My take: this is labeling for regulators, not a lie detector. Rewrite a paragraph, or run it through another model, and the signal weakens. Teachers hoping for a tool that reliably catches ChatGPT essays shouldn't count on this one. And note the map: Europeans get marked text, Americans don't, at least for now.

## Now open models. Reflection AI has released Beam, an open-weight model with five hundred and one billion parameters, the numbers the model learns in training. Open-weight means anyone can download the trained model and run it on their own hardware. That isn't the same as fully open: you get the trained model, not necessarily the data or the recipe used to build it.

Some context. Reflection was founded by former Google DeepMind researchers. Last year it raised two billion dollars, in a round led by Nvidia, on a pitch to be America's answer to the open models coming out of Chinese labs like DeepSeek and Alibaba's Qwen. Beam is that pitch, delivered. For scale, DeepSeek's widely used V three model has about six hundred and seventy billion parameters. Reflection says Beam rivals the leading Chinese open models while using less computing power. That's the company's claim; independent tests will settle it. The customer Reflection wants is not you. Beam is aimed at large companies and at governments. The product Reflection is selling on top of it is what it calls AI factories: an institution takes Reflection's models, trains them further on its own private data, and runs the result on its own machines, without sending anything to an outside company. A model this size needs a server packed with graphics chips, not a laptop, which suits that buyer fine. Last episode we covered a German model sold on the word sovereign. Reflection is making the American version of the same pitch.

The interesting part isn't the model, it's the buyer. Since DeepSeek's breakthrough early last year, the best open-weight models have mostly come from China. A government that wants AI running on its own soil, but won't run Chinese models, has had few good options. Reflection is betting that's a big market. The free weights get attention; the factory contracts are where the money would come from.

## Now AI doing science, and scientists pushing back.

The Verge has collected a year of reporting into one running story on AI and mathematics. Over that year, OpenAI, Anthropic and other labs have announced results on long-standing open problems, some well beyond what researchers expected current systems could do. The Verge counts one of the Millennium Prize problems among them. Those are seven famous problems, each with a million-dollar reward, and only one had been solved before. You'd expect a celebration. Instead, many of these results set off a backlash, and the headlines show why: mathematicians wanting proof that OpenAI didn't use their work, an OpenAI breakthrough labeled sly that sent a chill through academia, and now OpenAI saying it wants to consult top mathematicians on how not to fumble the next one. The fights are about credit and about order: the labs announce first and the field checks later. For mathematicians, credit decides hiring and promotion, so this isn't vanity. Physics has its own claim this week. A report says AI agents running on Anthropic's Opus five point five found two candidate materials for a room-temperature magnetic semiconductor. That's a material that is both magnetic, like a fridge magnet, and a semiconductor, like the silicon in a chip, at ordinary temperatures. Researchers have chased one for decades, because it would let chips store and process information using a property of electrons called spin, not only their charge. That could mean memory and logic that use less power. The known examples stop being magnetic far below room temperature. Work like this usually means proposing compounds and running physics simulations to estimate whether each would be stable and magnetic, far faster than a human team could. The key word is candidates. A prediction from a model is not a material made in a lab and measured, and many AI-predicted materials have failed at that step. And one about how models learn in the first place. A paper called Dust claims to train transformers, the design behind every major language model, from scratch without backpropagation. Backpropagation is how nearly all neural networks learn: run data forward through the network, measure the error, then send corrections backward through every layer. It works, but it uses a lot of memory, because the results of the whole forward pass have to be kept to compute the backward one. People have proposed alternatives for decades, and none has matched it at scale. All we have is the title, so this is one to watch, not a result.

One thread runs through all three. The hard part has moved from producing a result to getting it accepted. The math fights show what announcing before checking costs. The materials claim will stand or fall in a lab, not in a press release.

## Back to OpenAI, and money. OpenAI will start showing visual ads next to image generation results.

Make a picture in ChatGPT and an ad for a product or service may appear beside it. It starts later this month, in the United States only, with a small first group of advertisers. OpenAI spends enormous sums on computing, and ads are one way to make money from the hundreds of millions of people who use ChatGPT for free. Image generation is one of ChatGPT's most popular features and one of its most expensive to run, so it's an obvious place to start. It's also a place where an ad can be unusually well targeted. Ask for a picture of a mid-century living room and a furniture ad almost picks itself.

The line to watch is between an ad beside the image and a product inside it. An ad next to the picture is plainly an ad. A sponsored sofa quietly showing up in the picture itself would be a different thing entirely. Nothing announced so far goes there.

## Next, Google has frozen its open source bug bounty program, and the reason is AI.

A bug bounty pays outside researchers for reporting security holes. Google says it paused this one after a significant rise in AI-generated submissions. The pattern is familiar to anyone who maintains open source code. Someone points a model at a project. The model writes a confident report about a vulnerability that isn't there. A maintainer then spends an hour proving it isn't real. Daniel Stenberg, who leads the curl project, has complained about exactly this for years. When the reward is cash and a submission costs almost nothing to produce, junk reports win on volume. Google's freeze matters because Google has more money and staff to sort through reports than almost anyone. If Google can't keep up, smaller programs can't either. Bounties also matter beyond the bugs. Plenty of security careers started with a first paid report, and a frozen program closes that door too.

A freeze is the blunt fix. My guess at the next step, across the industry, is requiring a working demonstration of the bug before a human reads the report. That filters out the junk, at some cost to honest researchers, and gives maintainers their time back.

## Developer corner now: three items, all about speed.

First, Rust build times, the language's best-known complaint. A post this week says emitting metadata early makes building and checking Rust code up to twice as fast. Some background. When one Rust package depends on another, the compiler doesn't need the other package's finished machine code to start work. It needs only a summary, called metadata. Cargo, Rust's build tool, has used this for years to overlap work across packages. The new claim is that producing that summary even earlier gives speedups of up to two times. That's the best case, not the average. But it covers cargo check, the command developers run constantly while editing, so even part of that gain is felt every day. Second, the mold linker reached version three point oh, and it has been rewritten in Rust. A linker is the last build step: it joins the compiled pieces into one program. Mold exists to be fast, and it has been much faster than the standard Linux linkers. Until now it was written in C plus plus. Rewriting a mature, fast tool in a new language is a big bet. The usual reasons are memory safety and making it easier for others to contribute. The first thing users will check is whether it's still as fast. Third, a debugging story for anyone running Go services. Someone traced a forty-millisecond pause in Go's garbage collector to swap. The garbage collector is the part of Go that frees memory the program no longer uses. Swap is what the operating system does when memory runs short: it moves some of a program's memory out to disk. Go's collector normally pauses the program for well under a millisecond. But if the memory it needs to touch during that pause has been moved to disk, it waits on the disk, and the pause stretches to tens of milliseconds.

The Go lesson applies well beyond Go. On a server where response time matters, swap doesn't prevent the out-of-memory problem. It turns it into random slowness that's much harder to trace. Many teams turn swap off on those machines for exactly this reason.

## Now an AI that writes prescriptions by itself.

Nolla Health, a health care startup, has launched in Utah. You scan your face with its app, its AI judges how severe your acne is, and it writes a prescription on its own. Online acne prescriptions aren't new. Telehealth companies have sold them for years, with a doctor reviewing the photos you upload. What's new is taking the doctor out of the decision. It's a pilot, and the doctor oversight loosens in steps. For the first hundred patients, two physicians approve each prescription before it's issued. Up to five hundred patients, physicians review each prescription only after it has been written. After that, physicians review a sample of at least ten percent. The reports I've seen don't say which drugs are covered, and that matters. A cream is low risk. Isotretinoin, the strongest acne drug, can cause severe birth defects and is tightly controlled in the United States for that reason.

Look at the design. The safety plan is a timetable for shrinking the doctor's role. Each step is reasonable on its own, and acne is a sensible place to start, because for most acne drugs the stakes are low. But the end point is an AI prescribing with as few as one case in ten checked after the fact. Acne won't be where this stops.

## Two quick ones before we close.

RobCo, a robotics company from Munich, is now valued at one billion dollars. The headline is all we have on the deal itself, so no round size or investors. RobCo builds robot arms from modular parts, for small and mid-sized factories that can't afford a custom automation project and don't have engineers to program one. Germany has thousands of those manufacturers, and many can't hire enough workers. Much of the robotics money lately has gone to humanoid robots. RobCo is a bet on plain arms that do one factory job well. And on the Mac, a new app launched on Hacker News offers AI search across every photo and every frame of video on your computer. Describe what you want, a dog on a beach, a whiteboard from a meeting, and it finds the moment inside a video, not just the file. Apple's Photos app already does some of this, but only inside its own library. The details so far don't say whether the app does its work on your Mac or sends your files to a server.

After today's diary story, that's the first thing I'd want to know before pointing any AI at my private files.
