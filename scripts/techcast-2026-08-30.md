# TechCast Daily: The Bill With a B In Front Of It
*August 30, 2026*

Two of the biggest names in music just walked into a federal courthouse in San Francisco and asked for damages measured in billions. The defendant is Anthropic, and the legal theory is the same one that already cost the company one and a half billion dollars once this year. Also on today's show: why Nvidia's real lead has stopped being about the GPU at all, and a Go bug that only appears when your pointers are four bytes wide.

## Sony Music and Warner Chappell have sued Anthropic in the Northern District of California. To understand why this one lands harder than the average AI lawsuit, you need to know what happened earlier this year. Anthropic settled a class action brought by book authors for one and a half billion dollars. And the judge in that case had drawn a very specific line: training a model on books the company had legitimately bought was fair use. Downloading those same books from pirate libraries was not. The settlement was about the downloading, not the training.

The labels are aiming directly at that line. Their complaint covers what they describe as tens of thousands of copyrighted works, and they are asking for up to one hundred fifty thousand dollars per work, which is the statutory maximum reserved for infringement a court finds willful. On top of that they want up to twenty-five thousand dollars for each instance where identifying copyright information was allegedly stripped from a file. That second claim is the one worth watching. It is a separate cause of action from the infringement itself, and it does not depend on winning the fair use argument at all. If you obtained a music file that had ownership metadata attached, and that metadata came off somewhere in your pipeline, that is a distinct alleged violation with its own price tag. Multiply either number by tens of thousands and you get a headline figure in the billions. These are allegations, not findings, and the maximum award is a ceiling rather than an expectation. Anthropic has not yet responded in court. It is also not the first time the music industry has come after this particular company. Music publishers sued over song lyrics back in 2023, a case that produced a set of agreed guardrails rather than a verdict.

Here is the strategic read. The industry has stopped arguing about whether machine learning on copyrighted material is transformative, because that argument is genuinely hard and courts are split. Instead they are arguing about acquisition. How did the files get on your servers, and did you pay for them. That is a boring, factual, extremely winnable question, and it is answered by server logs rather than by philosophy. Every frontier lab has a data provenance problem sitting in its history, and the plaintiffs have now found the cheapest possible way to litigate it.

## Two hardware stories that are really one story. The Verge has a piece arguing that Nvidia's advantage in the new generation of data center systems is coming from smarter traffic control rather than more processor cycles. And separately, Samsung's processing-in-memory work resurfaced on Hacker News. Put them next to each other and you get the single most important shift in computing hardware right now.

Start with the physics. A rough rule of thumb that hardware architects have repeated for two decades: fetching a number from main memory costs something like an order of magnitude more energy than doing the arithmetic on it once it arrives. Compute got cheap. Moving data did not. That gap is why a modern accelerator running a large model spends a meaningful fraction of its life waiting rather than calculating. Nvidia's answer has been to attack the waiting. The interconnect between chips, the switch fabric between servers, the networking gear that Nvidia bought when it acquired Mellanox for just under seven billion dollars in 2019, the offload processors that handle storage and security so the main chips do not have to. None of that shows up in a benchmark of raw floating point operations. All of it shows up in how much of a cluster you can actually keep busy, which is the number that determines what a training run costs you. Samsung is attacking the same problem from the opposite end. Processing in memory means putting small compute units physically inside the memory banks, so simple operations happen where the data already lives and never travel to the processor at all. Samsung demonstrated this in high bandwidth memory years ago. The workload it suits best is exactly the one everyone now runs: generating tokens one at a time from a large model is limited by memory bandwidth, not by arithmetic. The reason it has not taken over is not physics, it is software. There is no standard way to program it, so nobody writes for it, so nobody ships it in volume.

The uncomfortable implication for anyone hoping to compete with Nvidia: a faster chip is a solvable engineering problem, and several companies have solved it. A faster chip plus the switches plus the cabling plus the software that makes ten thousand of them behave like one machine is a systems problem, and systems problems take years and are much harder to copy. The moat moved, and it moved to a place that is boring enough that most people are not looking at it.

## Two pieces on multi-agent systems trended together today, and the pairing is almost too neat. One is called The Rise and Fall of Agent Civilizations. The other is called Domain-Driven Agents. One is the autopsy, the other is the surgical technique.

The arc in that first title is the arc most people who have actually built these things will recognize. You put a lot of language model agents in a shared environment, give them roles and the ability to talk to each other, and for a while it is genuinely remarkable. They coordinate, they divide labor, they develop something that looks like convention. And then it degrades. The failure modes are well documented at this point and they are not mysterious. Errors compound, because one agent's confident mistake becomes the next agent's input and there is no ground truth anywhere in the loop to correct it. Agents agree with each other far too readily, so a bad plan gets ratified instead of challenged. Context windows fill with the transcript of the agents talking to each other rather than with the actual task. And the token bill grows with the square of how chatty you let them be. The second piece points at the discipline that fixes some of this, and it is borrowed from software architecture rather than from AI. Domain-driven design is an old idea: carve a system into bounded contexts, where each part owns a specific slice of the business and speaks a precise vocabulary for that slice, and the seams between parts are explicit contracts rather than vibes. Apply that to agents and you stop building one general agent that can do everything badly. You build a narrow agent whose tools, data access, and vocabulary are all scoped to one domain, and you make the handoff between agents a defined interface instead of a chat message.

My read is that this is the same lesson the industry learned about microservices, arriving about fifteen years later with a new vocabulary. Decomposition is not free. Every boundary you add is a place where meaning gets lost in translation, and an agent boundary loses more meaning than a function call does, because the interface is natural language. The teams shipping working agent systems this year are mostly the ones running two or three tightly scoped agents, not a civilization.

## A useful counterweight to the AI-fixes-everything narrative, from someone with unusual standing to offer it. Vijay Pande spent years running the biotech practice at Andreessen Horowitz, roughly four billion dollars of it. He is a former Stanford professor, the person behind the Folding at Home distributed computing project. Last year he left to start something much smaller and AI-native called VZVC, and he spoke to TechCrunch about why.

His framing of the science is the part worth carrying around. Biology, he argues, is finally moving from a discovery science to an engineering one. Discovery means you go looking and hope to find something. Engineering means you specify what you want and build it. If protein structure and molecular design become predictable enough to design against, the front end of drug development stops being a lottery and starts being a design process. But he is blunt about where that stops. Clinical trials remain brutally expensive, and no model tells you what a compound does inside a living human being. Nobody is compressing the multi-year, multi-hundred-million-dollar process of proving a drug is safe and works. So you get a strange shape: the cheap part gets much cheaper and much faster, while the expensive part does not move at all. Which explains the fund strategy. His line is that he is not doing thirty bets a year. When generating candidates is cheap, the scarce thing is not deal flow, it is the judgment and capital to push a small number of things through the part that stayed expensive. His third argument is the most contrarian one. He thinks open, shared datasets rather than proprietary walled-off ones are what will actually let AI transform medicine. That cuts against a whole generation of companies whose entire pitch to investors was that they own a data asset nobody else can touch.

That last point deserves scrutiny rather than applause, because it happens to be convenient. If you run a small fund, a world of shared data is a world where your companies are not locked out by the incumbents' hoards. But convenient is not the same as wrong, and the history of computational biology is largely a history of open datasets producing the breakthroughs. Protein structure prediction got solved on top of a public structure database that scientists had been depositing into for fifty years.

## Now into the developer tools block, starting with two stories that are both about the same thing: the industry's long, grinding process of admitting that not every computer is a sixty-four-bit x86 machine. RISC-V is now an officially supported platform for CPython. And separately, a writeup of hunting down a Go runtime bug on thirty-two-bit embedded systems.

Official support in CPython is a formal thing, not a vibe. The project defines tiers, and moving up means there is a named person responsible for the platform, there are continuous integration machines actually running the test suite on it, and a failure on that platform can block a release. Before that, an architecture works because someone patched it locally and it happened to compile. After, breaking it is a bug that stops the train. Why RISC-V, why now: the hardware became something you can actually buy and rent rather than simulate, and there is enormous state-level investment pushing it, particularly in China, as a way around export controls on chip designs. The practical caveat is that language support is the first mile, not the last one. The reason people can use Python on a platform is the compiled ecosystem underneath it, and prebuilt packages for numerical and machine learning libraries lag architecture support by a long way. The Go story is the other side of the same coin. Thirty-two bit is not a historical curiosity, it is what is running inside an enormous amount of industrial and embedded equipment, and language runtimes accumulate quiet assumptions that only break there. The classic example in Go is memory alignment: on a thirty-two-bit machine, doing an atomic operation on a sixty-four-bit value requires that value to be aligned on an eight byte boundary, and if a struct field lands on the wrong boundary the program crashes, sometimes not immediately and sometimes not reproducibly. Go eventually added dedicated atomic types that handle the alignment for you, precisely because so many people had been bitten. Add the fact that a Go integer is thirty-two bits wide on those platforms, and arithmetic that never overflowed on your laptop starts overflowing in the field.

The pattern in both is worth naming. Portability bugs are not usually exotic. They are ordinary assumptions that were never written down because they were true on every machine the author had ever touched. The only reliable fix is the boring one: a machine of that architecture in continuous integration, running the tests on every commit.

## Staying low to the ground. There is a piece going around about calling nested functions indirectly in GCC without needing an executable stack. That sentence means nothing until you know the backstory, and the backstory is genuinely one of the strangest corners of the C toolchain.

GCC has a non-standard extension that lets you define a function inside another function, and the inner one can see the outer one's local variables. Convenient. The problem appears the moment you take the address of that inner function and pass it somewhere, say to a sorting routine that takes a callback. A plain function pointer is just an address. It has nowhere to carry the information about which invocation of the outer function it belongs to. So GCC solves this by writing a small piece of machine code onto the stack at runtime, containing the outer frame's location baked in, and handing out a pointer to that. It is called a trampoline. It works, and it requires the stack to be executable, because you are jumping into instructions that live there. Every security practice of the last twenty years says a page of memory should be writable or executable and never both, since an attacker who can write to the stack should not be able to run what they wrote. Operating systems and linkers now default to non-executable stacks, and toolchains have gotten increasingly loud about binaries that ask for the old behaviour. So a comfortable language extension quietly became a security marker on your binary. The escape routes are the interesting part: allocating trampolines somewhere other than the stack, or using an indirection where the pointer you hand out is really a small record holding both the code address and the context, which is how several other architectures and languages have always done closures.

The general lesson, and it generalizes well beyond C: a feature that requires generating code at runtime will eventually collide with a platform that has decided runtime code generation is a threat. It happened here, it happened to browser engines on locked-down mobile platforms, and it will happen again.

## A reference page rather than news, but it surfaced today and it is the single most useful page for anyone who writes Python and has never had a performance problem yet. It is the table of time complexities for Python's built-in types.

The concrete items are what matter here. Indexing into a list is constant time. Appending to the end is effectively constant time. But inserting at the front, or popping from the front, costs time proportional to the length of the list, because every other element has to shuffle over. Checking whether an item is in a list means walking the whole list. Checking whether an item is in a set or a dictionary is constant time on average. Slicing copies, so it costs as much as the slice is long. Sorting is n log n. If you need to add and remove at both ends, there is a double-ended queue in the collections module where both ends are constant time. Here is why any of this is worth twenty minutes of your evening. Almost nobody writes an obviously slow algorithm. What people write is a loop over a list of records that checks whether each one is in another list. Each check walks that second list. Ten thousand records against ten thousand records is a hundred million comparisons, and the fix is one word: turn the second list into a set. The same shape shows up with popping from the front of a queue in a loop, and with building a string by adding to it inside a loop. In every case the code looks completely reasonable, runs instantly on the test data, and falls over in production, because the cost only becomes visible when the input grows.

The reason this page keeps trending is that it is exactly the kind of knowledge that is invisible until it is urgent. The language is deliberately designed so that a list and a set look and feel almost identical to use. The performance difference between them is not a detail, it is the difference between a job that finishes and a job that does not.

## Next, a piece on functional state machines in Rust using the typestate and newtype patterns. Both of those names sound more academic than the ideas actually are, and the ideas are portable to other languages.

The newtype pattern first, because it takes ten seconds. You have a user identifier and an order identifier, and both of them are just numbers. So somewhere, eventually, someone passes one where the other belongs, and it compiles perfectly, and you find out about it from a support ticket. The newtype pattern says wrap each one in its own distinct type. Same number underneath, zero cost at runtime, but now the compiler refuses to let you mix them up. Typestate is the same instinct applied to time rather than to identity. Objects usually have states: a connection is unopened, then open, then closed. A request builder is incomplete until required fields are set. The usual approach is to store the state in a field and check it at the top of every method, returning an error if you are in the wrong one. Typestate instead encodes the state in the type itself. The unconnected socket type simply does not have a send method. Connecting consumes the old value and hands you back a different type that does. The impossible call is not caught at runtime, it does not exist, and the code to check for it never gets written. That is the functional part: transitions take ownership of the old state and produce the new one, so the old value cannot be used again. Rust makes this natural because of how ownership works. The costs are real: the types get noisy, error messages get long, and the pattern fits sequential lifecycles better than states with lots of loops back on themselves.

The theme running through this and the Python complexity page is the same. Both are about pushing a category of mistake somewhere earlier and cheaper. One pushes it from production into your head. This one pushes it from your test suite into the compiler, which is the only reviewer that never gets tired.

## A Show HN worth talking about, because the positioning is sharper than most. It is called Typebase, and the author's pitch is direct: the developer experience of Convex with the openness of Supabase.

The complaint driving it is specific and a lot of people share it. Supabase gets you a database and authentication in minutes, but authorization is done with Postgres row-level security, which means writing your permission rules in SQL as policies attached to tables. Convex solves the opposite half well, in that your server logic lives in your own code and your frontend calls it like a local function with types flowing through, but you take its database model and its realtime-first defaults as a package. Typebase's answer is a folder. You drop a typebase directory into your existing repository, define your tables in a schema file, and export server functions that the frontend calls directly with full type checking end to end. Authentication is included. One command deploys the whole thing to Vercel, Cloudflare Workers or Deno Deploy, with Neon for the database, or it generates the deployment code so you can host it yourself. Underneath it is glue over three existing projects: oRPC for the typed calls, Drizzle for the database layer, better-auth for accounts. Being thin glue over popular parts is genuinely the right call, because it means you can leave. It also means you inherit four upstream projects' release schedules and breaking changes. The one design question I would push on is authorization. Row-level security is unpleasant to write, but it has a property that application-level checks do not: the rule sits in the database, so it holds no matter which code path reaches the data. Move the rule into TypeScript functions and it holds only as long as every path goes through those functions, which is true right up until someone adds a background job or an admin script.

Still, the escape hatch is the feature here. The reason people hesitate on backend-as-a-service is not the first month, it is the fear of the year they outgrow it. A tool that will print out its own deployment code and let you walk away with it has answered the only question that actually matters.

## And now the one that is pure joy. Someone posted a Show HN with the note that they missed watching the moving blocks, so they built a real disk defragmenter for Linux.

If you are under about thirty, the reference needs explaining. On Windows in the nineties, defragmenting your hard drive produced a full-screen grid of coloured squares that slowly rearranged themselves as the program moved file pieces around, and an entire generation watched it for hours with no productive purpose whatsoever. The underlying problem was real. A spinning disk reads fast in a straight line and slowly when it has to move the head, and files written and deleted over time end up scattered in pieces. Gathering those pieces back into one place made the machine noticeably faster. Then two things happened. Filesystems got smarter about where they place files, so severe fragmentation stopped happening on its own. And solid state drives arrived, where there is no head to move and the seek penalty essentially disappears. Defragmenting an SSD is at best pointless and at worst wasteful, since flash cells have a finite number of writes. Which is why this is mostly a delightful toy. But not entirely. Fragmentation still bites on spinning disks, which are still where large media archives live, and on any filesystem that has been run close to full for a long time. Copy-on-write filesystems have a specific problem here, since databases and virtual machine images written in place get chopped into thousands of extents. And even on flash, a file in thousands of pieces costs you in metadata and in the work the kernel does to read it.

Honestly though, the niche is beside the point. Someone wanted the moving blocks back and wrote the thing. Not every piece of software needs to be a business, and the ones that aren't are usually the ones people actually enjoy reading the code of.

## Last item, and it is the oldest fight in consumer tech. A project called Tether that promises iMessage, SMS and more on Linux.

The reason this is hard is not technical incompetence, it is deliberate design. Apple ties messaging identity to Apple hardware. Getting a client onto a non-Apple system means one of two approaches. The first is a relay: you own a Mac, or an iPhone, and it stays online and forwards everything, so all of the credentials and the actual protocol handling stay on genuine Apple hardware and your Linux machine is just a window onto it. That approach is stable, and it works precisely because it is not really pretending to be anything. The second approach is to reimplement the registration process directly, which is far more useful and far more fragile. That is what Beeper Mini did at the end of 2023, and it was working, and Apple shut it down within days, and then again after it came back. Anything built on that footing is one server-side change away from being dead. There is a partial peace treaty now. Apple supports RCS, the modern successor to text messaging, so conversations between iPhones and Android phones finally get high quality images, read receipts and typing indicators. But RCS is a phone carrier standard. It solves the Android problem, not the desktop Linux problem, and iMessage itself remains exactly as closed as it was.

What keeps this alive is not the feature set, it is that a messaging platform's real lock-in is not the software, it is the people already on it. Everybody's group chat is the switching cost. That is why projects like this keep getting built, and why Apple keeps closing the door on them.

It moved from arithmetic to moving data, from writing code to verifying it, and for the AI labs, from building the model to explaining where the training data came from.
