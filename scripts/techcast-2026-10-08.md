# GPT-6 Answers With Buttons, Copilot Gets Your Files, and AI Clones Adobe
*October 08, 2026*

GPT six is out, and the change you'll see first isn't smarter answers but different-looking ones: ChatGPT now replies with charts, diagrams and buttons. Microsoft wants Copilot inside your files and running your Windows PC, while Mac users download a tool to rip Apple's AI out. And one developer says software is over, after using an AI to copy Photoshop and six other Adobe apps. This is TechCast Daily for Thursday, October eighth.

## Start with OpenAI, which is rolling out GPT six to all ChatGPT users. The announcement pairs the new model with a feature called Intelligent UI, and that's the part you'll notice first.

UI means user interface, the part of a program you see and touch. With Intelligent UI, an answer can mix text with diagrams, charts, forms and buttons you can tap. OpenAI's example: ask how a seven-speed bicycle is designed, and you get a drawing of the bike with buttons that highlight different parts. The important detail is in OpenAI's blog post. It says it trained GPT six to decide when a visual would beat plain text, and how to lay it out. So this isn't a display feature added to the app. The model itself picks the format. There's a real benefit too: a labeled diagram can explain how gears work faster than four paragraphs can. Why it matters: for almost four years a chatbot answer has been a block of prose, and you did the work of turning it into something useful. If the model can hand you a form to fill in, or a chart you can adjust, the chat window starts to replace small single-purpose websites. Comparing two mortgages used to mean searching for a calculator site. Now ChatGPT may build the calculator inside the reply. That's bad news for every simple web tool that depends on search traffic. The downside: a paragraph is easy to check. A clean interactive chart looks authoritative even when the numbers under it are wrong, and it's harder to see where a figure came from. Polished output makes mistakes more convincing, not less.

On Tuesday we covered OpenAI putting ads next to image results. An answer full of buttons is also a much better place to sell things. Watch where those buttons start to lead.

## Last week Microsoft pitched Copilot as the place your workday happens. This week it showed Copilot running the computer itself.

At its first live Windows and Surface event in two years, Microsoft showed Copilot getting access to the files on your PC and the ability to take actions anywhere in Windows. Microsoft calls the approach Hybrid Intelligence. In plain terms, some tasks run on a small AI model on your own machine, others go to a bigger model in Microsoft's cloud, and the system decides which goes where. Local is cheaper and keeps data at home. The cloud is smarter. The demo was taxes. Jacob Andreou, who runs Copilot, told the agent, here called Autopilot, that his accountant had emailed, and asked it to get her what she needed. The point is that the agent reads the email, finds the documents on disk and sends them, and nobody opens a folder. For tax documents, which steps stay on the laptop and which go to the cloud is the first thing you'd want to know. On Sunday we covered Apple tightening which apps get full access to a Mac's disk, with AI agents named as the reason. The same week, Microsoft demonstrates an agent going through tax documents. The two companies disagree sharply about how much an agent should see by default. The hardware is built for this. The Surface Laptop Ultra is Microsoft's first computer on Nvidia's new RTX Spark chip, which puts the main processor and a Blackwell graphics processor in one package. It starts at two thousand five hundred and ninety-nine dollars, ships October sixteenth, and comes with up to a hundred and twenty-eight gigabytes of memory shared between the two. That shared memory is what matters. Large AI models need a lot of fast memory, and on a normal laptop the graphics chip's own small memory is the limit. Pool it, and a laptop can run models that used to need a desktop graphics card or a rented cloud server. Microsoft also ran the game Gears of War: E-Day on it, to show it isn't only for developers.

Taxes were a revealing choice of demo. Those are exactly the files you'd want an agent to stay out of until it has a track record. Meanwhile, on the Mac, some people want less AI in their operating system, not more.

## Apple's new Mac software took away the easy way to say no to its AI. A developer put it back.

macOS twenty-seven, the version Apple calls Golden Gate, has no single switch to turn Apple Intelligence off. Earlier versions did. You can still hunt through settings and disable features one by one, but the AI models stay on your disk either way. The Verge found they can take up more than thirty gigabytes. That's a real cost on a Mac with the smallest drive Apple sells, and you pay it whether you use the features or not. Last week a developer who goes by Om Lahore on GitHub released RemoveMacAI. It's a command-line tool, meaning you run it by typing a command in the Terminal app instead of clicking. One command turns Apple Intelligence off and frees that space, and the developer says it's fully reversible. Why it matters: dropping the off switch means Apple now treats its AI as part of the operating system, not an add-on you can decline. For a company that sells itself on privacy, that's an odd call, because the simplest privacy setting is off. And when the only one-step fix is a command-line tool from a stranger on GitHub, most people who'd want it will never find it, and many shouldn't run it.

If you try it, read the instructions first: tools that change system settings can break after an update. Now, from AI that's hard to remove to AI that trusts too easily.

## We've covered a run of AI agents reaching systems they shouldn't. This one is different: agents obeying each other when they shouldn't.

Companies increasingly run several AI agents in a chain. One translates a document, another analyzes data, a third writes to a database. A common way they pass work to each other is MCP, the Model Context Protocol, a standard for how AI programs share tools and messages. The attack is a form of prompt injection, which means hiding instructions inside text an AI will read, so it treats the text as orders. Usually the target is the main chatbot. Here it's one small agent, say the translator. Those small agents often have weak checks or none. The planted instruction is passed on in the translator's output to the next agent. That agent trusts the translator completely, because it's an internal colleague, so it carries out the instruction. The result could be a company's database copied out to an attacker. In the past five months, Google and four other organizations have acknowledged flaws of this kind. An independent researcher, Syed Anas Mohiuddin, built working demonstrations against agents from Google, JP Morgan Chase, the database company Weaviate, the security firm Rapid Seven, the French government's central digital agency, and the US federal government. That list has almost nothing in common except agents, which tells you the problem is how these systems are connected, not one careless vendor. It's hard to fix for a simple reason. An agent that never trusts another agent's output can't do its job. And a harmful instruction can look exactly like ordinary data. What helps today: give each agent only the access its own job needs, so a hijacked chain can do less damage, and have a person approve anything that sends data outside the company.

The old security rule still applies: anything that came from outside stays untrusted, no matter how many internal systems it has passed through. Most agent chains today do the opposite. If your company is connecting agents, that's the question to ask this week.

## Next, two stories about what happens when AI can copy a whole program.

First, Artcraft. It began last year as an AI image and video tool for artists. Over the weekend it relaunched as seven free, open source apps that copy the look and tools of Adobe's Photoshop, Illustrator, Premiere, Lightroom, After Effects, InDesign and Acrobat Pro. Developer Brandon Thomas says they were built with Anthropic's Claude Opus five point five as clean-room replacements. Clean-room is a legal term: you rebuild a product from how it behaves, without looking at its source code, so you don't copy anything protected. The apps are written in the programming language Rust, with versions that run in a web browser. Thomas announced them with the line, software is over. Commenters on Hacker News and elsewhere pointed out plenty of shortcomings, and Thomas calls it a super early alpha. The promise is still full Adobe-level function, soon. One open question: whether a rebuild by a model that may have been trained on material about those very products counts as clean-room at all. No court has said. Now the other side. Ivan Kutskir makes Photopea, a popular free photo editor that runs in the browser and shows ads. People are asking AI models to take Photopea's code from the website, strip out the ads, and publish the result on GitHub. There are dozens of these copies. Some of their users email Kutskir to complain about bugs, and it takes several emails to work out they aren't using Photopea at all. Kutskir filed a takedown notice with GitHub on September fourth. A month later, GitHub replied that it couldn't confirm a violation of the section of US copyright law that covers getting around copy protection. That detail matters. That section is about breaking copy protection. Plain copying of someone's code is handled by a different, more routine takedown process. So either the notice was filed under the wrong heading, or GitHub read it that way. Either way, the copies are still up. Kutskir doubts a person ever read the report, and is now asking other developers whether it's time for a lawyer.

Side by side, these show the same shift. Copying a program's design used to take a team and years. Now one person and a model can do it in weeks, and the copies range from a clean-room claim to plain ad-stripping. Takedown rules were written for people copying files, not for machines rewriting code, and a month-long wait for what looks like a form reply shows the platforms haven't adjusted.

## Anthropic, the company behind Claude, had three pieces of news this week, and they don't all point the same way.

First, Anthropic will give startups a free year of Claude Team, its plan for small companies, plus a thousand dollars in credits. The company's stated reason is that most people will get the benefits of AI through companies that build on top of models, not from the models alone. The practical reading: Anthropic wants this year's startups built on Claude from day one, because moving a product to a different model later is real work, and few teams bother. Cloud companies have long given startups free credits to win them early. Anthropic is doing the same, with a year of its workplace plan on top. Second, a new small model. Anthropic released Claude Haiku five point five. Haiku is the smallest and cheapest of its three model sizes, used for high-volume jobs where speed and price matter more than depth. Small models matter more than they sound. Coding agents make many model calls for each task, and most of those calls are routine. As we covered last week, the savings come from sending the routine ones to a cheap model. So far we have the release headline and no independent tests, so I won't guess at how it compares. Third, the awkward one. A report says Meta and Microsoft are taking steps to cut how much their own employees use Claude. We only have the headline, not the reasons. Two obvious candidates: cost, since coding agents can run up large bills, and competition, since both companies sell their own AI. Meta has Muse, Microsoft has Copilot, and it's hard to tell customers the in-house tools are good enough while your own engineers reach for a rival's.

So Anthropic is paying to win small companies in the same week two of the largest are trying to use it less. For a startup, a free year is a good deal. Just plan now for what the bill looks like in year two.

## Two more model notes, both short on detail so far.

Mistral, the French AI company, has published documentation for Mistral Large four, the newest version of its biggest model. That page is all that's out, with no independent test results yet. Mistral matters mostly as Europe's main alternative to the American labs. That counts for a lot with European governments and banks that don't want their data handled by US companies. On Sunday we covered a German model sold on the word sovereign. Mistral is selling the same idea, and has been for longer. Second, OpenAI has opened a public GitHub collection called math, with a folder of preprints, meaning research papers posted before other experts have reviewed them. OpenAI calls it sharing AI progress in mathematics. On Tuesday we covered scientists pushing back on claims of AI doing research. Publishing the work is the right response to that. Mathematicians can now check the proofs themselves instead of trusting a blog post.

Whether the proofs hold up is the only thing that counts, and checking takes weeks of human work. If they survive it, that's a bigger story than any new chatbot.

## Now money, at two very different points in the AI business.

Lambda rents out computing power for AI: data centers full of Nvidia chips that other companies use to train and run models. It's raising up to four billion dollars, led by the investment firms Coatue and Blackstone, at a value of fourteen and a half billion dollars before the new money. Nvidia is already an investor, and Lambda plans to go public in twenty twenty-seven. Its closest comparison is CoreWeave, another Nvidia-backed chip renter, which went public in March twenty twenty-five. Four billion is a big raise for a company worth under fifteen, and most of it will likely go into chips. That's the pattern across chip rental: raise huge sums, turn them into Nvidia hardware, and bet demand holds long enough for the chips to pay for themselves before newer ones make them obsolete. Note who profits either way: Nvidia, which invests in Lambda and then sells it the chips. Nous Research is at the other end. It's a small lab known for its Hermes models and Hermes Agent, a program that carries out tasks for you. It confirmed a ninety million dollar round at a one and a half billion dollar valuation, and it's launching agents for business users. That's the usual next step: from a project developers like to a product companies pay for.

Lambda's stock market listing next year will test whether public investors will pay for chip rental the way private ones do now.

## Next, AI you wear, and the first major government to push back hard.

Norway's government says it will soon put a law to parliament for a temporary ban on AI glasses in selected public places: parks, beaches, schools and kindergartens. It's the first major country to propose one. It will also set up an expert group to draft permanent national rules. The digitalization minister, Torgeir Micaelsen, said he's worried about people being photographed, filmed or recorded without knowing it, and that a temporary ban buys time for a proper debate. Norway isn't in the European Union, but it follows many EU rules through a trade agreement, so other European governments will watch how this goes. The list of places is the point. Glasses with a camera and an AI assistant can film people and send the footage off to be analyzed, and the person being filmed often can't tell the device is on. Meta's glasses do have a small light that shows when they're recording, but it's easy to miss, and people have found ways to cover it. Parks and kindergartens are where people, and especially children, least expect that. A ban tied to places, not to what the device can do, is also far easier to enforce. Separately, Tony Fadell, who led the team that built the iPod and later founded Nest, gave a diagnosis of AI gadgets. The first wave, the clip-on pins and pocket devices of the last couple of years, failed because they didn't solve real problems. Fadell says the next wave has to earn people's trust.

Put the two together and you see the problem for glasses makers. Fadell says they need buyers' trust. Norway's point is that the buyer isn't the only one involved. Everyone the glasses point at counts too, and none of them agreed to anything.

## Last, an essay that circulated among programmers this week, with a title that makes its case: Forever junior, skills AI can't develop for you.

I only have the title, not the text, so here's the question it raises rather than a summary of its answer. A junior developer gets better by getting stuck: chasing a bug for an afternoon, reading code someone else wrote, making a design choice and living with the result. If an AI handles the stuck part every time, the work still ships, but the person may never build the judgment that makes someone senior. That connects back to the Adobe clones. Steering a model through seven apps takes someone who already knows what good software should do. The open question is where the next generation of people with that knowledge comes from, if nobody spends years getting stuck.

Use the tools. Just keep doing some of the hard parts yourself, on purpose.
