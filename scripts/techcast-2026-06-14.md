# Who Pulled the Plug on Anthropic's Models, and Why That Should Worry You
*June 14, 2026*

The U.S. government ordered Anthropic to shut down two of its most capable AI models overnight. Not for a specific country. For the entire world. And the reason it happened traces back to a competitor's internal research and a phone call to the White House. That's where we start today. It's Sunday, June 14th, and this is TechCast Daily.

## Let's start with the story that broke Friday evening and is still unraveling.

On Friday night, the U.S. government issued Anthropic an order: block all foreign-national access to Fable 5 and Mythos 5, two of its newest frontier models, effective immediately. The national-security rationale was cited but not explained. Anthropic, to comply on that timeline, did the only practical thing it could — it cut the models off for everyone. Customers, foreign and domestic. Its own employees. All of it, dark. That's the blunt version. Here's the version with the teeth. According to the Wall Street Journal, the directive was triggered in large part by Amazon. Amazon's security researchers ran a series of carefully crafted prompts against Fable 5 and concluded that the model could be coaxed into producing information useful for cyberattacks. Amazon CEO Andy Jassy then took those findings directly to the White House. Shortly after that conversation, the export-control order landed. Now, Amazon is not a neutral party here. Amazon has invested billions in Anthropic through AWS, yes, but Amazon also builds its own competing AI models and sells them on the same cloud platform. So a corporation that competes with Anthropic used its own internal security research to walk findings to the government about a rival, and weeks later that rival's flagship products were switched off by government order. Even if every finding in Amazon's paper is legitimate, that structure should make you uncomfortable. Anthropic pushed back, quietly but clearly. The company said the government, quote, did not provide specific details of its national-security concern. Evidence was delivered verbally, not in writing. And in Anthropic's own assessment, the vulnerabilities were minor and already reachable through other models on the market. So weigh that asymmetry: a complete global product shutdown, based on verbal evidence, for flaws the affected company calls minor and non-unique. Whether Amazon's motives were pure or competitive, whether the government acted on solid intelligence or a competitor's framing, doesn't change the precedent. The state can now reach into an AI company and flip the switch on its products overnight, without showing its work. That's a new kind of power, and it just got used for the first time.

This story is still moving. Amazon hasn't commented. The government hasn't released any written findings. Fable 5 and Mythos 5 are still offline.

## If the Anthropic story is the American government asserting control over AI, what just happened to Meta is the Chinese government doing the same thing, in the opposite direction.

Earlier this year, Meta acquired Manus, the AI agent startup, for around two billion dollars. Manus had made a strong impression with its ability to handle complex, multi-step autonomous tasks — the kind of thing that's become the center of the AI agent race. Now Meta is being forced to unwind that acquisition. Not discouraged from it. Not delayed. Reversed. Beijing ordered it, and Meta is complying. The Chinese government's logic was probably straightforward: Manus was founded by a Chinese team with significant talent and infrastructure in China, and Beijing considers that AI capability strategically sensitive enough that it shouldn't pass into the hands of an American technology company. The specifics of the legal order haven't been made public, but the pattern is what matters. You now have two of the largest technology powers on earth simultaneously treating AI companies and AI models as strategic national assets — not ordinary commercial software, but something closer to weapons or critical infrastructure. The U.S. is blocking foreign access to American AI. China is blocking American ownership of Chinese AI. And the companies caught in between, the startups with international teams, the products with cross-border user bases, are discovering that the borders are real and they're closing fast.

Two billion dollars, committed. A deal, closed. And then a foreign government ordered it reversed. The math on international AI acquisitions just changed permanently.

## OpenAI is navigating its own regulatory front, and this one is coming from inside the country.

A coalition of state attorneys general has opened an investigation into OpenAI. We don't know yet which states are involved, but these multi-state probes are coordinated efforts, not single outliers making noise. The scope is broad: investigators are asking about OpenAI's advertising practices, its handling of health data, and apparently quite a bit more. That range is telling. Ad-policy scrutiny usually means regulators think consumers are being misled about what they're buying or what's being done with their information. Health-data scrutiny hits on something more fundamental: as AI products become genuinely conversational and embedded in people's daily routines, people are sharing sensitive information with them — medical questions, mental health disclosures, personal struggles. How that information is used, whether it ends up in training data, and whether that constitutes a privacy violation under existing health laws is unsettled legal territory. The FTC has been building its OpenAI case for two years. State AGs adding their weight means the legal pressure is coming from multiple directions at once, and they don't all need to win in court to do real damage. Discovery alone, the process of turning over internal documents, can be expensive, embarrassing, and revealing.

Federal export controls on one side, state-level consumer law on the other. The AI regulatory era isn't coming. It's here.

## Here's a story that should land differently now that we've spent the first part of the show on government control of AI. Because the threat to developer tools doesn't always come from Washington or Beijing. Sometimes it comes from a term sheet.

An AI open-source tool that had built real traction in the developer community raised seven-point-three million dollars in seed funding, and then, essentially overnight, archived its repository. No public announcement. No migration guide. No explanation. Just a closed sign on a project that hundreds of developers had integrated into their workflows. This is a pattern, and it's worth naming clearly. A lot of what gets labeled open-source in the AI space is actually a startup's marketing bet. You open the source to drive adoption, get the community building on top of you, use that traction to raise money, and then at some point the investors who just handed you seven million dollars start asking how they're going to get a return. The answer usually involves closing the free thing and selling a paid version, or pivoting to something the open-source community never signed up for. The seven million is a datapoint, not a proof of guilt. We don't know what happened here. But the effect is the same: developers who built on a free, community-labeled project woke up to find it gone. If you're building workflows on open-source AI tools, the due diligence question isn't just, is this project active? It's, who's funding it, and what do they expect to get back? The answer to that question will tell you more about the project's long-term viability than the GitHub star count ever will.

That said, not all open-source AI stories end with an archived repo. There's a more hopeful experiment happening at OpenAI right now.

## OpenAI is expanding Codex, its AI coding agent platform, to open-source projects.

The idea is to point an autonomous coding agent at the open-source ecosystem specifically: reading issues, writing patches, opening pull requests, handling the maintenance backlog that accumulates in every project where the volunteers don't quite outnumber the bug reports. Done well, this could be genuinely valuable. Open-source maintainers are often drowning, and a lot of the backlog is unglamorous work — small documentation fixes, test coverage gaps, dependency updates — the kind of thing human contributors deprioritize because it's not interesting but can't be ignored indefinitely. An agent that handles that tier reliably is a real contribution. The risk, and it's real, is the opposite: a flood of auto-generated pull requests that look plausible on the surface but introduce subtle errors, or simply drown maintainers in review work they didn't ask for. The difference between a helpful agent and an expensive distraction here is quality and selectability. Whether Codex for open source threads that needle depends on how good the underlying agent actually is, and whether projects can tune or opt out of the level of intervention they get.

Now let's get practical for a minute, because there's something circulating this week that directly affects how you're using AI tools day to day.

## There's a well-circulated technical piece this week with a blunt title: don't trust large context windows. It's one of the more practically useful things I've read in a while.

The common assumption is that larger context windows are strictly better. If a model supports a million tokens, you can dump your entire codebase or document library into the prompt and the model will reason over all of it equally well. This piece pushes back on that directly, with evidence. The problem is called lost in the middle. When you fill a large context, models reliably attend well to information at the very start and the very end, and get meaningfully worse at using information buried in the middle of that window. The bigger the context, the more pronounced the effect. You're not getting a billion-token brain that thinks uniformly across everything you gave it. You're getting uneven attention across a long scroll, with the middle getting systematically underweighted. The practical consequence: if you're feeding large documents to a model and trusting it to find the critical detail buried in paragraph forty of sixty, it may quietly miss it without flagging an error. The output will look reasonable. It'll just be wrong in ways that are hard to catch. The fix is old-fashioned curation. Put your most important context at the top or the bottom. Keep what you send lean and relevant. Treat the advertised window as a ceiling, not a recommendation. The skill is still in selection, even when the model claims it can handle everything.

That's going to matter more as context windows keep growing and people assume the problem is solved. It's not.

## Let's talk about the alternative to trusting someone else's infrastructure: running AI on your own hardware.

Two pieces this week add up to a picture of how realistic local AI has become for serious work. Someone posted benchmark results from a home setup that pairs an RTX 5080 with an older RTX 3090. The 3090 is a few generations old, but it carries twenty-four gigabytes of memory, which is what matters for inference. On that combination, they're running Qwen 3.6, a 27-billion-parameter model at full eight-bit precision, and hitting eighty tokens per second. That is genuinely fast. Eighty tokens per second is faster than most people read — it's a real-time conversational speed from a serious model, on hardware sitting under a desk, with no per-query cost and no dependency on a cloud provider whose models can be shut off by the government on a Friday evening. The second piece is more general: a guide to AI-assisted coding at home without blowing your budget. The argument is that the economics have shifted. Consumer GPUs that can run capable models are available new or used at prices a developer can justify. The tooling that connects a local model to an editor has matured. And for a large fraction of day-to-day coding work, the local model is more than sufficient. You're not getting GPT-4 class performance on the hardest tasks. But autocomplete, refactoring, explaining unfamiliar code, writing boilerplate — for that category of work, local is there. The people for whom this math makes sense are cost-sensitive developers who'd rather pay once for hardware than pay continuously per token, and anyone who, given what we talked about earlier in the show, prefers their AI assistant not to be subject to someone else's kill switch.

The irony of the Anthropic story is that it might be the best argument local AI has ever gotten.

## There's a more personal piece making the rounds this week that captures something a lot of people working in software are feeling but not quite saying.

A developer wrote about automating themselves out of their own development work. Not fired. Not replaced by a robot. Just — the actual coding, the implementation-level work they used to spend most of their day doing, is now largely handled by AI tools. What's left is architecture, judgment, prompting, reviewing the output, and catching what the model got subtly wrong. The developer's honest account is that it's more productive. And also genuinely disorienting. Because the things that got automated weren't just tasks. They were the mechanisms by which the developer built understanding, the grind through a hard problem that leaves you knowing the system in a way reviewing generated code doesn't. The optimistic read is that you're freed from the tedious and left with the interesting. The anxious read is that if the implementation can be automated, the leverage a developer provides decreases, and eventually that hits economic value. The piece doesn't resolve it, and that's the point. What makes it worth reading is the refusal to land on a comfortable conclusion. The tools are good now. That's real. What it does to the craft, the identity, and the long-term career arc of the people using them is still genuinely unknown.

I suspect that ambivalence is going to define the next few years of working in software. The tools work, and we're still figuring out what that means for us.

## Quick security note that deserves more than a passing mention.

Arch Linux had a supply chain incident this week. More than fifteen hundred packages were affected by what the Arch team is describing as a malware incident. The good news is they say it's now under control. The worrying part is the scale: fifteen hundred packages is a significant blast radius. Arch is popular with developers and power users precisely because it gives direct access to upstream packages and tends to ship the newest versions of everything. That's also what makes it a target. Supply chain attacks go after the build process or the repository rather than individual machines, which means a single successful compromise can propagate to every user who pulls an update. Full technical details from the Arch team haven't been published yet. If you're running Arch, update, review your installed packages against the advisory when it drops, and watch the security mailing list. No ecosystem is immune to supply chain attacks, and the open, fast-moving nature of Arch makes this a risk worth taking seriously.

This is the third notable supply chain incident in the Linux package ecosystem in the last twelve months. The frequency is worth paying attention to.

## Brief item, but it's almost too on-the-nose to skip.

KPMG, one of the largest auditing and professional services firms in the world, published a report on AI usage. Then pulled it, after parts of the report appeared to contain AI-generated hallucinations. A firm that many organizations hire to audit and validate their AI systems apparently failed to audit its own AI-generated content before publishing. KPMG hasn't disclosed which specific claims were hallucinated or how far the report had circulated before the retraction. The headline writes itself, but the problem underneath it is real. KPMG's reports influence enterprise decision-making on AI adoption, risk management, and compliance. If the hallucinated content reached decision makers before the correction did, those decisions have already been made on false information. The retraction doesn't undo that. The lesson isn't that AI can't be used in research and analysis. It's that the person publishing the output has to verify it. That step got skipped, at a firm whose entire value proposition is rigor.

The irony is almost too convenient. But the failure mode is real and it will happen again.

## And to close out today, something a bit lighter: the new Siri is in developer beta, and someone who turned off Siri years ago is paying attention.

macOS 27 Golden Gate, in developer beta right now, includes a rebuilt Siri that Apple is calling Siri AI. The Verge's reviewer hadn't used Siri in years and had given up on Apple Intelligence entirely. After 24 hours with the new version, the take is cautiously curious rather than enthusiastic — which, after two years of Apple Intelligence that barely worked, might actually be progress. The review is appropriately hedged: the system hadn't finished indexing the test machine's files, it's running on a developer beta, and there's runway for things to change before the fall release. But the direction sounds meaningfully different from the prior iterations. More integrated with the file system, better at multi-step tasks within the Apple ecosystem, more willing to engage with real questions rather than punting to a web search. Whether it delivers on that in the final version is the test. Apple has been embarrassingly behind on AI assistants for two years, during which the gap between Siri and what people were getting from competing products became genuinely hard to defend. macOS 27 is supposed to close that. A reviewer who turned Siri off and is now slightly reconsidering is a weak but meaningful signal. Low bar, maybe — but a bar Apple hasn't been clearing.

We'll revisit when the full release ships. For now, cautiously watching.
