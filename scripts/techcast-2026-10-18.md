# Websites Push Back on AI Agents, and Meta Backs Down
*October 18, 2026*

This is TechCast Daily for Sunday, October eighteenth. Every big AI company is selling an agent that goes out on the web and runs your errands. This week the web started saying no. An airline and a review site are turning agents away, and Meta had to back down from terms that would have let it train its AI on any business's website. Also today: Apple's two-thousand-dollar foldable, a laptop that runs a seventy-billion-parameter model, and a tool that quizzes you before you ship code your AI wrote.

## Start with the agents. You tell the AI to book a table or buy running shoes, and it goes to the websites and does it for you. On Wednesday we covered the pieces being built so agents can shop and pay for you. The new problem is the websites themselves.

The Verge reports that in recent weeks, people using several agents have hit blocks on major websites. The list includes Meta's Muse, xAI's Grok, OpenAI's Dots, and an agent called Instinct. Two names stand out. Yelp told TechCrunch it only lets in AI agents that pay for its data licensing program. Delta Air Lines is stopping agents from booking tickets. Why would a business turn away someone who is ready to buy? Because the agent is not the customer, and the business loses a lot when a robot stands in between. A website earns money from what you see while you are on it: the ads, the seat upgrade, the hotel offer, the credit card pitch at checkout. An agent skips all of it. The site also loses you. It does not learn what you looked at, and next time you may not visit at all. You may just ask your chatbot again. Then there is fraud. A bot that books flights looks a lot like the bots that hoard seats or test stolen card numbers, and a site cannot easily tell them apart. For you, the user, a block means the task does not get done, and you are back to doing it yourself. Yelp's answer is the one to watch. It is not saying no to agents. It is saying pay first. That is the deal publishers fought for over AI training, where the AI only read their pages. Now the same deal is being applied to AI that acts on the page. It makes sense for Yelp: its reviews are its whole business, and an agent that reads them and picks a restaurant for you means you never see Yelp at all.

My take: the agent pitch assumed the web would cooperate for free, and it will not. Expect a split. Big sites will open paid or official doors for agents, and everywhere else agents will be slow and break often. Which brings us to a company that tried to get web content for free by changing its fine print.

## Meta has a contract called its commercial terms. Every business that runs a Facebook or Instagram business account agrees to it. On Thursday, Nieman Lab, Harvard's journalism research site, reported that Meta was rewriting it.

The new terms take effect on October thirtieth. As first written, they would have let Meta train its AI on a business's linked website, not just on the posts it makes on Facebook and Instagram. There was no opt-out. Meta's suggestion was that a business that did not agree could stop using Meta's services. Legal experts quoted by Ars Technica warned it could let Meta avoid paying millions in licensing fees. It would hit every business account, but news publishers most of all. Nearly every newspaper has a Facebook page that links to its website. Publishers hope to charge AI companies real money for ongoing access to their articles. Right now they are fighting OpenAI in court over whether training on their work without a license counts as fair use, the legal rule that allows some copying without permission. Meta's terms would have made that fight irrelevant for Meta. Whichever way the courts ruled, the publisher would already have signed a contract saying yes.

Then, within days, Meta backed down.

## The Verge reports that after the backlash, Meta is changing the terms again.

The sentence that caused the uproar is gone. It said Meta may use any content from a business's domains, web pages, or URLs to develop, improve, research, and train Meta's products. Nieman Lab and James Grimmelmann, a Cornell professor of digital and information law, read it as permission for Meta to scrape those sites, news sites included. Meta has now deleted it. What we do not know yet is whether Meta changed its plans or only its wording. A contract that no longer grants permission is not the same as a crawler that stays away.

My guess at why Meta folded so fast: Meta needs businesses to keep posting and buying ads on its apps, and a contract that scares them off costs more than the content is worth. Put it next to the agent blocks and you see one fight from two sides. Websites want to charge for access. AI companies want access signed away in the fine print. This round went to the websites.

## Now Apple, and a rule change with a large exception.

Apple's updated App Store rules now explicitly ban so-called nudify apps. These are apps that use AI to make intimate images of real people without their consent. The pressure started earlier this year, when people on X used Grok, the chatbot from Elon Musk's xAI, to make sexualized fake images of real people, including minors. At Apple's developer conference in June, protesters called on Apple to remove Grok, X, and all nudify technology from the App Store. In July, San Francisco's city attorney ordered Apple to remove thirteen nudify apps. Apple did not pull X or Grok. It told senators it had threatened to pull Grok.

This matters because for most iPhone owners, the App Store is the only way to install an app, so Apple's rules decide what exists on those phones. The rule clearly covers small apps built only for this. The best-known case, a general-purpose chatbot that did it at huge scale, is still in the store. A ban on nudify apps is good. Until Apple applies it to the big app as well as the small ones, it is weaker than it reads.

## Apple's other big news is its long-rumored foldable. The iPhone Duo is up for preorder.

Closed, it has a five point four inch screen. Open, it has a seven point six inch screen with a matte nano-texture finish and barely any visible crease. There is no Face ID. You unlock it with a fingerprint sensor in the power button. It has two cameras, a main and a wide-angle, where Apple's Pro phones have three. Apple reworked iOS twenty-seven's controls and apps for the larger screen, down to moving the dock to the side. It starts at one thousand nine hundred ninety-nine dollars for two hundred fifty-six gigabytes, and goes up from there. Apple calls preorder demand extraordinary, and new orders are already pushed into twenty twenty-seven. One number to know before buying: The Verge reports that repairing the folding screen costs as much as a new iPhone.

Samsung and Google have sold folding phones for years, and they stayed a niche. Apple's usual move is to arrive late with better software, and that is the bet here. Early demand says it may work. The repair bill is the part I would think about before buying a phone you will open and close a hundred times a day.

## From a two-thousand-dollar phone to a laptop that wants to replace two computers. Ars Technica has reviewed Microsoft's new Surface Laptop Ultra.

It runs on Nvidia's new RTX Spark platform. That is an Arm chip, the kind of chip design used in phones and Macs, paired with Nvidia graphics. Over a week, the reviewer used it to summarize books and movies, make images, and run a seventy-billion-parameter AI model. Parameters are the numbers a model learns, and a model with seventy billion of them is too big for most laptops. It also played big-budget games at a speed and quality the reviewer would have called impossible on an Arm laptop a year ago. Microsoft is aiming squarely at people who would otherwise buy a MacBook Pro. The pitch is one machine for work, games, and local AI, instead of a gaming desktop plus a work laptop. The verdict in the headline: it delivers on most of Microsoft's promises. It arrives in a bad market. PC shipments fell twenty percent from a year earlier in the third quarter, across homes and businesses alike. Memory prices have shot up, a spike the industry calls the RAMpocalypse, and PC makers have been chasing business buyers instead of building things enthusiasts want.

A twenty percent drop means buyers are holding on to old machines, and a laptop has to do something new to change their minds. Local AI and real gaming on one machine is at least new. If Nvidia can make Windows on Arm good for gamers, that will do more for the platform than any Microsoft ad. But if local AI is the reason you are shopping, there is a simple test to run first.

## It comes from a Show HN, a post where developers show what they built, called Llmspeed.

The speed of a local model is quoted in tokens per second. A token is a piece of a word, about three quarters of a word on average. Those numbers mean little until you see them. Llmspeed plays text on screen at the speed a chosen model and machine would produce, so you can feel the difference between eight tokens a second and forty before you pay for either. A rough guide: most adults read about four words a second, which is roughly five tokens. Anything slower than you read feels like waiting. Anything far faster only matters when the model writes long code or documents that you skim rather than read.

It is a simple idea, and for some people it will be the cheapest way to avoid an expensive mistake.

## Now models. DeepSeek, the Chinese lab whose cheap and capable models shook the industry early last year, put out an update yesterday.

It is called DeepSeek version four point one Flash, ten seventeen. Flash is DeepSeek's smaller, faster, cheaper tier, meant for work where speed and price matter more than top quality. The ten seventeen is the release date, October seventeenth, which tells you this is a refresh of an existing model, not a new generation. It is available through DeepSeek's own API, the interface developers call from their code, and through OpenRouter, a service that lets developers reach many companies' models through one account. We will wait for independent tests before saying how it compares. Cheap tiers matter more than their headlines suggest. Many real products run on them, because an app making millions of calls a day cannot afford the top model for each one.

The date in the name is a good habit. A developer building on a model wants to pin an exact version, so that a quiet update does not change how their product behaves overnight. More labs should name models this way.

## Now work, and a look inside an AI company at what AI is doing to its own staff.

Anthropic, the company behind Claude, says its workforce changed significantly over the past year. TechCrunch reports two shifts: the staff is less junior than it was, and people spend more of their time in meetings. Here is my reading. Anthropic's engineers write much of their code with Claude. The tasks that used to go to junior engineers, small fixes, tests, first drafts of a feature, are exactly the ones AI now handles best. What is left for people is deciding what to build, checking what the AI wrote, and agreeing with each other. That last part is what meetings are. So the company needs more people with the judgment to direct and review, and fewer people to type. This is one company, and an unusual one, since it builds the tool it is measuring. But it is the clearest look so far at what a team looks like after AI does much of the writing.

Earlier this month we covered an essay arguing AI could keep programmers junior forever. Anthropic's report points at the other half of that problem. If companies stop hiring juniors, where do the next seniors come from? Today's seniors learned by doing the small work that now goes to the AI.

## One developer has built a small answer to that problem, at least for the person at the keyboard. It is a Show HN called Sphinx.

Sphinx is free and open source. It plugs into the coding agent you already use as a hook, which is a small script that runs automatically at a set moment. Here the moment is when you push code to production, meaning the live product your users see. Every time you do, Sphinx puts up a questionnaire about the code you are shipping, to check that you actually understand how it works. If the AI wrote it and you only skimmed it, you find out before your users do.

Most tools for AI coding try to remove friction. This one adds it on purpose. It also shows how the question has changed. A year ago the worry was whether AI code worked. Now it is whether anyone on the team understands it. I suspect the people who need Sphinx most will be the first to switch it off. But as a habit for anyone who wants to keep learning while the AI types, it is a good idea.

## Now two problems with AI coding tools, both worth a minute if you use them.

First, a post on Hacker News titled OpenCode Hacked says user credentials were leaked. OpenCode is a popular open-source coding agent that runs in your terminal and can connect to many different model providers. We do not yet have a reliable account of what was taken, how many people are affected, or how it happened. If you use OpenCode, the safe move takes ten minutes. Rotate any API keys you gave it. API keys are the secret codes that let software use your accounts at model providers, and rotating means creating new ones and deleting the old ones, so a leaked copy stops working. Then check those accounts for usage you do not recognize, since anyone holding a stolen key can run up charges on your bill.

An agent that holds the keys to your accounts is worth attacking. Treat it that way. The more tools you hand your keys to, the more places they can leak from.

## Second, OpenAI's Codex desktop app.

A bug report describes a problem you would not notice until your disk fills up. Crashpad is the crash-reporting system that Chrome and many desktop apps use. When an app crashes, Crashpad saves a snapshot of the app's memory, called a dump, into a folder of reports waiting to be sent. According to the report, Codex Desktop adds new dumps to that folder continuously. Dumps can be large, so the folder keeps growing.

If you run Codex and your free space has been shrinking for no clear reason, that folder is the first place to look.

## Back to the math thread we keep returning to, this time from the side of trust.

Lean is a programming language that mathematicians use to write proofs a computer can check, step by step. It has become a standard way to check math produced by AI. At its center is the kernel, a small program that has the final word on whether a proof is valid. A bug anywhere else in Lean cannot make a wrong proof pass, as long as the kernel is right. Until now, people trusted the kernel because it was small and many experts had read it. The news is that Lean now ships with verified kernels, meaning checkers that have themselves been proved correct. Proved here means checked by a computer, against a precise description of what the kernel is supposed to do.

Why this matters now: AI models are very good at finding loopholes. If a model turns out thousands of proofs, and one passes only because of a bug in the checker, no human would catch it. A checker that has itself been proved correct closes most of that gap. When machines write the math, the checker is the one piece people have to be able to trust.

## Last, a cleanup on the web that some old pages are going to feel.

Chrome and Edge, which are built on the same engine, are removing XSLT. XSLT is a language from the late nineteen-nineties for turning XML, a format for structured data, into web pages. Browsers have quietly supported it for decades. The main reason for dropping it is security: the code that runs it inside browsers is old and hard to keep safe, and very few pages use it. What breaks is an XML file that relies on the browser to transform it on the fly. The most common case is a styled RSS feed, a news or podcast feed that shows a readable page when you click it, instead of raw code. Without XSLT, visitors will see the raw code. What does not break is XSLT that runs on a server or in a build step, before the page ever reaches the browser. Feed readers and podcast apps are fine too, since they read the XML directly. And a site can add a JavaScript replacement, called a polyfill, that brings the feature back.

If you run a feed with a styled page, do the transformation on your server now, and your readers will never notice the change.
