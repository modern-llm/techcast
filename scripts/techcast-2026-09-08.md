# Three Billion Euros, One Burning Data Center, and a Compiler That Lies
*September 08, 2026*

Mistral just raised three billion euros, which makes Europe's answer to OpenAI officially unignorable. But the more interesting money story today isn't a funding round at all. It's a three point two billion dollar data center on a former coal mine in upstate New York, where a fire department walked into a burning building and couldn't find out what chemicals were inside. Both stories are about the same thing: what happens when capital moves faster than the paperwork.

## Start with the headline number. Mistral, the Paris-based lab, raised three billion euros. That's a lot in absolute terms, and it's an enormous amount for a European AI company, which historically has been a category people talked about politely and then didn't fund.

Here's the context that makes the number make sense. Mistral has spent its short life occupying a specific position: open weight models when the American labs were closing theirs, European data residency when that was becoming a procurement requirement, and a pitch to governments and large enterprises that they can run serious models without routing everything through a handful of US companies. That's not a technical differentiator. It's a jurisdictional one. And jurisdictional differentiators are worth much more in 2026 than they were in 2023, because sovereignty has stopped being a talking point and started being a line item in defense, banking and health procurement contracts across the EU. Now the hard part. Three billion euros sounds like a war chest until you compare it to what it's competing against. The American frontier labs are signing individual compute deals larger than Mistral's entire raise. Training runs at the frontier have gotten more expensive every year, and inference at scale is a permanent cost, not a one-time one. So this money doesn't buy Mistral parity. What it buys is time, and enough compute to stay close enough that the sovereignty argument still has a credible product behind it. A European champion nobody wants to actually use is a subsidy. A European champion whose models are within shouting distance of the best is a real business.

The test I'd apply in twelve months is simple. Not benchmark scores. Revenue from customers who chose Mistral over a cheaper American model and can articulate why. If that number is large, the raise was cheap. If it's small, three billion euros bought a very expensive flag.

## Now the money story with actual smoke in it. In early June, a fire broke out in an unfinished building at the Lake Mariner data center in Somerset, New York. What the firefighters found when they arrived is the story.

According to reporting on the incident, the crew found no working fire alarm, no suppression system, and three dead hydrants. Worse, the safety data sheets, the documents that tell responders which chemicals are on site and what happens when those chemicals burn, were reportedly destroyed in the fire itself. Those are documents a fire department is legally entitled to have. The chief, Steve Matisz, said his crew went in, quote, kind of blind, into heavy black smoke from chemicals they couldn't identify. He also said he wasn't sure what to make of the claim that the sheets had burned up. Now look at who owns this thing, because the ownership structure is the point. The site is a former coal mine on Lake Ontario, and the campus is one of the largest AI buildouts in New York state at three point two billion dollars. A company called TeraWulf owns and operates it, on land it leases from a company owned by TeraWulf's own CEO. A UK company called Fluidstack will actually run the facility. Google holds warrants for a future fourteen percent equity stake and has agreed to guarantee Fluidstack's lease payments. And Anthropic is among the AI companies whose compute demand the site exists to serve. Count the entities. The operator, the landlord who is also the operator's chief executive, the foreign tenant, the guarantor with an option to become an owner, and the customers at the end of the chain who need the electricity. Five layers between the compute and the fire hydrant.

This is how the AI buildout actually works, and it's worth understanding because the structure isn't an accident. Complexity like this exists to distribute risk and obscure who is accountable when something goes wrong. When a rural volunteer fire department can't get a straight answer about what's inside a building on a former coal mine, that's not a paperwork failure. That's the structure functioning as designed.

## Let's talk about whether any of this is producing value, because there's a study making the rounds arguing that the early employment effects of AI look positive, and it deserves better treatment than either side is giving it.

The finding, broadly, is that in the sectors where AI tools have been adopted fastest, employment and wages have not collapsed the way the doom forecasts predicted. In some cases they've gone up. Now, three caveats that matter more than the headline. First, early adoption is not random. The firms that deploy AI tools aggressively tend to be firms that are already growing, already profitable, already hiring. That correlation flatters the technology. Second, aggregate employment is a blunt instrument. A sector can hold total headcount steady while the composition shifts underneath, more senior people, fewer entry-level roles, which is exactly the pattern people worried about and exactly the pattern a headcount number hides. Third, and most important, we are early. The mechanism by which technology destroys jobs has never been fast. It's attrition. You don't fire the junior analysts, you just stop replacing them. That takes years to show up. Set against that, there's a post going around from someone doing actual economics work with Fable five, and it's a useful counterweight to the abstract debate. The interesting part of these accounts is never the output quality in isolation. It's which parts of the job moved. Literature search, data cleaning, replicating someone else's specification, checking whether a result survives a different set of controls, those are the tasks that eat a research economist's week and produce no credit. When a model absorbs them, the economist doesn't get replaced. Their week gets reallocated toward the part that was always the bottleneck, which is asking a good question.

So the honest read on both stories together is this: the current evidence says augmentation, not replacement. But the current evidence is also two or three years old at most, measuring a technology that changes every six months. Believing the good news requires believing this generation is the last one, and nobody believes that.

## Three separate posts today are all circling the same question, and together they're the most practically useful thing on the front page: how do you actually know if a coding agent is any good?

The first is a bake-off. Someone ran ten different combinations of model and harness against the same Three.js task and wrote up what happened. The word harness is doing heavy lifting there, so let me define it: the harness is everything wrapped around the model. The system prompt, which tools it can call, how it reads files, whether it can run the code and see the errors, how many times it's allowed to retry. Same model, different harness, wildly different results. And that's the finding worth taking away. We talk about models as if the model is the product. In agentic coding, the model is maybe half of it. A mid-tier model in a harness that lets it run the code and iterate will routinely beat a stronger model that has to write the whole thing blind. The second post asks a sharper version of the same question: how well do agents actually use testing and verification? This is the crux. An agent that writes code and declares victory is a text generator. An agent that writes a test, runs it, watches it fail, and fixes the code is doing engineering. The gap between those two behaviors is the entire difference between a demo and a tool, and it depends far more on whether the loop exists than on how clever the model is. Which brings us to the third post, a project called Coop that gives coding agents isolated virtual machine environments to run in. This is the unglamorous prerequisite for everything above. If you want an agent to freely run commands, install packages, and execute the code it just wrote, you need to be genuinely relaxed about what happens if it does something stupid. A real VM boundary buys that. Without isolation, every safety concern turns into a restriction on the agent's tools, and every restriction on tools makes the agent worse at verifying its own work.

The through-line: capability is increasingly a property of the environment, not the model. If you're evaluating agents by reading model benchmark cards, you're measuring the wrong half of the system.

## Two security stories today, forty years apart in origin, making the same uncomfortable point about trust.

The first is a demonstration of a trusting-trust attack against an entire Linux distribution. The original idea is Ken Thompson's, from his 1984 Turing Award lecture. It goes like this. You backdoor a compiler so that when it compiles the login program, it inserts a secret password. Obvious, and easy to catch by reading the compiler source. So you add a second trick: you also teach the compiler to recognize when it's compiling a new version of itself, and to reinsert both backdoors into that new compiler. Then you delete the evidence from the source code. Now the source is clean. Every audit passes. And every binary built from that clean source is compromised, forever, with the attack living only in the chain of binaries compiling binaries. Doing this to a whole distribution is the scaled-up version, and the reason it matters is that it attacks the one thing every other security control depends on, which is that the tools you use to inspect a system are themselves honest. The defense is real, though. It's reproducible builds, meaning two independent parties building the same source get byte-for-byte identical output, plus a technique called diverse double-compiling, where you rebuild the compiler using a completely different, unrelated compiler and check the results match. Distributions have been grinding on reproducible builds for over a decade, and this is exactly what that work is for. The second story is more concrete: someone factored the RSA keys of a certificate authority from the 1990s. Factoring means recovering the two secret prime numbers that make up the key, which gives you the private key, which lets you sign certificates as that authority. Nineties key sizes were small enough that modern hardware and modern factoring algorithms make this a weekend project rather than a national-scale effort. The practical question is not whether those certificates are still valid on the public web, because they aren't. It's whether those old root certificates are still sitting in a trust store somewhere on a piece of industrial equipment, or a set-top box, or a payment terminal that shipped in 2004 and never got an update.

Both stories are about the same failure mode. Security decays quietly. A key that was strong in 1997 didn't become weak on any particular day, and nobody sent a notification. The bill just eventually comes due, usually on a device nobody remembers deploying.

## Here's a story that connects directly to everything I've said about data centers so far, and it's going to show up in your bank account. Your next phone is getting more expensive, and the reason is memory.

Apple's next iPhone generation is expected to carry a price increase, and the driver is the cost of memory chips. People have started calling it chipflation, or RAMageddon, and the scale of concern is measurable. According to AlphaSense data cited by The Verge, the terms memory prices and memory shortage showed up in four hundred and seventy-three company earnings transcripts last quarter. What makes this notable isn't a price increase, it's a reversal. Memory has gotten cheaper per gigabyte, year after year, for decades. That steady decline is the reason consumer electronics kept getting more capable without getting proportionally more expensive. It's an assumption baked into every product roadmap in the industry. And it's now running backward, because AI accelerators consume enormous quantities of high-bandwidth memory, and the manufacturers who make it would rather sell to data center buyers at data center margins than to phone makers at phone margins. Your handset is competing for silicon against the buildout we discussed twenty minutes ago, and it's losing. Which makes the second story land harder. A report says smartphone makers largely aren't complying with the EU's repairability requirements, the rules covering spare parts availability, battery longevity and repair documentation. If devices are getting more expensive, the case for repairing the one you have gets stronger, and the manufacturers appear to be treating the obligations as optional. And then there's the pure hacker's version of the same instinct: a writeup on decapitating a MacBook, physically removing the display to run the machine headless. That's someone deciding that a laptop with a broken or unwanted screen is a perfectly good computer rather than electronic waste.

The pattern is hard to miss. Devices are getting pricier, official repair is getting harder despite regulation that was supposed to fix exactly that, and the people who actually keep hardware alive are doing it with a screwdriver and a blog post. Buying more storage in your next phone will cost real money, so think about it before you're standing in the store.

## Two AI infrastructure stories that are genuinely about capability rather than valuations. First, weather.

Google DeepMind published WeatherNext 3, the latest in their line of machine-learning weather models. The reason this line of work keeps mattering is worth restating. Traditional forecasting simulates the physics of the atmosphere on a grid, which is enormously expensive and runs on supercomputers at national weather agencies. The learned approach trains on decades of historical reanalysis data and produces forecasts vastly faster, on far less hardware. The cheapness is the point, not a footnote. When a forecast costs almost nothing to produce, you can run hundreds of them with slightly different starting conditions, which is called ensemble forecasting, and ensembles are how you get honest probabilities instead of a single guess. That's what actually matters for deciding whether to evacuate a coastline or how much power a grid needs to hold in reserve. The second story is deeply unglamorous and I like it more. Speculative decoding, running in vLLM, on AMD GPUs. Speculative decoding is a genuinely elegant trick: a small fast model guesses the next several tokens, then the big model checks all of those guesses in a single pass instead of generating them one at a time. When the guesses are right, you got several tokens for the price of one. When they're wrong, you throw them away. The output is identical to what the big model would have produced alone. It's pure speed, no quality tradeoff. The AMD part is the story. Nearly all of this optimization work has historically landed on NVIDIA hardware first, and often only there. Every serious inference technique that gets working properly on AMD chips weakens the thing that's actually constraining this industry, which is that one company controls the supply of the hardware everyone needs.

One of these is a headline model, the other is a performance patch. The patch is probably worth more to more people, because inference cost is what determines whether AI features ship or get cut.

## Close with three things built by people who are not raising three billion euros, and are arguably having a better time.

Ladybird published its August progress report. Ladybird is a web browser being built from scratch, with its own engine, not a reskin of Chromium. That sounds quixotic until you notice how few independent engines are left. Chromium, WebKit, Gecko, and Gecko's funding depends largely on a deal with Google. When the standards body for the web is effectively whoever ships the dominant engine, a fourth implementation is a structural good, not a hobby. Progress on it is slow by necessity, because the surface area of the modern web is absurd, and monthly reports are how you tell whether slow means moving or means stalled. Second, Emacs Bedrock hit version two. Bedrock is a starter configuration for Emacs, and the philosophy is the interesting part: rather than handing you a thousand-line config that does everything, it gives you a small, readable, heavily commented starting point you're expected to grow yourself. That's a deliberate rejection of the batteries-included distributions. It costs more up front and leaves you understanding your own tools, which for something you'll use for twenty years is the correct trade. And third, a VLDB paper revisiting the Dataflow Model. The original 2015 paper is one of the genuinely important systems papers of the last decade. It made the case that batch and streaming are not two different things, and gave the industry the vocabulary it still uses: event time versus processing time, windows, watermarks for deciding when you've probably seen all the data, and triggers for deciding when to emit a result. That model shaped Apache Beam, Flink, and most of what came after. A ten-year retrospective from the people who wrote it is rare and valuable, because papers almost never come back to say which parts held up and which parts the industry quietly ignored.

None of these three will be in a funding announcement. All three will still exist in ten years, which is more than you can say for most of what gets covered.

So the ledger for today. Three billion euros into Mistral, betting that where a model runs matters as much as how good it is. Three point two billion into a data center on a former coal mine, where the ownership chain is five layers deep and the fire department went in blind. Memory prices going up for the first time in decades, because your phone is now competing with those data centers for chips. And a forty-year-old compiler attack demonstrated at distro scale, reminding everyone that the tools you use to check your systems are themselves systems. Take care of your hardware, keep your trust stores current, and I'll see you tomorrow.
