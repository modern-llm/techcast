# The Compute Economy Meets the Power Grid
*August 05, 2026*

SpaceX now makes more money selling compute than it does selling rides to orbit. That one line from this week's earnings tells you most of what you need to know about 2026: every large pool of capital in tech is quietly turning itself into a compute business. Today we've got the money, the electrical grid that can't carry it, a twenty billion parameter model running on a phone, and a supply chain worm that's back for another round. This is TechCast Daily for August fifth, 2026.

## Start with the number that reframes a company. SpaceX reported quarterly earnings, and the AI segment brought in two point six billion dollars in revenue. That's more than triple what it did a year earlier, and it is now a bigger line than the space business.

The growth came almost entirely from selling compute to other AI companies. SpaceX signed a deal with Anthropic in May and another with Google in June, which puts it head to head with neoclouds like CoreWeave. In its filings ahead of going public, the company said the AI division was the source of most of its value, which is a remarkable thing for a rocket company to write down on paper. But read the second number: that division lost one point five billion dollars this quarter. Slightly less than the same quarter last year, so the losses are narrowing, but they are enormous relative to revenue. That is the entire neocloud business model in one line. You use cheap capital to buy hardware that depreciates on roughly a four year clock, you rent it out at thin or negative margin, and you bet that demand outlives the depreciation schedule. SpaceX can play that game longer than most because it has a balance sheet and a public listing behind it. The pattern extends across the whole Musk portfolio. There's a nice analysis out this week of the last seven years of Tesla earnings calls, and the finding is that Musk now spends roughly half of his talking time on robots and AI, and remarkably little on the business that actually sells cars.

So the repositioning is not subtle and it is not one company. The launch business and the car business are being recast as funding vehicles for a compute business. Which raises the question of who is buying all that compute, and with what.

## AMD gave us the supply side of that answer. Data center revenue more than doubled year over year to six point seven billion dollars, up from five point eight billion just the previous quarter, and up one hundred and seven percent from the same period last year.

Lisa Su told analysts she expects data center revenue to more than double year over year again in 2027, which is an unusually specific commitment to make about a year that hasn't started. AMD is finally a credible second source to Nvidia rather than a rounding error, and that matters for pricing across the entire industry. Now the other half of AMD's report, which nobody is talking about but should. Gaming revenue fell thirty one percent to seven hundred and seventy nine million dollars, blamed on price hikes and component shortages slowing sales of the Xbox, the PlayStation 5, and the Steam Deck. Those two facts are the same fact. The component shortage is the AI boom. Memory and advanced packaging capacity that would go into consoles is going into accelerators instead, because accelerators carry margins that consoles cannot dream of. Gamers are paying an AI tax and mostly don't know it. Alongside that, Anthropic reportedly signed a ten billion dollar deal with an AI cloud startup called Volta, which continues a genuine partnership spree over the last few months. Anthropic is deliberately multi sourcing its compute, which is a hedge against depending on any single vendor, and also a way to lock in capacity years before it physically exists.

Notice how circular this is getting. Chipmakers invest in clouds that buy their chips, model labs sign multi billion dollar contracts with clouds that haven't been built yet. All of these commitments have one shared assumption underneath them, and this week, Texas poked it.

## On August third, Governor Greg Abbott declared a moratorium on all new power grid connections for data centers in Texas. That is less than a year after he called the state the epicenter of AI development.

The order directs the Public Utility Commission of Texas and the grid operator, ERCOT, to run a comprehensive verification and audit of every data center currently moving through the interconnection process. Developers will have to provide much more information about the actual impact of their projects on the grid and on surrounding communities before they get a connection. Context on why Texas specifically: it has cheap land, relatively abundant generation, tax breaks, and lighter regulation, and it's on track to pass Virginia as the largest data center market in the country. It also runs a grid that is electrically islanded from the rest of the United States, so when Texas has a shortfall it cannot simply import its way out. There is a real technical problem buried in here that the audit is well aimed at. Interconnection queues are full of phantom projects. Developers file applications at five sites to preserve optionality on one, which inflates apparent demand and clogs the pipeline for everyone, including the projects that are real. Auditing the queue is the correct engineering response. But the politics are the bigger signal. The moment residential power bills start climbing, data centers become the most legible villain available, and a governor who spent a year courting them will turn around inside a news cycle.

Here's my read. The binding constraint on AI in 2027 is not chips. It is transformers, turbines, and interconnection queues, and none of those respond to capital on an eighteen month timeline. Every compute contract signed this quarter is implicitly a bet on grid capacity that does not exist yet.

## Now the counter argument to all of that spending, and it came from three different directions this week. Start with the most eye catching: a Show HN called Maple Preview, a twenty billion parameter mixture of experts model running at a hundred and twenty tokens per second on an iPhone.

The trick is two compression techniques stacked. First, ternary weights, meaning every parameter is stored as negative one, zero, or positive one, which is about one and a half bits instead of the sixteen you'd use for half precision. Second, mixture of experts, meaning only a small slice of those twenty billion parameters actually activates for any given token. Multiply those together and a model that would need something like forty gigabytes in half precision fits inside phone memory and generates text faster than you can read it. Second data point, same direction: another Show HN demonstrating fine tuning of an eight billion parameter model on a laptop GPU with four gigabytes of memory. Fine tuning was the last piece of the stack that genuinely required renting someone else's hardware if you were an individual. Between low rank adapters, aggressive quantization, and offloading, that requirement is dissolving. Third, DeepSeek V4 Flash running on a single AMD MI300X. That card carries a hundred and ninety two gigabytes of high bandwidth memory, and a strong model that serves entirely from one accelerator changes deployment economics completely. No multi node interconnect, no tensor parallel sharding, no NVLink domain to design around. One card, one process, and your inference cost per token collapses.

The interesting frontier right now is not the top of the scale, it's the bottom. It's the capability floor rising to meet hardware people already own. And every dollar in that compute buildout we just discussed assumes inference stays expensive. Ternary weights on a phone are a fairly loud argument that it won't.

## That capability compression has a governance shadow, and a new report from SaferAI put a number on it. Their finding is that Z dot AI's open weight model, GLM 5.2, is approaching frontier capability while lacking key safety mitigations that the closed labs apply.

The specific concern is a structural one, not a complaint about any single model. Essentially every AI governance regime currently being drafted assumes a small number of labs serving models through APIs. That assumption is what makes the rules enforceable. You can rate limit an API, you can log it, you can audit it, you can shut it off. None of that applies to a file that has already been downloaded a hundred thousand times. And the safety work that closed labs do is largely applied at the end of training, which means it can be removed by anyone with a modest GPU budget and an afternoon. So the gap SaferAI is measuring is real. Where I'd push back on the usual framing is the conclusion people draw from it. Open weights are also the only way outside researchers get to inspect these systems at all. Every mechanistic interpretability result that isn't produced inside a frontier lab depends on open weights existing. The answer is not to stop releasing them, it's that a release should ship with its evaluation results attached, so at minimum the gap is documented rather than discovered later by someone else.

And connect this back to the previous segment. If a twenty billion parameter model runs on a phone, the question of who can access near frontier capability has already been answered. It's everyone with a phone. Policy that hasn't internalized that is writing rules for a world that ended.

## Two research items that belong together, because they're both about the industry measuring the wrong thing. First, a systematic study of benchmark saturation, looking at what actually happens when AI benchmarks plateau.

The core finding is one that anyone who reads model release posts should tattoo somewhere visible. Once the top models cluster within noise of each other on a benchmark, that benchmark stops discriminating between them entirely. Worse, a meaningful share of the remaining headroom at the top of a saturated benchmark is not real capability, it's label error. Some of those last few percentage points of questions are simply wrong, or ambiguously worded, and a model gets them by matching the mistake. Which means a lab announcing a two point improvement on a saturated benchmark may be reporting a measurement artifact rather than progress. The second paper is more specific and, honestly, more useful day to day: why large language models fail at tabular prediction. The explanation is architectural. Language models see a spreadsheet row as a sequence of text tokens. They have no built in notion that a column is a variable with a consistent scale, no monotonic splitting, none of the inductive bias that makes tree based methods so devastating on structured data. Numbers get tokenized in ways that destroy magnitude relationships. So for the single most common data type in enterprise software, tables, gradient boosted trees from a decade ago still beat the technology of the decade, while training in ten seconds on a laptop.

Put those together and you get an uncomfortable picture. The industry's sense of forward motion is calibrated on benchmarks that have stopped resolving differences, while the highest volume real world task quietly favors a boring algorithm. Practical advice: if your data is a table, use the tree. Use the language model for the parts that are actually language.

## Three developer tooling stories that are all, underneath, the same story. Start with the Rust project adopting a formal policy on large language models.

Rust is one of the most process mature open source projects in existence, so when its maintainers write a policy, it's usually because the informal norms already broke. The problem they're responding to is contribution volume: plausible looking pull requests and issue reports that cost a reviewer more time to evaluate than they cost to produce. The approach in a policy like this is disclosure plus accountability. Tell us if a model wrote it, and understand that the human submitting is fully responsible for the code, including being able to explain it. Second, Warp shipped an agent as a command line tool. Warp's original product was a graphical terminal, and that GUI was supposed to be the moat. Shipping the agent as a plain CLI is a concession that agents need to live where the code and the CI live: headless, scriptable, something you can run over SSH inside a container. Third, a piece making the rounds called Eight Myths on Software Engineering and GenAI. The one worth carrying around is that speeding up the typing step does not move the delivery bottleneck, because the bottleneck was never typing. It was review, integration, and establishing that the thing is correct.

So, one theme across all three. Generation became free and verification did not. Rust is rationing review capacity, Warp is putting agents where the tests run, and the myths piece is telling you the constraint moved. Every serious tooling decision this year is really a decision about verification throughput.

## The Apple versus OpenAI trade secrets fight escalated on both fronts this week. Apple filed new claims saying additional former employees may have retained or accessed confidential information, widening the investigation. And OpenAI, unusually, answered in public.

The background: Apple sued last month alleging OpenAI obtained hardware designs as it prepares to launch its own AI focused consumer devices. In a blog post Monday evening, OpenAI called the suit careless, aggressive, and oddly personal, said flatly that it does not have and does not want any of Apple's trade secrets, and accused Apple of making vague accusations and trying to change its narrative. Two things worth understanding here. First, why a trade secrets claim at all. California does not enforce non competes. If you're Apple and a group of hardware engineers walks out the door to a competitor, a trade secret claim is essentially the only legal lever you have left. That makes these suits far more common in California than the underlying facts might suggest, and it means the filing itself is weaker evidence of wrongdoing than it looks. Second, notice what's actually at stake. This is a fight over a consumer hardware market that does not exist yet. Apple is defending a device franchise against a product that has not shipped. That's not paranoia, exactly. It's what you do when your entire business rests on one form factor and someone credible is aiming at it. On the other side, the fact that Apple has widened the claim to additional employees suggests forensic evidence, download logs, device records, rather than a general suspicion.

The oddly personal line is the real tell. OpenAI is fighting this in the press because the legal calendar runs in years and the product calendar runs in months. Whoever ships first shapes how a jury eventually reads all of this.

## Security, and this one needs action from some of you today. The Keyv family of npm packages has been compromised in an active Shai Hulud supply chain attack.

Keyv is a small key value storage abstraction with a set of adapters for different backends. It is exactly the kind of package you depend on without knowing it, pulled in transitively by caching layers and data libraries several levels up your tree. Shai Hulud is the self replicating npm worm we've now seen come around repeatedly. The mechanism is what makes it dangerous. Malicious code runs during install, harvests credentials from the developer machine or the CI environment, and then uses any npm publish tokens it finds to push trojanized versions of other packages that maintainer has rights to. That's what makes it a worm rather than a single compromise. Each victim becomes the next distribution channel. Two consequences worth internalizing. The blast radius follows maintainer permissions, not package popularity, so a small package with a well connected maintainer is a bigger risk than a huge package with a locked down one. And earlier waves exfiltrated stolen secrets to public repositories, meaning anything taken should be treated as already burned. Practically: check your lockfiles for affected versions in the exposure window, install with lifecycle scripts disabled by default, and rotate npm tokens and any cloud credentials that CI had access to. Then check your own account for repositories you did not create.

The uncomfortable part is that this keeps working for the same reason every time. Any holder of a valid token can publish any version and have it on machines worldwide within seconds. Fixing that means adding friction to publishing, and the ecosystem keeps deciding it would rather have the convenience.

## Two deals to close on, and they're the same market viewed from opposite ends. Bending Spoons made its first post IPO acquisition, buying Airtable for one point three billion dollars.

Bending Spoons is the Italian firm that has spent years buying mature software companies, Evernote, Meetup, WeTransfer, Vimeo, AOL, cutting costs hard, adjusting pricing, and running them for cash. They are very good at it and very unsentimental about it. The number is what matters. Airtable was valued around eleven billion dollars at the 2021 peak. This is roughly a ninety percent haircut. The lesson worth taking is that those late stage 2021 valuations were financing terms, not prices. They came bundled with liquidation preferences and ratchets that made the headline number nearly meaningless, and the exit market is now clearing at plain cash flow multiples. There's a product story too: the no code category got compressed from both sides, by general purpose AI agents that can just write the automation, and by vertical software that does one workflow properly. Meanwhile, at the other end, Oxide Computer filed a Form D for four hundred and forty five million dollars. Oxide builds rack scale computers with their own boards, their own hypervisor, and their own firmware, deliberately replacing the commodity server stack. That thesis was a hard sell for years. It is much easier now, when enterprises want AI infrastructure they actually own, public cloud GPU capacity is rationed and expensive, and, as Texas just reminded us, efficiency per rack is a real constraint rather than a spec sheet bullet.

So capital is flowing out of the application layer and into the metal. A no code darling sells for a tenth of its paper value while a hardware company raises nearly half a billion dollars to build servers. Four years ago you would have gotten that exactly backwards.

The through line today is that money and physics are converging on the same bottleneck. SpaceX, AMD, and Anthropic are all pricing compute as though scarcity is permanent. Texas just pointed out that the grid gets a vote. And a twenty billion parameter model running on a phone is the strongest argument yet that some of that scarcity is a software problem, not a physical one. Two things to watch: what the ERCOT audit finds in that interconnection queue, and whether ternary quantization holds up outside a demo video. In the meantime, go rotate your npm tokens.
