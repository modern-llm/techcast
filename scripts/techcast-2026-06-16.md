# Why the Government Really Pulled Mythos, OpenAI's $34 Billion Hole, and the Skill Rot Reckoning
*June 16, 2026*

Over the weekend, a new detail leaked that reframes the entire Anthropic shutdown story, and it has one word in it: China. Meanwhile, OpenAI's losses just multiplied nearly eight-fold, and developers are quietly admitting that coding through an AI all day is making them worse at their jobs. This is TechCast Daily for June 16th. Let's dig in.

## If you were with us Friday, you know the U.S. government forced Anthropic to pull its two newest models, Mythos 5 and Fable 5, offline. Over the weekend we got the inside account of how that went down, and it reads like a corporate thriller.

Here's the timeline. At 5:21 PM on a Friday, Anthropic received a U.S. export-control directive ordering it to suspend access to Mythos 5 and Fable 5 for, quote, any foreign national, inside or outside the United States, including foreign national Anthropic employees. Read that last part again. The order didn't just target customers abroad. It said the company couldn't even let its own non-American staff touch these models. And Anthropic determined there was only one way to actually comply with something that sweeping on a Friday night: completely disable the products. The same products it had spent the entire prior week hyping at launch. So while the rest of the country celebrated the U.S. winning its first World Cup and the Knicks taking a championship, a frontier AI lab spent its weekend in crisis mode, then physically traveled to Washington to try to talk the president out of it. Sit with the operational reality. A government agency, after business hours, issued an order so broad that a company's only path to compliance was to switch off a flagship commercial product entirely. There was no surgical option. It was all or nothing.

The obvious question all weekend was: why? What's so dangerous about these two models? And over the weekend, a far more alarming answer surfaced.

## A new report from Semafor adds the missing piece, and it changes how you read this entire story. The trigger may not have been a jailbreak at all. It may have been China.

According to Semafor, the White House's decision to restrict Mythos was driven in part by fears that a group linked to China had already accessed it. Think about why that's so serious. The real danger isn't a leaked product, it's reverse engineering through a technique called distillation. Here's how distillation works: you take a weaker student model and train it on the outputs of a much stronger teacher model, and it learns to imitate the teacher's behavior. In effect, you can clone a lot of a frontier model's capability without ever seeing its weights, just by querying it enough. So if an adversary had real access to Mythos, they could potentially distill a copy of America's most advanced AI. That reframes the Friday-night panic completely. It wasn't bureaucrats overreacting to a theoretical safety bug. It was, at least in part, a counterintelligence response to a possible breach. Now, the caveats matter. The White House has not confirmed this. And tellingly, when Trump advisor David Sacks posted about the episode on X, he didn't mention China at all. So we have a leaked national-security justification that the administration itself won't say out loud.

And that gap, between the reason that's leaking and the reason officials will state on the record, is exactly what has security experts uneasy.

## Let's connect the dots, because the most useful framing is that the government's stated reason and its real reason may have never matched.

The original public story was a jailbreak, a clever prompt that slips past Anthropic's safety filters to extract dangerous cyber, chemical, or bio information. But the analysis emerging now argues this ban was never really about a jailbreak. Whether the true motive was reactionary, possibly retaliatory, the China access fears, or some blend of all three, the underlying message is the same regardless: the AI industry is not immune from U.S. government interference. That's the durable lesson, and it's bigger than any one model. For years the frontier labs operated as if they were building global products for a global market. This weekend established that a single export-control directive, issued on a Friday evening, can instantly turn off your flagship product worldwide, and can even bar your own employees from using it based on their nationality. If you're building an AI company, your entire deployment can now hinge on a national-security judgment call you don't get to see, can't predict, and may never have explained to you. The state has discovered it has a kill switch, and it has now used it.

Not everyone thinks that kill switch made anyone safer. In fact, the people whose entire job is security are arguing the opposite.

## Here's a wrinkle that cuts against the government's whole rationale. Dozens of cybersecurity veterans, the actual defenders, came out publicly to protest the ban. Their argument: pulling these models makes us less safe, not more.

A group of dozens of cybersecurity experts urged the White House to remove the restrictions on Fable and Mythos, and their reasoning is the inverse of the government's. The administration's worry is that powerful models help attackers write better malware or find exploits. But these defenders point out that the exact same capability is what they rely on to secure their own software, scanning code for vulnerabilities and hardening systems faster than humans can alone. So when you yank the most capable models off the market, you don't just disarm the attackers. You disarm the defenders, and arguably you hurt them more, because serious attackers, especially state-backed ones, have ways to get access regardless. This is the central tension in AI security policy in one snapshot. The same tool that worries you on offense is the one protecting you on defense, and a blunt ban hits both edges of the blade. The people who do this work for a living are telling Washington it picked the wrong tradeoff.

And step back far enough, and this whole saga sends a message that lands hardest not in Washington, but everywhere else in the world.

## For everyone outside the United States, this weekend was a wake-up call. If the U.S. government can switch off the best AI on the planet for foreign users overnight, then depending on American AI is a strategic risk.

Here's the uncomfortable lesson the rest of the world just absorbed. It's not only that the U.S. dominates frontier AI. It's that the U.S. government wields direct power over who's allowed to use it, and it will exercise that power swiftly and with little explanation. Put yourself in the shoes of a company, a hospital, or a government ministry in Europe or Brazil that built workflows on top of Mythos. This weekend, your critical tool vanished because of a decision made in a room you have no access to, for reasons no one will confirm. So what's the rational response? Reduce your dependence. Build or buy AI that isn't subject to an American kill switch. And here's the catch that makes that hard: the most advanced AI chips are American-designed, made in Taiwan, and already governed by U.S. export policy. So the same country that can switch off your model access is also the gatekeeper for the silicon you'd need to build your own alternative. This single incident is the strongest argument anyone has ever made for sovereign, non-American AI, and the irony is that the U.S. made that argument for them.

So that's the politics of frontier AI. Now follow the money, because the economics underneath all of this are getting genuinely wild.

## Let's talk about what it actually costs to play at this level. New figures on OpenAI's finances dropped, and the headline number makes you read it twice. The company's losses increased nearly eight-fold in 2025.

Here are the numbers. OpenAI's losses grew almost eight times over 2025, with total spending hitting around 34 billion dollars in a single year, and losses accelerating, not shrinking. A lot of people will see that and panic. But let's be precise, because the story isn't simply that OpenAI is failing. The spending is the strategy. Training frontier models and serving hundreds of millions of users is staggeringly expensive, the compute, the chips, the data centers, the talent, all front-loaded. OpenAI is deliberately spending enormous sums today betting that scale translates into dominance and revenue tomorrow. The real question isn't whether they're losing money. It's whether the losses are converging toward a business or diverging into a bottomless pit. An eight-fold jump in losses is the signature of a company pressing the accelerator as hard as it physically can, racing to stay at the frontier we were just talking about. And it tells you why every AI player is so desperate for capital. At 34 billion in annual spend, you don't fund this from product revenue. You fund it from the markets, continuously, or you fall behind.

And right on cue, the most important company in this entire ecosystem went to those markets this week to raise an enormous pile of cash.

## Nvidia, the company selling the shovels in this gold rush, just did something it hasn't done in five years. It went to the bond market to borrow a massive amount of money, and the response tells you everything about the appetite for AI right now.

Nvidia planned to sell 25 billion dollars of investment-grade debt, its first bond sale since 2021, and the detail that matters is the demand. It was originally sized at 20 billion, but they upsized it to 25 billion after the order book blew past 85 billion dollars by early afternoon. Let that sink in. Investors lined up to lend Nvidia more than four times what it was originally asking for. The obvious question is why the most profitable company in tech, sitting on enormous cash flow, needs to borrow at all. The answer is that this is what supreme confidence looks like. When you can borrow tens of billions cheaply because investors are desperate to hand you money, you take it, and you use it to fund the AI buildout while keeping your own cash flexible. But there's a second reading. This bond sale is also a thermometer, a direct test of how much exposure to the AI sector investors will take on. An 85-billion-dollar order book is the market shouting that it still wants in. The same money chasing OpenAI's losses is chasing Nvidia's debt, and the same hunger is now pulling a wave of AI startups toward public listings, all trying to ride the current IPO wave before the window closes. The capital wave we've tracked all year is very much still cresting.

From companies raising money, let's turn to one being bought, in a deal that says a lot about where AI value is actually accruing.

## Salesforce just made a big acquisition. It's buying a company called Fin, which you might know by its old name Intercom, for 3.6 billion dollars. And the rebrand itself is the whole story.

Start with that name change, because it's the tell. Intercom built its business on customer-service chat, the little message bubble in the corner of a website. Then it launched an AI support agent called Fin. And at some point the AI agent became so central to the company's value that they renamed the entire company after it. The product ate the company. Now Salesforce is paying 3.6 billion to own it. Why does Salesforce want this? Salesforce is the giant of customer relationship management, the software companies use to manage every customer interaction. AI agents that can actually resolve support issues, not just route tickets, are the next obvious layer on top of that. So this is Salesforce buying its way into agentic customer service rather than building it from scratch. The strategic signal is what matters. The value here isn't the legacy chat widget. It's the autonomous agent that handles support conversations end to end. A company that rebranded itself around its AI just sold for billions, which tells you exactly where incumbents think durable value in business software now lives. Not in the interface. In the agent behind it.

So that's AI creating enormous value at the corporate level. But down at the level of the individual developer, there's a quieter, more unsettling story unfolding.

## Here's a fear a lot of working programmers are admitting out loud right now, and it's worth taking seriously. After months of coding almost entirely through AI agents, developers are noticing their own skills atrophying. One veteran built a tool to fight it.

The developer behind this, a 20-year industry veteran named Djoumé, describes the trap precisely. Coding through an agent is amazing for vibe-coding prototypes in any stack. But the moment he needed to build something reliable and scalable, he found he couldn't effectively guide the agent unless he genuinely understood the technology underneath. And the scary part, in his words, is watching his own technical skills decrease from leaning on AI. He's also worried about the next generation, the AI-native developers who may never build deep technical knowledge in the first place. His response is a tool called Fata, which uses spaced repetition, short daily review sessions, the same memory technique language learners use, applied to programming skills like Rust, React, and architecture. There's a delicious irony in how it's built: generating one course takes about 3,000 LLM calls, with every code sample run through compilation, linting, testing, and review. So he's using AI to manufacture the very drills meant to keep humans from over-relying on AI. But the underlying insight is the real story. We've spent two years asking whether AI can replace developers. The sharper question is whether developers who lean on it too hard quietly lose the expertise that let them supervise it in the first place. This is the fault line some now call vibe coder versus software engineer. A vibe coder describes intent and accepts what the AI generates. A software engineer understands what the system is doing, where it'll break, and how to make deliberate tradeoffs. AI made the first eighty percent of building software dramatically easier. It did not make the last twenty, the reliability and the edge cases, any less important. Skill is a muscle, and the agent is doing your reps.

That anxiety about leaning too hard on cloud models also shows up in a very practical question developers keep asking each other.

## There's a recurring question in developer circles that's really a question about independence. Has anyone actually replaced Claude or GPT with a local model, running on their own machine, as their main everyday coding tool? Not a weekend experiment, the real thing.

The framing is the interesting part. People aren't asking whether a local model can write code. They obviously can. They're asking whether anyone has fully swapped the cloud models for a local one as their primary driver, and crucially, they want real numbers, setups and tokens per second. Given everything we just discussed, the appeal is obvious. A local model can't be switched off by an export-control order on a Friday night. It doesn't send your proprietary code to someone else's servers. It costs nothing per token once you own the hardware. It works on a plane. But the honest reason this is still a question, not a settled practice, is the capability gap. The best cloud models remain meaningfully more capable than what you can run on a laptop, and for daily professional work that usually wins out over independence. Still, watch this space, because the incentives all point the same direction. The better local models get, and the more the cloud models look like something a government can revoke or a company can rate-limit, the more attractive the machine on your own desk becomes. Today it's a niche pursuit. The events of this very weekend are quietly making the case for it.

And if you want a vivid reminder of why human depth still matters, of what happens when you trust AI output without checking it, this next one is almost too perfect.

## KPMG, one of the largest accounting and consulting firms in the world, had to pull one of its own reports. The reason is the punchline of the entire AI era. The report appears to have contained hallucinations.

Here's what happened. KPMG, a firm whose entire brand is rigor and trust, published a report, presumably with AI assistance somewhere in the pipeline, then had to retract it because it apparently contained fabricated information. Hallucinations, the polite term for when a language model confidently invents facts, citations, or figures that simply aren't real. The reason this matters beyond one embarrassing report is the setting. This isn't a student padding an essay. It's a top-tier professional services firm whose product is literally credibility, getting caught shipping AI-generated content that wasn't properly verified. It's a perfect illustration of the failure mode that should worry every organization racing to deploy AI: these models produce output that's fluent, confident, and formatted exactly like the truth, which makes the fabrications incredibly easy to miss precisely when you're moving fast. And it ties a bow on the whole developer thread. Whether you're writing code or an audit report, the AI hands you something that looks finished and authoritative. The human's job, the irreplaceable part, is being expert enough to catch the place where it confidently lied. KPMG just learned that in public.

Let's end on two stories about the models themselves, one about AI getting quietly more capable in your pocket, and one that's just plain fun.

## Apple is making more noise about its Foundation Models, the on-device AI that runs locally on your iPhone and Mac. And it dovetails with everything we've discussed about independence from the cloud.

The significance of Apple's Foundation Models is less about raw benchmark scores and more about where they run. These are models designed to operate on the device in your hand, not in a distant data center. And the appeal of that is exactly the appeal of local models we just talked about, scaled to a billion-plus devices. On-device AI keeps your data private, your photos and messages never have to leave your phone. It works without a connection. And it can't be revoked by a Friday-night government order or a company's rate limit. Apple is making a deliberate, strategic bet that for most everyday AI, good-enough capability running privately and locally beats maximum capability running in someone else's cloud. It's the opposite philosophy from the frontier labs burning 34 billion dollars to train the single most powerful model. Apple isn't trying to win the frontier. It's trying to make competent, private AI ambient on hardware people already own. After everything that happened this weekend, that quieter strategy is looking smarter by the day.

And finally, the story that made me laugh and then made me think.

## From the city of Rio de Janeiro, of all places, comes a delightful headline. The city government's own AI model, called Rio 3.5, reportedly beat Qwen 3.7 on recent benchmarks. Yes, a municipal government has a frontier-ish model, and it's apparently good.

Let's appreciate how strange this is. Qwen is a serious, well-funded model family from Alibaba, one of the most capable open model lines in the world. And a model branded after a city government in Brazil reportedly outscored it on some recent benchmarks. Now, the appropriate caveat: benchmark wins are slippery. A model can be tuned to ace specific tests without being broadly better, so take any single leaderboard result with a grain of salt. But zoom out, because the real story isn't whether Rio 3.5 is genuinely superior. It's that we've reached a point where a city government can field a model competitive with the output of a global tech giant. That's a stunning collapse in the barrier to entry. A few years ago, building anything near the frontier required being OpenAI or Google. Today the techniques, the open base models, and the know-how have diffused so widely that a municipal team can benchmark against Alibaba. And that ties right back to where we started. The U.S. spent this weekend demonstrating its control over frontier AI. But stories like Rio 3.5 are the counter-current. Capability is leaking out, downward and outward, to cities, to smaller players, to everyone. The frontier may have a gatekeeper. The rising floor underneath it does not.

And that tension, between a gatekept frontier and an unstoppable rising floor, is the story of this entire moment in AI.

So here's the thread tying it all together. This weekend the U.S. government proved it can switch off the best AI on earth, possibly because China got too close to it. That single act made the case for sovereign AI, for local models, and for keeping capability on hardware you control, all while OpenAI burns 34 billion dollars chasing the frontier and a city in Brazil quietly out-benchmarks a tech giant. The frontier has a gatekeeper now. But the floor keeps rising, and no one controls that. That's TechCast Daily for June 16th.
