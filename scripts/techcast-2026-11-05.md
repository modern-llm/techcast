# Voters Turn on Data Centers, AMD Closes In on Intel, and Patch Your Phone
*November 05, 2026*

This is TechCast Daily for Thursday, November fifth, 2026. On Tuesday Americans voted, and data centers lost: thirty-eight of forty-four ballot measures to restrict them passed. The tech industry says voters were angry at Trump, not at tech. We'll test that claim, then look at AMD, which may soon pass Intel in revenue for the first time ever, and at a phone attack that only works if you skipped an update.

## Start with Tuesday's elections, because for the AI build-out they matter more than any earnings report this week.

A polling firm called Redbud Consulting tracked every ballot measure about data centers. Forty-four of them, in ten states, sought to restrict data centers. At the last count, thirty-eight passed. Most were moratoriums, meaning a pause on new building for a set time, or outright bans. Only one measure passed that lets a data center go ahead, in a North Dakota city, and even that one adds conditions: developers there must now pay for independent reviews and hold public hearings before they build or expand. Voters also elected candidates who promised to crack down on AI, in the states building the most data center capacity. Now the limits. Analysts say these measures won't touch data centers already being built, or ones already approved through 2027. They hit projects planned for 2028 and later. So nothing stops tomorrow. But data centers take years to plan, so the 2028 projects are the ones being financed right now, and their backers just learned that building on the sites they picked may now be banned. The industry's answer is that this was a vote against President Trump, who has pushed the AI build-out hard, not a vote against tech. That's convenient, and it may be partly true: in a midterm, the party in power usually loses ground. But a vote against a candidate is not the same as a yes on a local ban. Someone reading a ballot measure that says no new data centers in their town is deciding about their town, its water and their power bill, not about Washington. There's a second fight over AI and local control this week, and it involves Google. It faces criticism for a plan that would turn off Google Search for schools that restrict AI. We have only a one-line summary, so we don't know how the plan would work. We do know who objected: New York City's mayor, Zohran Mamdani, and Lina Khan, who used to run the Federal Trade Commission, the agency that enforces competition law. Both accused Google of acting like an unregulated monopoly. Their point: when one company runs the search engine nearly every school depends on, telling schools to allow AI or lose search is the kind of pressure competition law exists to stop.

Put the two together. The industry wants to build fast and sell everywhere. Towns want a say, and on Tuesday they used it. Blaming the president is easier than asking why so many towns voted against a project that brings in tax money.

## Now the companies selling the chips that fill those buildings. On Tuesday we reported that chip stocks had lost three and a half trillion dollars as Intel's slide spread. AMD's results the same day went the other way.

AMD had its best quarter ever: thirteen point four billion dollars in revenue, up forty-five percent on a year ago. Nearly all the growth came from one place. Its data center business, which sells server processors and the chips used to run AI, nearly doubled, to eight and a half billion dollars. AMD credits its EPYC server processors and its new Instinct M I four-fifty chips, which compete with Nvidia's for AI work. Its gaming business went the other way; Ars Technica says it cratered. Chips for laptops and desktops grew seven percent, to just under three billion. The number to remember is the forecast. AMD expects about seventeen billion dollars next quarter. Intel expects fifteen to sixteen billion. If both land there, AMD will take in more revenue than Intel for the first time in the two companies' history. For decades AMD was the smaller rival. What flipped it is the thing behind most of this week's news: data centers buying chips for AI. Qualcomm shows the same split from the other side. It makes the processors and modems in many Android phones, and The Verge says smartphone sales are down around twenty percent from 2025. So on its latest earnings call, Qualcomm made its case for data centers, building on deals with Amazon and Meta announced earlier this year. It forecasts about six hundred and fifty million dollars in data center revenue in the first quarter of 2027, then five billion by the end of 2027, and fifteen billion by 2029. Those are forecasts, not sales. Qualcomm has little history in this market, and it's walking into a fight already crowded with Nvidia, AMD, and the cloud companies' own chips.

So the chip business has split in two. Sell to data centers, and this is your best year ever. Sell to people, for games or phones, and it's one of your worst. That's why Tuesday's votes matter well beyond those towns: the whole industry is now betting on buildings that voters are starting to refuse.

## On Tuesday's show, we heard that Anthropic's own AI use, at customer prices, now costs more than its payroll. Today, two signs that the bills are becoming a problem for buyers and sellers alike.

TechCrunch reports that Apple is cutting what it spends on AI for its engineering teams, and by a lot. We don't have the figures or the report's sources. But the timing stands out. Apple just reported record iPhone revenue. It is not short of money. The likeliest reading is that someone at Apple compared the bill with what it bought and decided it wasn't worth it, at least at this level. Another possibility is that Apple wants its engineers using its own models rather than paying outside companies. The report, as summarized, doesn't say. TechCrunch also reports that Anthropic's compute margins are getting worse, just when they should be improving. Compute margin is how much Anthropic keeps from what customers pay, after paying for the computers that run its models. As a company grows, that usually improves: chips do more work per dollar, and the company runs them more efficiently. If it's going the wrong way, two common causes are customers using far more computing for each dollar they pay, for example in long agent runs on flat-price plans, or Anthropic paying more for chips than it planned. We only have a one-line summary, so we can't say which.

These two stories describe one problem from opposite ends. The buyer thinks AI costs too much. The seller still isn't making enough on each sale. Both can be true at once, and that is a hard thing to explain to investors before a stock market debut.

## Staying with Anthropic, one more item, and this one is new: a way to prove which AI you're actually talking to.

TechCrunch reports that Anthropic is working on a system called FlashProve. It would let anyone check that an AI response really came from the model it claims to come from. Why would you need that? When you use a model through another company's app or service, you trust that company to tell you what is underneath. A reseller could advertise a top model and quietly swap in a cheaper one. A host could run a cut-down version, one shrunk to run faster and cheaper, under the full model's name. This has already been a live complaint with open models: public tests have found the same model giving noticeably worse results depending on which company served it. Today, from the outside, you can't prove what you got. We don't know how FlashProve works. Proving which model wrote an answer, without simply trusting the server that sent it, is hard, and the summary doesn't say how Anthropic does it.

If it works, the biggest winners are businesses buying AI through middlemen. Anthropic gains too: it could catch services that claim to sell Claude and don't.

## Now two acquisitions on the same day, both of small companies building tools for programmers who work with AI.

Meta is buying Conductor. Conductor is a Mac app that lets a programmer run several AI coding agents at once, each on a different part of a project, each in its own separate copy of the code so they don't overwrite each other's work. An agent, here, is an AI that does the work itself, writing and running code, rather than suggesting it. Conductor's app has mostly been used to run other companies' agents, such as Anthropic's Claude Code and OpenAI's Codex. Meta buying it suggests Meta wants a say in how programmers work with agents, not just a model of its own. Conductor's own announcement says it is joining Meta. We don't know the price, or whether the app stays available to everyone. OpenAI, meanwhile, is buying the Pierre Computer Company, according to Pierre's own post on X, which is all we have. Pierre is a small company whose public work has been tools for storing and reviewing code. On Tuesday's show we mentioned a post called Scaling Git at OpenAI. A company that works on that kind of problem being bought by OpenAI is no surprise, but that link is our reading, not something either company has said.

Put them side by side and the direction is plain. The big companies no longer want just the model. They want the tools programmers use around it: the app that runs the agents, the place the code is stored, the review before it ships. Small companies that built those tools on top of other people's models are being bought by the people who make the models.

## Investors, meanwhile, are still paying up for the companies that run other people's models.

Fal, a five-year-old startup, raised one point three billion dollars in a round led by the venture firm Bessemer, and its valuation doubled to sixteen billion. Fal sells inference, meaning it runs AI models for other companies and charges for each use, rather than training its own. It is best known for models that make images, video and audio, which need a lot of computing per request. A company building an app that turns text into video can send each job to Fal instead of renting its own chips and keeping them busy.

The bet is simple: whichever model wins, someone has to run it. That's a safer bet than picking the winning model. Set it next to the Anthropic margin story, though. Running models is exactly the part that's proving expensive, and Fal is betting it can do it cheaper than its customers could.

## Next, a lawsuit that names five AI companies without suing any of them.

The New York Times and other newspaper companies sued Brave Software this week in federal court in New York. Brave makes a web browser and a search engine. The papers make two claims. First, that Brave's browser illegally gets around paywalls, the barriers that make you subscribe before you read. Second, and more important, that Brave sells news content to AI companies. Here is how that second part works. Brave keeps its own search index, a constantly updated copy of a large part of the web. It sells access to that index through an API, a way for one program to request data from another. AI companies use it so their chatbots can look things up as you ask. Brave's own website says the API is used by Cohere, Mistral, Perplexity and You.com, and the suit quotes that line. It also names Anthropic and xAI. The papers say AI companies use the content both to answer users' questions and to train their models. Citing outside estimates, they claim Brave has made more than a hundred million dollars this way, while publishers got nothing. They want Brave to stop getting around paywalls and to end its deals with AI firms. We don't have Brave's answer yet.

Going after the supplier is a smart move. The Times has been suing OpenAI and Microsoft directly since late 2023. Suing one company that sells pages to many AI firms at once is cheaper, and a win cuts off all of them. Any AI company that buys search results should now be asking how many of those pages came from behind a paywall.

## A security story now, with one thing to do today: update your phone.

Researchers said Wednesday that hackers used flaws in iPhones and Android phones to attack people visiting certain websites. They believe, with what they call moderate to high confidence, that the hackers work for Russia's foreign intelligence service. It worked like this. You visited one of possibly hundreds of websites. Code slipped into the page checked what kind of phone you had, then sent an attack built for that phone. The attack tried to steal browser cookies, the small files that keep you logged in to sites, so whoever has them can often get into your accounts without your password. In some cases it also took saved Wi-Fi passwords and all the data on the phone. Many of the sites had links to the war in Ukraine. One was a fake copy of the website for America's two hundred and fiftieth anniversary. The attackers registered a look-alike address that passed along the real site's pages with their own code added, so visitors saw nothing wrong. The detail that matters: every flaw used here had already been fixed. Apple and Google had released the fixes. Phones that had installed them were safe. The attack only worked on people who had put off updating.

So check for an update today. On Android, that also means checking whether your phone maker still sends updates at all. If it doesn't, attacks like this one are the best reason to replace it.

## While you're in your settings, here's one for anyone who uses Gemini.

A post on Hacker News pulled together Google's own help pages on how Gemini treats chat history for personal accounts, including people who pay for Google's AI subscription. If a setting called Keep Activity is on, Google can use your saved chats to train its AI models. Paying doesn't change that. Some chats are read by human reviewers. Google says it disconnects those from your account first. But chats a reviewer has seen are not deleted when you delete your history. Google keeps them for up to three years. Its help page says, in plain words, not to enter confidential information you wouldn't want a reviewer to see. The only ways out are to turn Keep Activity off, or to use temporary chats. Turning it off means Gemini stops keeping your chat history. This is about personal Google accounts. Work and school accounts are covered by separate terms.

Many people assume a paid plan buys privacy. With Gemini, it doesn't. If you use it for anything you'd mind a stranger reading, change the setting today. Chats already reviewed stay on file either way.

## Two open-weight model releases, both headline only. Open-weight means the maker publishes the model's weights, the numbers it learned in training, so anyone can download it and run it on their own machines.

First, the weights for Kimi K three point one Pro are out. Kimi is made by Moonshot AI in Beijing, and its earlier models have been among the strongest anyone can download. We have no test results for this one yet. Second, a model called Ripple sixty-five B Preview. The headline claims it is the best American open-weight model at agentic coding, meaning coding where the AI works through a task on its own. The sixty-five B means sixty-five billion weights. That's small enough to run on one powerful workstation. We don't know who made it. Notice the wording: best American open model, not best open model. That is a quiet admission that the strongest downloadable models right now come from China.

Treat both as claims until independent testers run them.

## To finish, four more Hacker News items where all we have is the headline. Short ones.

First, a post arguing that a widely shared claim, that a system called Astra can read and write a model's weights to improve Qwen, Alibaba's open model, is misleading. Improving a model by editing its weights directly, rather than by training it on more examples, would be a big deal if it worked as described. That is exactly why a rebuttal deserves attention. We don't know which part the author says is misleading. Second, a post titled There is no posttraining data commons. Post-training is the stage after a model first learns from huge amounts of web text. It's where the model is taught to follow instructions, use tools and give useful answers. For the first stage, there are big free collections of web text that anyone can use. That is what a commons means here: a shared pool. The title says there's nothing like it for post-training. If that's right, the data that turns a raw model into a useful assistant sits mostly with the big labs, and that helps explain why open models from small teams struggle to catch up. Third, a company says it is training a Figma-fluent editing model. Figma is the tool most app designers use to draw screens. A model that works in Figma's own format could change a design directly, instead of describing the change for a person to make. We don't know who is behind it or how well it works. And last, an essay titled AI is forcing us to pay off forty years of technical debt. Technical debt means shortcuts taken in old software that make it harder to change later. The title suggests a simple idea: coding agents struggle with messy old code, so companies are finally cleaning it up so the agents can work on it. That would be an odd result: machines getting the cleanup human programmers spent decades asking for.

We'll come back to any of these if more than a headline turns up.
