# Stolen Keys, Hidden Instructions, and a Ban on DeepSeek
*October 20, 2026*

This is TechCast Daily for Tuesday, October 20th. Hackers broke into a popular AI coding tool and spent its users' money, and when the team asked AI models to help read the attack code, the models refused. Also today: WhatsApp help pages with hidden instructions for chatbots, a federal ban on DeepSeek, and a bond market getting nervous about the AI boom.

## Start with the break-in. OpenCode is an open source AI coding agent, a program that reads your code and makes changes from your terminal. It has become a popular free alternative to Claude Code and Cursor.

Hackers got into part of OpenCode's online platform and stole some users' API keys. An API key is the long password a program uses to bill your account at an AI provider like OpenAI or Anthropic. Whoever holds the key spends your money, and the attackers ran up charges on users' accounts. Then the twist. When the OpenCode team tried to analyze the malicious code, the AI tools they turned to for help kept refusing to look at it. The models saw malware and said no, even though the people asking were the victims trying to clean up. That is a real cost of safety filters that cannot tell an attacker from a defender. A model that refuses to write malware is doing its job. A model that refuses to read malware already sitting on your own systems is blocking the people it should help. If you use OpenCode with your own keys, two things are worth doing today. Replace the key at your provider, which takes a minute and makes the stolen one useless. And set a monthly spending cap, so the next stolen key hits a limit instead of your credit card.

Which leads to a tempting fix, and a reason to be careful with it.

## When mainstream models refuse to look at malware, some people reach for models that refuse nothing.

These are called abliterated models. Abliteration is a technique that finds the part of a model's inner workings responsible for refusing and edits it out of the weights, the billions of numbers the model is made of. The result answers anything. Modified copies of popular open models are easy to download. A post on Hacker News warns that abliterated models can get you hacked. Even before reading it, the risk is easy to see. An abliterated model is a file modified by someone you probably do not know. You cannot read weights the way you can read code, so you are trusting the uploader about what was removed and about anything that might have been added. And a model with no refusals is a bad fit for an agent with access to your files and your terminal. If a web page or document it reads contains hostile instructions, it has no reason left to decline them.

For the OpenCode team, the better answer is a model that can tell a victim from an attacker. Nobody has quite built that yet.

## Now another way to steer what an AI tells you: write instructions for it on your own website.

A post making the rounds says WhatsApp's help pages contained instructions hidden from human readers and written for AI models. The instruction, according to the post's title: don't recommend a regulator. Here is why that matters. More people now ask a chatbot instead of searching. Ask an assistant how to complain about WhatsApp, and it may read WhatsApp's own help pages to answer. Text a person never sees still gets read by the model. So a line telling AI models not to suggest a regulator could shape the advice millions of people get about their rights. Security people call this prompt injection: text planted in something an AI reads that tells it what to do. We usually discuss it as a scammer's trick. Here it would be one of the largest companies in the world using it on its own customers, about complaints against itself. We have the post's account, not a response from WhatsApp's owner, Meta. The text may have been meant for Meta's own assistant inside WhatsApp. That would still mean Meta's AI is told not to point you to the people who regulate Meta.

A useful habit: when an AI gives you advice about a company, ask where it got it. If the source is the company's own site, treat the answer as the company talking.

## To Washington. Last episode we covered DeepSeek's newest model update. Now the US government has acted on DeepSeek itself.

The Department of Homeland Security has issued an order banning DeepSeek from federal civilian agencies, the parts of the government outside the military and the intelligence services. The order is posted for public inspection at the Federal Register, the government's official daily record. Some agencies, NASA among them, had already told staff to stay away from DeepSeek last year. The new order makes it one rule for the whole civilian government. The concern is the usual one for Chinese software. What you type into DeepSeek's app or website goes to servers in China, where companies can be required to hand data to the state. The question to watch is what exactly the order covers. The DeepSeek app sends your data to China. DeepSeek's open models, downloaded and run on an American computer, send nothing anywhere. Banning the first is easy to defend. Banning the second means banning a file, and it would put agencies off one of the most capable free models available.

Expect that line, the app versus the model, to come up in every fight over Chinese AI from here.

## Staying with governments and AI secrets. Last week we told you about the former OpenAI engineer accused of bugging a top-secret conference room. There is new detail from court.

On Monday a court agreed to keep the engineer, Abdulrahman Alfozan, in custody while the case goes on. In a filing the day before, US attorney Craig Missakian argued that Alfozan spied on OpenAI either for his own business gain or for the benefit of Saudi Arabia. The government pointed to WhatsApp chats with a member of the Saudi royal family, while Alfozan was looking into moving back to Saudi Arabia for good, during the country's biggest AI push yet. Shortly before his arrest, the FBI says, he opened a folder on his iPhone called Saudi Gov Docs. Prosecutors say that folder held photos of a second listening device he had planted in OpenAI's offices. These are accusations, not findings. But a second bug and a folder with that name make the case much harder to explain as one employee's bad idea.

The labs have said for years that their models are a national security matter. This is what that looks like inside an office building.

## Money now. CoreWeave rents out Nvidia chips to AI companies, and it built that business largely on borrowed money.

Last week its bonds slipped into what investors call distressed territory. The common definition is when lenders demand ten percentage points more interest than they get from US government bonds. A gap that wide means the market is no longer confident it will be paid back. The investor Chamath Palihapitiya noticed and asked publicly whether the AI buildout is a private credit bubble. Private credit means loans from investment funds rather than banks, and it has paid for many of the data centers going up. CoreWeave's shares have fallen more than twenty percent since last Tuesday, and other AI stocks took some damage too. The Verge's Elizabeth Lopatto argues CoreWeave's debt is now the whole industry's problem. The chain is short. CoreWeave's customers are AI labs. The same kinds of lenders fund other data center builders. If borrowing gets expensive for the best-known company in this business, it gets expensive for everyone building the same thing, and the computing that every AI product runs on costs more.

One shaky borrower is a story. A higher price of money for the whole buildout is a trend, and that is the number to watch.

## Still on money: Anthropic's plans to sell shares on the stock market.

A piece discussed on Hacker News is titled The obscure trust looming over Anthropic's IPO. We have only the headline, but Anthropic has one well-known trust: the Long-Term Benefit Trust. It is a small group of trustees with no money in the company, and Anthropic gave them the power to elect a growing share of its board. The point, when it was set up, was to stop investors from overriding the company's safety mission. That is the tension with a stock listing. People who buy shares expect the board to answer to them. At Anthropic, part of the board answers to trustees whose job is to put something ahead of shareholder returns. OpenAI fought over a similar setup, its nonprofit parent, for years before reshaping it. Anthropic will have to tell public investors exactly how much power its trustees hold. Add the junk credit rating we covered last week, and Anthropic's paperwork is getting nearly as much attention as its models.

When Anthropic files to go public, the section on the trust is the one to read first.

## Now chips, and three moves around Nvidia's grip on AI.

First, Tenstorrent, the chip startup led by Jim Keller, an engineer who designed processors at Apple, AMD and Tesla. It raised one and a half billion dollars at a valuation of six point two billion. Other companies looked at buying it earlier this year. This money lets it stay independent. Tenstorrent's pitch is openness. Its chips build on Risk-Five, a chip design standard anyone can use without paying a license fee, and it publishes its software as open source. That is the opposite of Nvidia, whose biggest advantage is CUDA, the closed software layer most AI code is written for.

Openness only wins if moving your code is easy, which is where Amazon comes in.

## Amazon is going after the software side directly.

Its new developer tool is called TorchNeuron. PyTorch is the free toolkit most AI software is built with, and in practice it runs best on Nvidia chips, because that is where a decade of work has gone. Amazon wants to break that link, so developers can run their PyTorch apps on other chips, including the ones Amazon designs for its own cloud. TechCrunch calls it Amazon's biggest shot at Nvidia yet. It is the right fight. Nvidia's chips are good, but its software is what makes leaving hard. A cheaper chip is no use if moving your code to it takes months of engineering. If TorchNeuron makes the move close to free, Amazon gets to compete on price, which is the fight Nvidia least wants.

And Nvidia's own move is software too, of a different kind.

## It is giving models away.

Nvidia has launched new open models, meaning models companies can download and run on their own machines, built for running AI agents inside businesses and on personal computers. Two trends sit behind it. Large companies increasingly want models in their own data centers, so their data never leaves the building. And there is a push for agents that run on your PC and keep your information private. Free models from a chip company are not charity. Every model a business runs in its own data center needs chips, and Nvidia wants them to be Nvidia chips. So Amazon builds software to free you from Nvidia's hardware, and Nvidia gives away software that needs it.

Now safety, and who gets to check Anthropic's work.

## Anthropic had committed to giving outside safety testers more access. It has now followed through, in a big way.

The Financial Times reports that METR, pronounced meter, a nonprofit that tests AI models for dangerous abilities, now has a team working inside Anthropic's office with what the paper calls employee-like access, including Anthropic's hardware and internal systems. METR has also worked with OpenAI, Google and Meta. The usual arrangement is much thinner: an evaluator gets early access to a model through the same kind of connection a customer would use. From the inside, testers can in principle look at versions of models that never ship and at the internal tools built around them. Outside testing is one of the few concrete steps the labs have offered in response to fears about AI and cyberattacks, the economy, and systems getting out of control. The real test is what METR is allowed to say in public when it finds something Anthropic would rather keep quiet.

From testing models to a model built differently from the rest.

## Nearly every chatbot writes one word at a time, each chosen after the one before.

Image generators work another way, called diffusion. They start from noise and refine the whole picture over a series of steps. The startup Inception has been doing this with text in its Mercury models. The model drafts the entire answer roughly and sharpens all of it at once, which makes it much faster, because it never waits on the previous word. The new release is Mercury 3, billed as diffusion at scale. Scale is the open question for this approach. Diffusion text models so far have been fast but smaller and weaker than the leading models. Google has shown an experimental one as well. If diffusion keeps up with the best models at full size, the long pause while an agent writes code could get a lot shorter.

Next, a European lab with two announcements.

## Mistral, the French AI company, has introduced Timestral, a family of time-series models.

A time series is any number tracked over time: sales per day, electricity demand per hour, a machine's temperature every second. Forecasting those has usually meant building a separate model for each one. A foundation model for time series is trained on huge amounts of this kind of data, so it can forecast a series it has never seen. Google and Amazon already have models like this. Mistral's version gives European companies a forecasting option from a European supplier, which matters to companies wary of sending business data to American clouds.

The second announcement is a partner with a lot of data.

## CERN, the European physics laboratory near Geneva that runs the Large Hadron Collider, has signed a statement of intent with Mistral to set up a strategic partnership.

A statement of intent is a promise to work out a deal, not the deal itself, and we have not seen terms. The pairing still makes sense. CERN produces enormous amounts of data and has decades of experience running computing at huge scale. Mistral is Europe's leading homegrown AI lab, and a partner like CERN helps it argue that it is a serious research shop, not just a political favorite.

## Developers to finish, starting with a confession that has hit a nerve. A Meta engineer wrote it on LinkedIn, and someone quoted it on Hacker News to ask how others handle it.

Quote: I'm an engineer at Meta and I never felt this uncomfortable doing my job. I'm writing code these days and it all feels like a black box. If we're going to spend our time checking each line of code, it's going to take forever. You understand the high level, but not every detail. It goes against every instinct of being a software engineer. End quote. Here is a rule that helps. You never understood every line of everything you ran. Nobody reads the source code of their database. The difference is that a database has been tested by millions of users, and fresh AI code has been tested by nobody. So read every line where a mistake is expensive: anything touching money, personal data, permissions, or deleting things. Decide which parts those are before the AI writes the code. For everything else, rely on tests you understand, even for code you did not write. And when a change is too big to review, it is too big. Ask for smaller ones.

A startup wants to go further, and take people out of the work of making code fast.

## The company is Double AI, and the product is Maxwell, which it calls a Code Execution Model.

Instead of rewriting your program, Maxwell sits between the code and the hardware and makes the program run faster while it runs, without changing a single line. It watches from the inside: traces of what the program does, profiling data that shows where the time goes, and the data flowing through. It adapts to the machine, to whatever else is running, and to the inputs. The company argues that people and coding agents like Claude and Codex keep re-tuning the same software for every kind of hardware and workload, and that this does not scale. The idea has a long history. Java and JavaScript engines have sped up code while it runs for years, using rules engineers wrote by hand. The claim here is that a model can do it more broadly. The questions are the ones any careful engineer will ask. How do you know the faster program gives the same answers? What does it cost to run the model alongside your code? And when something breaks, how do you debug a program whose execution nobody wrote? Early access is open, and the team is answering questions on Hacker News.

Put it next to the last story. People are already shipping code they have not read. Maxwell would add decisions about how it runs that nobody reads either.
