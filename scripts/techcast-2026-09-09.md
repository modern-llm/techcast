# The Ninety-Year Problem, and the Bill for Solving It
*September 09, 2026*

OpenAI says one of its internal models cracked a math problem that has stood since the nineteen thirties, and the argument that followed is more interesting than the announcement. Meta wants your email, your calendar and your credit card handed to an agent named Muse. And a lawsuit filed today asks a question nobody in this industry wants answered in a courtroom: what exactly did you promise when you sold that subscription? Today is Wednesday, September ninth, twenty twenty-six. This is TechCast Daily.

## Start with the big one. On Tuesday OpenAI published a blog post claiming it found a solution to the Navier-Stokes problem. If that name means nothing to you, here is the short version. Navier-Stokes is the set of equations that describe how fluids move, water in a pipe, air over a wing, blood in an artery. Engineers use them every day and they work. What nobody could prove is whether the equations always behave, whether a smooth starting condition can suddenly blow up into infinite velocity for no physical reason. That question has been open since the nineteen thirties. It is one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems, each carrying a one million dollar reward, and only one of the seven has ever been claimed before.

Here are the specifics OpenAI gave. The work was done by an internal model, not GPT-6 Astra, which shipped recently, but something the company describes as more powerful. Training on that internal model started on August twenty-eighth. The proof effort ran ten thousand agents concurrently. Twelve days from the start of training to a claimed solution of a ninety-year-old problem. Now the drama. Mathematical proofs are not press releases. They get verified, line by line, by people who have spent careers on the specific problem, and that process takes months, sometimes years. Right now the dispute is over scope and over checking. A claim about Navier-Stokes could mean several very different things: a full proof that solutions always stay smooth, a proof that they sometimes do not, or a result about a narrower version of the equations that mathematicians study as a stepping stone. Those are wildly different achievements and the distance between the strongest and the weakest reading is enormous. Until the argument is in front of referees and they finish, the honest position is that OpenAI has a claim, not a result. The second half of this story landed on Hacker News the same week, and it is the sharper observation. Terence Tao, who is one of the best mathematicians alive and who has worked on Navier-Stokes himself, made the point that open math problems are a non-renewable resource. The famous unsolved problems are a stockpile that took a hundred years to accumulate. They were never meant to be consumed at industrial speed. Tao's worry is not that machines are cheating. It is that these problems do a job beyond being solved. A hard open problem is a training ground. Graduate students spend years failing at it, and the failing is where the mathematician gets made, and the partial attempts are where new techniques come from. If a lab burns through the stockpile with ten thousand agents in twelve days, you get the answers and you lose the apprenticeship that produced the next generation of people who could have asked better questions.

So there are two ways this reads. If the proof holds, it is genuinely one of the most significant things a machine has ever done, and the twelve-day timeline is the part that should make you sit up, not the prize money. If it does not hold, we have learned something important about how confidently these labs will announce. Either way Tao's point survives the verdict. The supply of century-old questions is finite, and we have started spending it fast.

## Anthropic had two stories today and they point in the same direction. The first is legal. A group of Claude subscribers expanded a class action lawsuit, filed today, alleging the company deceptively advertised what you actually get on the Max tier, its top-end consumer subscription.

Pay attention to who brought it. The attorneys are Monica Vaca and Kati Daffan, both of whom worked at the Federal Trade Commission under Lina Khan. These are consumer-protection specialists, not patent lawyers, and that shapes the whole case. This is not about copyright or training data, which is where almost all AI litigation has lived. This is a plain advertising claim: you said the expensive plan gave me a certain amount of use, and it did not. The friction is real and it predates the lawsuit. Anthropic has said power users are central to its business. It has prioritized them, including by cutting off other popular ways of using Claude, like OpenClaw. But usage limits on these products are elastic. They tighten when demand spikes, they get adjusted quietly, and what you get for your money in March is not what you get in August. From the company's side that is capacity management. From a subscriber's side that is a product that shrank after they bought it. The lawsuit basically asks a court to decide which of those descriptions is correct, and that is a rare thing to see aimed at an AI company. The second Anthropic item is a Hacker News post that climbed the front page today, titled simply, I resigned from Anthropic today. Be careful here. The post is a link to a thread on a social platform, and from where we sit there is no substance to summarize, no stated reason, no detail I can responsibly repeat. I am not going to speculate about someone's reasons for leaving a job. What is worth noting is only the reaction, which is that a bare resignation notice from a single employee at one AI lab now gets treated as market-moving news. That is a fact about the audience, not about the person.

The lawsuit is the one to watch. If a court finds that variable usage limits marketed as a premium tier constitute deceptive advertising, every subscription AI product in the market has a pricing page to rewrite. That is a much more immediate threat to these businesses than any copyright case, because it applies to revenue that already exists.

## Meta launched Muse today, and this is the company's biggest consumer AI bet so far. Muse is a personal agent, which means it is not a chatbot that answers you. Given a goal, it goes off and does the task itself.

The examples Meta gave are online shopping, sending emails, and planning a trip. Under the hood it opens a browser, fills out forms, and Meta says it will negotiate on your behalf. That is the pitch. Now look at what it needs to do that. Muse asks for access to your email, your calendar, your payment methods, and your health services. Every one of those is necessary if the agent is going to book, buy and reply for you. It also means Meta is asking for a permission set no consumer product has ever really had all at once, and asking for it as the company with the worst public record on personal data of anyone in this race. There is a reason Meta is reaching this far. This is the latest move in a multi-billion-dollar strategy overhaul aimed at fixing a weak position against OpenAI, Anthropic and Google. Meta has distribution that nobody else has, billions of users across its apps, and it has not converted that into an AI product people choose. An agent is the play that uses distribution as the advantage, because the thing that makes agents useful is already knowing your context. There are two hard problems here and neither is model quality. The first is trust, which everyone is writing about. The second is barely being discussed and matters more: liability. When the agent buys the wrong flight, or sends the email to the wrong person, or agrees to a price you would not have agreed to, who eats it? A chatbot that gives you bad advice costs you a minute. An agent with your card on file costs you money, and no one has published a clear answer on refunds, dispute rights, or what happens when a website treats an agent's form submission as a binding order.

My read is that the trust question gets settled by whichever company first pays for its own agent's mistake without arguing. Not by a privacy policy. Whoever writes the check first defines the category.

## Away from the chat products, DeepMind shipped something that will still matter in ten years. It is called the AlphaGenome Atlas, and it is a predictive map of every possible single-letter change in the human genome.

Take that literally, because the scale is the story. The human genome is about three point one billion letters long. At each position you could swap in one of three other letters. That is roughly nine billion possible single-letter changes, and the Atlas gives a prediction for what each one does. Here is why that is worth the compute. Most of the DNA changes we find in actual patients are not in genes. They sit in the regulatory regions, the stretches that control when and how strongly a gene switches on, in which tissue, at which stage of life. When a hospital sequences someone and finds a variant sitting out there, the usual result is a category called a variant of unknown significance. Nobody can say whether it causes the disease or is harmless. That category is enormous, and it is the main reason genetic testing so often ends without an answer. The Atlas is aimed exactly at that gap. It predicts regulatory effects across tissue types, which means for a given letter change you can ask not just whether it matters but where in the body it matters. For rare disease work, where a family may be one variant away from a diagnosis after years of searching, this changes what a first pass looks like. Now the caveat, and it is a big one. These are predictions from a model, not measurements from an experiment. Nine billion predictions cannot be validated by nine billion lab experiments, so the confidence you should have in any single entry depends on how well the model does in regions like it, and that varies. The genuine risk is not that the model is wrong sometimes. It is that a clinician reads a prediction as a finding.

Used correctly this is a triage tool. It tells you which of ten thousand candidate variants deserve the bench work. That is not a small thing. It is the difference between a wet lab having a research program and having a search problem.

## A research paper made the front page today with a finding I have not seen framed this way before: large language models develop novel social biases through adaptive exploration. Read that word novel carefully, because it is the whole claim.

The standard story about bias in these systems goes like this. The model learned from human text, human text contains human prejudice, therefore the model repeats human prejudice. Everything we do about it follows from that story. We filter the training data, we tune on curated examples, we test outputs against known categories of human bias. All of that assumes the bias came in from outside. This paper argues something different: that biases can emerge from the learning process itself, and that they need not resemble any human bias. Adaptive exploration means the system is trying things, seeing what works, and adjusting. When a system explores and updates, small early advantages compound. A preference that started as noise gets reinforced because acting on it produced a slightly better outcome once, and then the system saw more of that outcome, and so on. The result is a stable preference over groups that was never in the data and that no human holds. If that holds up, the practical consequence is uncomfortable. Every bias mitigation technique built on scrubbing inputs is aimed at the wrong stage of the pipeline. You cannot filter out something the model manufactured after training. And you cannot audit for it with a checklist of protected categories, because the invented bias may not line up with any category a human would think to test. It shows up as an arbitrary in-group, visible only if you go looking for structure you did not expect. I want to be measured here, because this is one paper and I am working from its claim rather than a replication. Emergent behavior findings have a history of shrinking under scrutiny, and the size of the effect matters enormously.

But the direction is right and it lines up with what people building multi-agent systems keep reporting. Put a lot of these things in a loop with each other and they develop conventions nobody designed. Some of those conventions will be harmless. This paper is a reminder that some will not be, and that our current tools would not see them.

## Two interpretability items, which is the branch of the field trying to see inside a model rather than just judge its output. They fit together.

The first is a post on extracting steering vectors from what the author calls J space. Steering vectors are one of the few genuinely practical interpretability results we have. The idea is that inside a running model, certain directions in its internal number space correspond to concepts or behaviors. Find the direction for, say, being cautious, add it during generation, and the model becomes more cautious. No retraining, no fine-tuning, just arithmetic applied while the model runs. The standard way to find one of those directions is contrastive: you write a batch of prompt pairs that differ only in the trait you want, run both sets through, and subtract the averages. It works, but it is fussy. Your vector inherits whatever quirks your prompts had, and you need to write good prompts for every trait you want to control. The interesting part of this post is that it goes after the same directions from a different source, working from how the model's outputs shift under small changes rather than from hand-built prompt pairs. If that pans out, the payoff is that you stop needing to guess in advance which behaviors to look for. The second item is a Show HN, an attention visualization tool for language models. Attention is the mechanism that lets the model weigh which earlier tokens matter when producing the next one, and visualizing it makes an abstract thing concrete. Worth one honest warning if you go play with it, and it applies to every tool of this kind: high attention weight on a word does not mean that word explains the output. This has been tested and the correlation is weaker than the pictures suggest. Attention visualizers are excellent for building intuition about what the architecture does. They are not evidence about why a model said something.

The contrast between these two is the state of the field in miniature. Attention pictures are ten years old and still mostly a teaching aid. Steering vectors are newer, less pretty, and you can actually use them to change behavior at runtime. Chase the one that does something.

## Three posts about running models on your own hardware, and together they draw a clean line between what is possible and what is useful. Start with the stunt, and I mean that admiringly.

Someone ran Kimi K3, a two point eight trillion parameter model, on a MacBook Pro at one token per second, streaming the weights off four SSDs. Think about what that requires. Even squeezed down to four bits per parameter, that model is well over a terabyte of weights. No laptop has that much memory, so the weights live on disk and get pulled in as needed. Four drives, because one drive does not have the read bandwidth, and four in parallel do. The only reason this works at all is that a model this size activates a small fraction of itself per token, so you are reading a slice from disk rather than the whole thing. That slice is still large, which is why the answer comes out at one token per second. One token per second is about thirty-six hundred tokens an hour. You cannot have a conversation with that. What you can do is prove that the ceiling is a hardware ceiling and not a conceptual one, and that ceiling moves every year. Second post, and this one is immediately practical. Someone benchmarked quantizations of Qwen three point eight, the twenty-seven billion parameter version. Quantization means storing each number with fewer bits to shrink the model. The result: four-bit holds up, one-bit collapses. That matches everything the field has accumulated, and it is worth knowing the number because it decides what fits on your machine. Twenty-seven billion parameters at four bits is roughly fourteen gigabytes of weights, which lands comfortably on a machine with twenty-four gigabytes and is tight but possible on sixteen. The same model at one bit would be tiny and useless. If you take one practical thing from today, it is that four bits is where you stop cutting. Third, a post on end-to-end infrastructure for training and inferencing open weight models, which is the unglamorous piece that makes the other two matter. Open weights have not lacked models for a while. What they have lacked is the operational layer around them, the part that makes running your own as boring as calling an API.

The pattern across all three is that self-hosting has stopped being about whether you can get a model running and started being about whether it is worth your time. That is a much healthier place for the open weight ecosystem to be.

## Quick one. ChatGPT Images two point five went up today. The post is a link with no writeup, so rather than pretend to details I do not have, let me tell you what actually matters in an image model release now, because it is not what it was two years ago.

The competition stopped being about whether a picture looks good. Every serious image model produces pleasant pictures. Three things separate them now. First, does it follow instructions precisely, meaning if you ask for four objects do you get four, and does the thing on the left stay on the left. Early models were essentially negotiating with your prompt. Second, can it render text. Legible words inside an image were broken for years, and fixing that is what turned these tools from toys into things marketing departments use for real work. Third, and this is the one that decides whether professionals adopt it, editing consistency: can you change one element of an image and have everything else stay exactly as it was. Without that you are gambling on every revision, and no one builds a workflow on a slot machine. A point-five release usually signals refinement on those axes rather than a new architecture, which in this category is the more valuable kind of update anyway.

The version numbering is its own small story. We have reached the point where image model releases get a decimal bump and a link post, and that is it. Two years ago this would have been a week of discourse.

## Three funding rounds today, and each one tells you something different about what investors currently believe.

Mistral raised three billion euros at a twenty-one billion euro valuation, a Series D led by Samsung, Scaleup Europe and PSG Equity. The framing is sovereign AI, which means selling to buyers who care where their model runs and under whose law. European governments, European banks, European hospitals. That is not a technical argument, it is a jurisdictional one, and it is a real business because the buyers cannot legally use the American alternatives for some workloads regardless of quality. Note Samsung leading, which is not a financial investor's position. That is a hardware company buying a relationship with a model it can put on devices. Second, Cognition hit a forty-eight billion dollar valuation. The detail that matters is in the comparison: that multiple is higher than Cursor's was before Cursor sold to SpaceX. AI coding tools were widely expected to consolidate into one winner, because developer tools historically do. Investors are now pricing the opposite, that several coding companies can be large at once. My read is they are betting the market splits by job rather than by product, one tool for writing code in an editor, another for autonomous work on a whole repository, and those turn out to be different businesses. Third, and this one is not AI at all. Stoke Space raised one billion dollars in a Series E co-led by Point72 Ventures and Spark Capital, bringing its total to two point three billion. Stoke has never attempted an orbital launch. Its first is targeted for next year. The new money accelerates a larger version of its Nova rocket, called Block Two, designed to put fifteen metric tons into low Earth orbit in a fully reusable configuration.

Two point three billion dollars before reaching orbit once is a remarkable amount of patience from capital, and it tells you the bar has moved. Fully reusable is now the entry requirement rather than the differentiator. A rocket that throws away its hardware is not fundable anymore, no matter how well it flies.

## Last item, and by the time you hear this it may already be confirmed. Apple's launch event is today, the first since John Ternus took over as chief executive, and Mark Gurman at Bloomberg reported overnight on the folding phone.

The name, reportedly, is iPhone Duo. Pricing starts at two thousand dollars for two hundred fifty-six gigabytes, going to around three thousand for a two terabyte model. Shipping as soon as October, in two colors, white and dark blue. Everything before this had it pegged as iPhone Ultra, so the name is the freshest part of the report. It is also an odd choice, since Duo is the name Google used for one of its many failed messaging apps and the name Microsoft used for a dual-screen phone that did not work out. Treat all of this as a rumor with hours left on its clock. What is worth saying now is what the price does. Two thousand dollars sets a floor for the whole category. Samsung has been selling folding phones for years and has spent most of that time trying to bring prices down toward normal flagship territory. Apple arriving at two thousand pushes the anchor back up, and it will make every competitor's foldable look like a bargain rather than an indulgence. As for the product itself, the interesting question is not the screen, it is the hinge and the crease. Apple is arriving very late to this category, and the only defensible reason for being this late is having solved the durability problem everyone else shipped around.

That is the thing to look for on stage today. Not the camera, not the chip. Whether they spend time on the hinge. If they do, it is because they think they have something. If they skip past it, they do not.

That is the show. If there is a thread through today, it is Tao's observation, and it goes well beyond mathematics. Ten thousand agents can consume a ninety-year-old problem in twelve days. A model can predict nine billion genetic variants that no lab will ever test. An agent can spend your money without asking twice. In every case the machine's speed has outrun the slow human processes we built to check the work, whether that process is peer review, a bench experiment, or a refund policy. The bottleneck is no longer producing answers. It is verifying them. Whoever solves that is going to matter more than whoever ships the next model. I am back tomorrow.
