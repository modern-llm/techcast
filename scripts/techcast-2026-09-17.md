# The House Keys, the Off Switch, and the Charcoal Library
*September 17, 2026*

Google just handed your door locks, cameras and thermostat to any AI agent that speaks the right protocol, including ones Google did not build. That is either the end of smart home lock-in or the beginning of a very strange new category of bug. Also today: Apple reverses a fifteen-year retreat from the server business, a four billion parameter model out-plans Postgres, and a two thousand year old library gets closer to readable. It is Thursday, September seventeenth, twenty twenty-six. This is TechCast Daily.

## Google Home is opening up to outside AI agents. The plumbing is something called the Model Context Protocol, or MCP, which is just a standard way for an AI model to discover and use tools it did not ship with. Google has built one for Home, and it is in early access now.

Here is what that means in practice. An agent that speaks MCP — Claude, ChatGPT, Open Claw, Hermes, Google's own Antigravity — can now see every device in your Google Home setup, read your event history, pull camera summaries, and act on your behalf. Not read-only. Act. Lock the door, set the thermostat, run a scene. You talk to it in plain language and it works out which of your forty devices that maps to. Google says any MCP client can connect, and that is the part worth noticing. This is Google letting a competitor's assistant drive Google's own hardware.

Two things follow. First, the failure mode changes shape. A chatbot that gets a fact wrong wastes your afternoon. An agent wired to your locks and your cameras that gets a fact wrong is a different category of problem, and nobody has a good story yet for what happens when a model misreads an ambiguous instruction at two in the morning. Second, this is a real crack in platform lock-in. For fifteen years the smart home strategy was: pick our assistant, buy our ecosystem, stay forever. A protocol that anyone can implement dissolves that. Google is betting it wins by being the devices rather than by being the voice.

## Three Apple stories today that are really one story. Apple is reportedly building a server again, and macOS 27 just made its AI impossible to remove.

The server first. According to The Information, Apple is designing an enterprise machine packing either two or four of its future M8 Ultra chips, possibly with Nvidia involved somewhere, targeted at 2029. Apple has not sold a server since it killed the Xserve in 2011. What changed is that Mac minis and Mac Studios started selling out to AI developers, who buy them for one reason: unified memory. On an Ultra chip the processor and the graphics side share one large pool of memory, so a big model fits in it without the expensive shuffling a discrete graphics card needs, and it draws very little power doing it. Apple watched people rack-mounting Mac minis and concluded it could just sell that directly. Meanwhile macOS 27 Golden Gate shipped, and the Ars Technica review puts it bluntly: the toggle that let you switch Apple Intelligence off and delete its models from your disk is gone. It is the operating system now. The review also says the rest of the release is genuinely good, fixing the design complaints from Tahoe and adding a pile of fit and finish.

The server is three years out, which in this industry is a forecast, not a plan. The signal is not the product, it is the demand behind it: enough people want memory-rich, low-power inference boxes that Apple is reversing a fifteen-year retreat. And deleting the off switch tells you Apple has stopped hedging. Apple Intelligence is not a feature you opt into anymore. It is the price of the operating system.

## Three pieces of research landed this week that look unrelated and are not. DeepSeek shipped v4.1 Flash, built around aggressive KV cache compression. A second paper claims to break the one-point-five-eight-bit barrier for ternary models. And a third proposes measuring local AI by intelligence per watt.

Start with the KV cache, because it is the least understood bottleneck in serving models. As a model reads your conversation it keeps a running set of notes on every word it has already processed, so it does not have to re-read everything from scratch each time it produces the next word. That pile of notes is the KV cache. It grows with the length of the conversation, it lives in expensive fast memory, and it, not the model weights, is usually what limits how many users one graphics card can serve at once. Compress it well and long conversations get cheap. That is the entire pitch of Flash. The ternary work attacks cost from the other end. A normal model stores each weight as sixteen bits. Ternary models allow only three values — minus one, zero, and plus one — which works out to about one-point-five-eight bits per weight, and that number has been treated as the floor. This paper goes under it. And intelligence per watt is the benchmark that ties the other two together: not what is the best score, but what is the best score per unit of energy on hardware you already own.

The frontier used to be a capability race. Increasingly it is a cost-per-answer race, and that is the more consequential one. Every factor of two in efficiency moves a whole class of models from a data center onto a laptop, and a model on your laptop has different politics than a model behind an API. Nobody meters it, nobody logs it, and nobody can take it away.

## A study called HarnessTax asks a question that should have been asked much louder a year ago: when a coding agent performs well, how much of that is the model, and how much is the harness around it?

The harness is everything wrapped around the model — the loop that decides when to call a tool, how the file tree gets shown, how error messages get fed back, how much of the conversation is carried forward, when to stop and ask. The finding is that if you hold the model completely fixed and swap the harness, coding benchmark results move substantially. Same weights, different scaffolding, different score. Which means a lot of what gets published as model versus model is really product versus product. And there is a related move this week: Anthropic folded Claude Cowork, its longer-running work tool, into the main Claude app. One surface now, chat and agent together.

Those two fit together. If the harness is doing this much of the work, then the harness is the product, and everyone is going to stop shipping a chat box and an agent runner as separate things. Anthropic just did. The practical takeaway if you are picking tools: scores in a vendor's blog post are only comparable when the harness is held fixed, and it is almost never held fixed. Run the thing on your own repository. That is the only number that transfers.

## A thoughtful post made the front page about learning to program when a model will write the code for you.

The worry is straightforward. Learning to code has always run on productive struggle. You write something broken, you stare at it, and while you are staring you build a mental model of how the machine actually works. If a model hands you working code in four seconds, you never stare, and you never build that model. The counter-argument is equally real, though. The struggle was never sacred. A lot of it was tooling friction, environment setup and unreadable compiler errors, and it filtered out plenty of people who would have been excellent at the actual job.

My read is that the hard part moved rather than vanished. Producing code is close to free now. Reading code you did not write, deciding whether it is correct, and spotting the moment a confident answer is quietly wrong — that is the whole job now, and it is harder than it used to be, because you are reviewing a persuasive stranger's work all day long. Which flips the advice for beginners. Type it yourself at the start, while you are building the mental model. Lean on the model later, once you can catch it lying. Most people are doing exactly the reverse.

## Now something narrow and genuinely impressive: a four billion parameter model trained to write database query plans, reportedly producing plans that run eighty-one percent faster than the ones Postgres picks.

Context on what a query planner does. You write a query describing what data you want. The database then decides how to actually get it — which index to use, what order to join the tables in, whether to sort or hash. There can be thousands of valid ways to execute the same query, and the fastest one can be hundreds of times quicker than a mediocre one. Postgres chooses using a cost model built on statistics about your data, and those statistics are frequently stale or simply wrong, especially when two columns are correlated in ways the statistics do not capture. That has been a known weakness for decades. So a small model trained against real measured execution times, rather than against estimates, is aimed at exactly the right target.

The reason this works is worth generalizing. The task is narrow, the output is verifiable — you can just run the plan and time it — and the reward signal is free. That is the sweet spot for small models, and it is the precise opposite of a chatbot, where nobody can score the answer. Expect more of this: models disappearing into infrastructure as a better rule of thumb, where the user never learns there is a model in there at all.

## Nvidia announced native GPU programming in Rust.

Until now, writing code that runs on an Nvidia graphics card has meant CUDA, and CUDA has meant C and C plus plus. Rust users had community wrappers of varying quality and no guarantee any of it would keep working after the next release. This makes Rust a first-class way to write kernels, which are the small programs that run on thousands of GPU cores simultaneously. The practical appeal is that Rust checks memory safety when you compile, and GPU code is a particularly nasty place for memory bugs, because they often do not crash. They produce silently wrong numbers, and you discover three weeks later that an expensive training run was subtly garbage the whole time.

Read the strategy underneath, though. Nvidia's real moat has never been the silicon. It is CUDA and the fifteen years of software piled on top of it. Every language it absorbs is another community of developers who now never have to seriously evaluate an alternative. This is a genuine gift to Rust programmers, and it is also the moat getting wider.

## Two items about measuring models rather than building them. Someone published a tracker showing release age and training cutoff for twenty models, and separately, an evaluation asks how good frontier models really are at physics.

The staleness tracker is the kind of simple thing that should already exist. Every model has two dates that matter and almost nobody surfaces either one clearly: when it was released, and when its knowledge of the world stops. Those can sit a year apart, and that gap is precisely where the confident wrong answers live. A model will cheerfully describe the interface of a library that was rewritten eighteen months ago, with no hint to you that it is describing the past. The physics evaluation is useful for a different reason. Physics separates two abilities that most benchmarks blur together. Recalling that a formula exists is retrieval. Setting up a problem, choosing what can be safely ignored, and carrying twelve steps of reasoning without drifting is something else entirely. Physics problems are long, checkable, and completely unforgiving of one wrong sign.

Both are corrections to the same bad habit, which is treating a model as a single number. It is not one number. It is a capability profile with a date stamped on it, and the date stamp is the part every vendor would rather you not look at.

## A research post called Dream-RSI proposes recursive self-improvement through evolving worlds.

The setup is this. Instead of training an agent on a fixed set of tasks, you generate the environments themselves, and the environments get harder as the agent gets better. The agent and its curriculum evolve together. The motivation is the data wall. We are running short of high quality human-written text, and self-play was the escape hatch that worked spectacularly for board games. But a board game hands you a score for free. The open world does not. Generating the worlds is an attempt to manufacture that missing scoring function.

Worth watching, worth doubting. The classic failure here is that you end up with a curriculum the agent becomes brilliant at and that transfers to nothing, because the generator learned to produce problems that are hard in a way the agent finds tractable rather than hard in the way reality is hard. So the claim to check is not whether the agent improves on the worlds it was given. It will. The question is whether it improves at anything nobody handed it.

## Two governance stories that belong together. A widely read post warns against taking model welfare seriously, and separately, Anthropic and OpenAI both want independent safety evaluators embedded inside their own labs.

Model welfare is the position that these systems might one day deserve moral consideration, and that we should hedge now in case they do. The warning makes a structural argument rather than a philosophical one. It says the question of whether models can suffer matters less than what the belief gets used for. A company that has told the public its model might have interests has handed itself a ready-made reason not to shut it down, not to modify it, and not to let outsiders poke at it. Concern for the system quietly becomes a shield for the system's owner. On the evaluator proposal, researchers are genuinely enthusiastic about the access on offer. Watching a frontier model during training, rather than after release, is something outsiders have never had.

But independence is not a job title, it is a structure. If the lab grants the access, funds the role, writes the confidentiality agreement and can end all three, then the evaluator's leverage on the day it matters most is close to zero — and the day it matters is always the day the finding is expensive. This is a real improvement over nothing. It is not oversight. Oversight means somebody who can publish over an objection, and nobody has proposed that yet.

## Mistral and Mozilla are partnering on private, multilingual AI browsing.

Two organizations with perfectly matching problems. Mozilla has a browser with real users and roughly ninety percent of its revenue coming from a Google search deal it cannot count on forever. Mistral is Europe's frontier lab: strong models, almost no distribution. And the browser is where AI assistance is landing next — summarize this page, fill this form, compare these tabs — so if you do not own that surface, somebody else's model is reading over your user's shoulder. The multilingual part is not marketing filler either. Most assistants are quietly English-first and degrade into stilted, slightly wrong output in everything else, which is to say in most of the world.

The word to press on is private. That covers two very different arrangements: a model running on your own machine that physically cannot leak anything, or a model on somebody's server with a promise attached. Only the first one survives a change of management. Both of these organizations have earned enough goodwill to be believed and more than enough incentive to be checked.

## Closing with the most unusual result of the week: new work in the journal PLoS ONE on the Herculaneum scrolls.

The background, briefly. In 79 AD Vesuvius buried Herculaneum, and with it a villa holding several hundred papyrus scrolls, believed to be the working library of an Epicurean philosopher named Philodemus. The eruption did not burn them to ash. It carbonized them. They survived as lumps of charcoal, and every attempt to physically unroll one destroys it. The Vesuvius Challenge has been X-ray scanning those lumps and using machine learning to find the traces of ink and flatten the layers back into readable pages, and it works. Real Greek text has come out. The new paper solves an adjacent problem. Researchers made their own papyrus, wrote on it, and charred it the same way the volcano did, in order to validate a screening method that identifies which scrolls used lead-based ink. Lead shows up sharply in a scan. Carbon ink barely shows up at all.

That is triage, and triage is the actual bottleneck. Beam time at a particle accelerator is scarce and the computing is not cheap, so knowing which lumps deserve the spend multiplies everything downstream. And here is the thing worth sitting with. The only complete library to survive from the ancient world has been sitting unreadable for two hundred and fifty years since it was dug out of the mud. We are probably going to read it. That is what this technology is for on its best days.

That is the show. The thread running through most of today: the interesting work has moved away from making models smarter and toward making them cheaper, narrower, and closer to the thing they are acting on — your database, your browser, your front door. Raw capability is not the binding constraint anymore. Judgment about where to point it is. Back tomorrow.
