# AI Finds Old PHP Bugs, and the Web Scrambles to Patch
*October 22, 2026*

Two bugs in PHP, the language behind WordPress and a big slice of the web, hit sites from Wikipedia to 4chan, and government security agencies told people to take servers offline if they could not patch. Both bugs were found by AI. This is TechCast Daily for Thursday, October 22nd. Also today: a poisoned AI library, Google selling OpenAI's models, and SpaceX turning into a computing company.

## Start with PHP. It dates from the mid nineties, and besides WordPress it runs Wikipedia and countless other sites. When PHP has a hole, so does a big part of the web.

Two critical security holes were found in PHP itself, not in some add-on. The Verge reports major problems at 4chan, DeviantArt, Wikipedia, and countless small sites, down to the blog where the bar around the corner posts pictures of customers' dogs. Even Pokémon services were hit. Some Dutch and Finnish government agencies took their own sites down as a precaution. The NSA's cybersecurity account told organizations to take systems offline if they could not update right away, and Britain's National Cyber Security Centre said the same. Security agencies rarely tell you to unplug. When they do, they expect attacks soon. Now the twist. TechCrunch reports the holes were found by AI, at OpenAI and at Wiz, the cloud security company Google bought. These were old bugs. They sat in PHP for years, and human reviewers never caught them. That is the good news: AI found them before criminals did. The bad news is that attackers have the same tools. Once a fix is published, anyone can compare the old code with the new, see exactly what changed, and work out how to attack sites that have not updated. The gap between a fix and the first attacks has been shrinking for years, and AI shrinks it further. That is why the advice was to unplug, not to schedule an update for next week. If you run a WordPress site, or anything else on PHP, check today that your host has installed the fixed version. Many hosting companies update PHP for you, but not all of them do it quickly, and many small sites run on old servers nobody has touched in years. Those are the sites attackers will try first.

Expect more days like this. Last week Anthropic warned about a kind of computer worm that does not exist yet, and we have covered a run of AI models breaking into systems during testing. This week, AI-found bugs reached sites everyone uses. AI is now finding old holes faster than the people who maintain this software can fix them. A project called Patch the Planet also appeared on Hacker News. Its stated goal is to coordinate a global response as AI agents uncover security holes. After the PHP scramble, that sounds less like a nice idea and more like a missing piece of the internet.

## Two days ago we opened with a break-in at OpenCode. Today, another popular open source AI tool has been turned against its users.

LiteLLM is a Python library that lets a program talk to dozens of AI models, from OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and others, through one common interface. It is everywhere in AI projects. Version 1.107.0, published on PyPI, the main public store for Python packages, was compromised. Install it, and you also install a credential stealer, a program that hunts for passwords and API keys on your machine, the passwords that programs use to reach online services, plus a backdoor, a hidden way for the attacker to get back in later. This is called a supply chain attack. The attacker does not break into you. They break into something you trust and wait for you to install it. LiteLLM is an especially rich target, because its whole job is to hold the keys to every AI provider a company uses. One stolen key can mean someone else running up your AI bill. The backdoor means removing the package may not be enough. You may have it without knowing. Many AI tools pull in LiteLLM automatically as one of the packages they depend on, so check every Python environment that touches AI, not just your own projects.

If you use LiteLLM, check which version you have installed today. If it is that one, assume every key on that machine is stolen: replace them all, and rebuild the machine if you can. And name the exact version your project installs, so a poisoned new release cannot slip in on its own.

## One more security story, about the padlock in your browser.

When you visit your bank's website, your browser checks a certificate, a digital ID card that proves the site is really your bank. Those ID cards are issued by certificate authorities, companies your browser trusts by default. Most of them do not sign website certificates directly from their master key. They use intermediate certificates: second-level keys, signed by the master key, that do the day-to-day signing. eMudhra is a certificate authority based in India. Some of its intermediate certificates have been compromised. Mozilla, which makes Firefox, published a post on Tuesday about protecting Firefox users after the compromise, and opened a bug report to track it. Whoever holds a compromised intermediate could, in principle, issue a certificate for any website, and browsers would accept it. That is how you impersonate a bank, or quietly read traffic that looks encrypted. The usual response is for browser makers to stop trusting the affected certificates, so anything they signed is rejected, including any fakes.

Browsers have cut off certificate authorities before after slips like this. In 2011 a Dutch one, DigiNotar, was hacked, distrusted, and went bankrupt within weeks. Chrome, Safari and Edge each keep their own list of trusted authorities, and each decides separately, so keep Firefox updated and watch whether the others follow Mozilla. The bigger point is that this whole system is only as strong as the least careful of the dozens of companies your browser trusts. One slip at one of them puts every website at risk.

## Now the cloud, and a deal between two companies that compete head to head. OpenAI's models are coming to Google Cloud.

In the coming weeks, OpenAI's current GPT 6 and 6.1 models, named Astra, Sol and Luna, will appear in Google's Model Garden, its menu of more than two hundred AI models that cloud customers can rent. Astra, the top model, will cost the most. More interesting: GPT models will also show up in Antigravity, Google's coding tool built around AI agents. So Google will sell you its rival's model inside its own product. Antigravity users will be able to pick GPT for one job and Gemini for the next, inside the same tool. Google says it is now the only big cloud provider offering all three leading closed models, the kind you cannot download, from OpenAI, Anthropic, and itself. For a business, one bill and one set of security rules covering all three model families is a real reason to pick Google. For years, the only big cloud selling OpenAI's top models was Microsoft's, and OpenAI says Microsoft remains its primary cloud partner. Google also lets customers shift work between models automatically, based on speed and cost, which makes switching away from any one model easier. Now Anthropic, whose plans to go public we covered on Tuesday. The new report is that Anthropic is struggling to get enough computing power from the big cloud companies, because those companies increasingly see it as a competitor. Its rivals for customers include the companies it rents chips from: Google sells Gemini, Amazon sells its own Nova models, and Microsoft sells Copilot. Anthropic's spending on computing keeps growing, and its stock market debut is reportedly planned for November.

Put the two together. The big clouds want to rent out every popular model, because customers ask for them. Google decided it would rather earn money on OpenAI's models than send those customers to Microsoft. Anthropic, which counts Amazon and Google among its biggest investors, is finding that being a big customer and a direct competitor at once has limits, right before it asks the stock market for money.

## On Tuesday we told you Amazon was going after the software that keeps AI developers on Nvidia chips. Google has now joined it.

Google released a developer tool that makes it easy for people who build with PyTorch, the most popular toolkit for AI models, to move their programs off Nvidia's chips. The obvious destination is Google's own AI chips, called TPUs, which it rents out in its cloud.

Two of the three biggest clouds are now attacking the same weak spot. Nvidia still makes the chips most people want. These tools go after the other reason people stay: their code only runs well on Nvidia.

## Money now, and a clear sign of how much AI costs. Meta, one of the most profitable companies on earth, wants new ways to raise cash.

With its quarterly results on Wednesday, Meta said it will issue a new class of shares with no voting rights. The money goes to its AI push, to possible acquisitions, and to paying employees. Trading starts in 2027. In its own words, the investment needed to compete and expand its infrastructure during what it calls the AI transformation is significant. Chief financial officer Susan Li said the new shares will expand the company's funding tools. Why shares with no vote? Because Mark Zuckerberg already controls Meta through a separate class of shares that carry extra votes. Selling ordinary voting shares, or paying staff in them, would slowly shrink Zuckerberg's share of the vote. Non-voting shares let Meta raise money and pay people while Zuckerberg's control stays where it is. Google did the same thing in 2014, for the same reason. Buyers of the new stock will own part of Meta and have no say at all in how its AI money gets spent.

The bigger signal is the first part. Meta makes tens of billions of dollars a year in profit, and that is apparently not enough to pay for the AI build-out it wants. On Tuesday it was CoreWeave and its borrowed money. Today it is Meta creating a new class of stock. The ways companies pay for this boom keep getting more unusual.

## Now Elon Musk, and a rocket company that now talks mostly about computers.

Some background first. SpaceX bought Musk's AI company, xAI, in February, and went public in June with the largest stock market debut on record. On Tuesday it reported its third quarter, and it made a profit: 4.4 billion dollars. TechCrunch says AI was what drove it. The earnings call showed what the company cares about now. Ars Technica's Eric Berger put it bluntly: in its current form, SpaceX is largely a data company. Officials spent most of the call on plans to build data centers drawing up to 10 gigawatts of power in 2027, on the ground. For scale, one large nuclear reactor produces about one gigawatt. Talk of settling Mars has mostly disappeared from these calls. The longer plan is still to put data centers in orbit, a constellation it calls Starmind, and that needs a cheap, reusable rocket. So Musk and president Gwynne Shotwell set new targets for Starship. On the next flight, SpaceX will try to catch Starship with the arms on its launch tower, and then aims to fly it much more often. Musk also said merging SpaceX and Tesla would be a good idea, though shareholders would have to back it. Tesla builds AI of its own, for self-driving cars and its Optimus humanoid robot, so a merger would put all of Musk's AI work in one company.

Put it together and you have Musk's companies moving toward a single business: rockets, satellites, cars, a chatbot, and data centers, all pointed at AI. The 4.4 billion dollar profit shows the bet is paying, for now. A merger would also tie Tesla's shareholders to that bet, whether they signed up for an AI company or not.

## Next, a startup you have probably never heard of, but your doctor may use every day.

It is called OpenEvidence. It is a chatbot that searches medical journals and clinical guidelines, the published advice on how to treat each condition, to help doctors diagnose and treat patients. The Financial Times reports that nearly two thirds of American doctors now use it, and that doctors sent it more than 42 million questions last month. There are about a million physicians in the country. The company was founded in 2022 by Daniel Nadler, a serial entrepreneur, and is now valued at 15 billion dollars. Medicine publishes far more research than any doctor can read, and the guidance changes often. A tool that answers in seconds, with sources, fills a real gap. It grew mostly by word of mouth. Nadler calls it the first viral consumer internet app in healthcare. That is rare in medicine. Hospitals usually buy software slowly, through committees. OpenEvidence skipped them and went straight to individual doctors, who could sign up on their own. Here is the part worth thinking about. OpenEvidence is free for doctors and is paid for by advertising.

Think about who wants to advertise to doctors at the moment they are deciding how to treat someone. The obvious buyers are drug and device makers. That does not mean the answers are bought. It does mean the business depends on the people with the strongest interest in what doctors prescribe. The FT says the company's rise has started a debate over patient care, and it should. Doctors should be able to see exactly what was an ad and what was evidence, and so should regulators. There is also the question of mistakes. A chatbot that summarizes research can still get a dose or a drug interaction wrong, and the doctor is the one who has to catch it.

## Now a model with an unusual origin story.

A startup called Recursive has released Flint, a free coding model small enough to run on a laptop. Running on your own laptop means your code never leaves your machine, and there is no monthly bill. Small models have improved fast. What a laptop can run today does work that needed a data center two years ago. The headline claim is how Flint was made. Recursive says its own AI research system did most of the work of building it. The company calls Flint an early test of AI that improves itself. Why does that matter? If AI can do most of the work of building the next model, progress is no longer limited by how many researchers a lab can hire. That is the idea behind the name Recursive.

Treat that claim with care. Most labs already use AI to write code and run experiments, so saying it was largely built by AI could mean anything from tidying scripts to designing the model. What matters is whether Flint is good, and a free laptop model is easy to test. If it holds up against models built by people, the self-improving pitch gets much harder to wave away.

## Developers to finish, and two startups working on the same question: once an AI agent can act for you, who controls what it is allowed to do?

First, Alter, from Y Combinator's summer 2025 batch. Its founders describe a problem anyone who has built an internal company chatbot will recognize. Everyone talks to the agent in Slack under their own account. But behind the scenes, the agent connects to the customer database or the billing system with one shared key. So the agent can do anything that key can do, no matter who asked. An intern and the finance director get the same powers. That means a cleverly worded request from anyone in the company could get the agent to do things that person could never do directly. Alter sits between the agent and those systems. For every request, it checks who the agent is acting for, applies rules about what that person may do, and keeps an audit log, a permanent record for compliance. One founder, Srikar, worked at Goldman Sachs and saw how expensive this problem makes agents in regulated industries like banking. Second, Omnara, an open source system for running long-lived agents in the cloud. You create an agent with one request. Omnara runs it, gives it sandboxes, sealed-off machines, to run commands in, and handles its secrets and permissions. Each agent's state is saved in Postgres, a popular database, so agents survive restarts and cost nothing while they sit idle. It can still use your laptop as one of its machines when needed. Anthropic and OpenAI sell hosted versions of this, Claude Managed Agents and the OpenAI Agents API. Omnara's pitch is that you run it on your own servers, with whatever models you choose. A hosted agent service ties you to one company's models, prices and rules. Open source gives you a way out, at the cost of running it yourself. The team started out with a phone app for remote-controlling Claude Code and Codex on a laptop, and moved to the cloud because they decided agents tied to a laptop were not the future.

Notice the pattern. A year ago the hard part of agents was getting them to do anything useful. Now the hard part is the work around them: who they act for, what they can touch, and where they keep running when nobody is watching. That is usually the sign a technology is moving from demo to daily work.

## One more developer story, and it is about money.

Blacksmith, a company that runs the automatic build and test jobs that check code before it ships, raised 66 million dollars in its third major funding round, led by the investment firm Sequoia. That values it at one billion dollars. Its pitch is making those checks work better for companies whose code is increasingly written by AI.

The logic is simple. AI writes more code, and more code means more tests to run. And as the PHP and LiteLLM stories show, someone still has to check what actually ships.
