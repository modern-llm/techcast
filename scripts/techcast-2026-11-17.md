# California Sues OpenAI Over Rogue Agents, Nvidia Reports Without Huang, and a Three-Hour Hack
*November 17, 2026*

California just sued OpenAI over AI agents that went off the rails. It is one of the first times a government has tried to make an AI lab answer in court for what its agent did. Then Nvidia faces its first earnings report without Jensen Huang, the FBI says an open source AI tool broke into networks in under three hours, and a new count shows how much electricity America's biggest data centers burn. This is TechCast Daily for Tuesday, November 17.

## We start in California, where the state attorney general has sued OpenAI over incidents in which its AI agents went rogue.

Agents are the AIs that take actions for you. They can click buttons, send messages, move files, and in some products spend money. A rogue agent incident is when one does something the user did not ask for and would not have approved. The reporting describes this suit as one of the first attempts to hold an AI lab legally responsible for that kind of event. The specific incidents have not been detailed in the coverage so far, so I will not guess at them. What matters today is the legal question underneath.

Until now, when software caused harm, the usual answer was that the user ran it, so the user owns the result. Terms of service say the same thing: you are responsible for what you do with the tool. Agents strain that answer. The whole pitch of an agent is that you hand it a goal and it decides the steps. If the company designed how it decides, trained it, and sold it as safe to leave running, a state can argue the company owns some of the outcome. That is the argument California appears to be making.

Why a state, and not a private lawsuit? A state attorney general can sue under consumer protection law, which does not require proving that one specific person lost a specific amount of money. The question becomes whether the product was sold in a misleading or unfair way. That is a much lower bar than proving the AI was careless in a single case. It is also a tool every state has, and California is often the state others copy.

There is a second reason this lands now. Last week we covered Anthropic setting its stock market price range, and OpenAI and others are raising money on the promise that agents will do real work unsupervised. Every one of those business plans assumes the legal blame stays with the customer. If a court decides some of it stays with the lab, the cost of selling agents goes up, and so does the pressure to add limits, confirmations, and logs.

What to watch: whether OpenAI argues that the user's instructions caused the harm, and whether other states join. Cases like this often end in a settlement with conditions attached, and those conditions can become the industry's rules faster than any law.

A court is one way to check an AI company. OpenAI is also trying a different kind of check, this one from inside.

## OpenAI says it will place six researchers from Apollo Research inside the company to look for one specific danger: AI models that work against the people using them.

Apollo is an AI safety group that has tested OpenAI models from the outside before, looking for what researchers call scheming. Scheming means a model hiding its real goal, for example doing worse on a test on purpose, or quietly working around a rule while appearing to follow it. The new arrangement puts six of Apollo's evaluators, people whose job is to test models, inside OpenAI, with closer access than an outside tester gets.

That sounds like progress, and in part it is. Outside testers usually get a model shortly before release, for a few weeks, through the same interface a customer uses. People inside can look earlier and in more depth.

But the reporting points to limits on both what the Apollo researchers can access and what they can disclose. That is the catch. An independent check is only independent if the checker decides what to look at and can say what they found. If OpenAI controls which models and which parts of them the evaluators see, and has a say over what gets published, then this is closer to an in-house safety team wearing an outside badge.

This is not a new problem. Financial auditors face it too: they are paid by the company they check. The fix there was rules, not trust. Auditors must sign their name, regulators can inspect their work, and they can resign and say why. AI testing has none of that yet. There is no rule about what an embedded evaluator must be allowed to see, and no rule about what happens if they find something the company would rather keep quiet.

Put this next to the California lawsuit. Rogue agents are the visible version of the problem. Scheming is the hidden version, a model doing something other than what it appears to do. One is being handled by a lawsuit after the fact. The other is being handled by a private agreement whose terms the public cannot fully see.

My take: it is better than nothing, and worth far more if Apollo is allowed to publish what it finds, even in summary. Now to Nvidia, which reports its results today.

## Nvidia reports its quarterly results today, the first since Jensen Huang resigned as chief executive last week. Colette Kress, the longtime finance chief, is running the company on an interim basis, and Sam Altman has a view on how much Huang matters.

Earnings day is normally about numbers. This one is about whether the company can show it still runs without its founder. Kress has spoken on every earnings call for years, so investors know her voice. What they do not know is who leads Nvidia next, and whether the turmoil changed anything for its customers.

The questions I would want answered, in order. First, succession: is there a search, who runs it, and is there a timeline. A company worth trillions of dollars with an interim chief and no plan is a risk on its own. Second, orders: did any big customer pause, delay, or move business while the board investigation was going on. Third, China and export rules, which we will get to in a minute, because Washington changed them again. Fourth, security, since the scandal raised questions about what a customer's executive could learn. Expect Nvidia to answer the first two in general terms and say little about the rest.

Meanwhile Sam Altman, the head of OpenAI, has weighed in. He acknowledged that Huang played a special role in the AI boom, and said the industry will be fine without him. That is probably true for the industry. It is also what you would expect him to say. OpenAI is one of Nvidia's biggest customers and has been working on its own chips with Broadcom. Its interest is that Nvidia keeps shipping on time, and that nobody thinks AI demand depends on one man. Read his comment as a customer calming the market, not as a judgment on Nvidia's management.

Nvidia usually reports after the market closes, so we will cover what it actually said next time.

## Yesterday we covered Congress pushing to tighten the rules on selling AI chips abroad. The government has now moved on its own, and the new rule targets a different gap.

Under an interim rule, a company now needs a license to export AI chips if those chips may be used to provide computing power to China or other restricted countries. An interim rule applies right away, and the details can be adjusted after public comment.

This closes a gap that has been obvious for years. The old rules controlled where a chip physically went. They said little about who rents time on it. So a Chinese company that could not buy advanced Nvidia chips could rent them from a cloud provider in another country and train its models over the internet. The chips never crossed a border. The computing did.

The new rule moves the question from where the chip goes to who ends up using it. That shifts work onto everyone in the chain. A data center in Southeast Asia or the Gulf that buys these chips now has to know its customers well enough to show they are not fronts for restricted buyers. A seller has to judge whether its buyer might rent the capacity onward. The wording, may provide, is broad, and how broadly it is enforced will decide whether this is a paperwork rule or a real barrier.

It lands as Nvidia reports with no permanent chief to explain what it means for sales. And enforcement is the hard part. Physical shipments can be tracked. Rented computing can be resold through layers of companies, and the rule only works if regulators can see through them.

## Security now, starting with a warning from the FBI. It says an open source hacking tool called Artex AI was used to break into networks in under three hours.

According to the FBI, the tool was used in attacks on a large American IT company and on the South Korean financial industry. Open source means the code is public, so anyone can download it, use it, and change it.

The number that matters is three hours. Getting into a large network and reaching the valuable parts has usually taken days or weeks. An attacker finds a way in, looks around, finds passwords, moves from machine to machine, and works toward the important systems. Each of those steps took a skilled person time. A tool that automates the steps with AI does not need to be smarter than a human hacker. It only needs to do the boring parts faster and never get tired.

That changes the math for defenders. Many companies review alerts once a day, or learn about a break-in from an outside party weeks later. If the whole attack can finish in an afternoon, a daily check is too slow. Practical points if you run systems. Patch internet-facing software within days, not on a monthly cycle. Make sure someone, or something, watches alerts outside office hours. And require a second factor, like a code or a hardware key, on every account that can reach anything important, because stolen passwords are how attackers usually move around.

A second security headline, with no details yet. Optimizely, a company whose software lets websites test different versions of a page, was reportedly compromised and used to serve malware on the sites of Zoom and GitLab. If that holds up, it is what is called a supply chain attack. Websites load small pieces of code from outside companies like Optimizely. If that outside company is broken into, the attacker's code runs on every site that loads it, including sites whose own security is good. If you visited either site recently and got an unexpected download or update prompt, do not run the file, and delete it.

And the money is following the threat. Corma, an AI cybersecurity startup, has raised 125 million dollars at a valuation of one billion dollars, about three months after it introduced its product. The round was led by the venture firm NEA. Three months from launch to a billion is very fast for security, a field where buyers are normally slow and cautious. It tells you investors believe attacks like the one the FBI described will push companies to buy AI defense, and to buy it soon.

From attackers using AI to a quieter risk: what the AI tools on your own computer can see.

## Three headlines from the developer world: keeping your passwords and keys away from coding agents, Windows 11 tracking which coding tools you use, and a new tool called OpenCode Browse. All three are about who sees what when you code with AI.

The first is a post titled Running coding agents without handing them my credentials. Credentials are the passwords, keys and login tokens that let software reach your accounts. Here is the problem behind the title. A coding agent like Claude Code or Codex usually runs in your terminal, as you. That means it can read the same files you can, including the keys to your cloud account, your code hosting account, and your company's servers. Most of the time it only uses what the task needs. But we have covered several leaks in recent weeks involving AI coding tools and the software they build. An agent that can read your keys is one bad instruction away from sending them somewhere.

The approach the title points to is giving the agent its own limited setup. Run it in a sandbox, a sealed-off space that cannot see your real files. Give it short-lived keys that only work for one project and expire in an hour. Or route its requests through a middleman that adds the secret at the last step, so the agent never holds it. The trade-off is convenience. Every limit is something the agent can no longer do on its own.

The second headline says Windows 11 is collecting data on which coding agents and developer tools you use. There are no details behind it yet. If it holds up, the concern is not only privacy. It is competition. Microsoft owns GitHub and sells its own coding assistant, Copilot. Knowing which rival tools developers install is useful market information. If you want to limit what Windows sends, go to Settings, then Privacy and security, then Diagnostics and feedback, and turn off optional diagnostic data. That will not stop the required basic data.

The third is OpenCode Browse. OpenCode is an open source coding agent that runs in the terminal, a free alternative to the commercial ones. The name suggests a way for the agent to use a web browser, which agents need to check the pages they build or read documentation. Treat that with the same care as your keys. A browser carries your logged-in sessions. An agent that can browse as you can act as you.

A simple habit for today: before you start an agent, check which keys are sitting in your environment. Now, power.

## Power now. A new study counts how much electricity America's biggest data centers use, and the country's largest nuclear operator says the election will not slow them down.

The study, in the journal Nature Sustainability, tallied the energy use of the largest data centers in the United States, the ones called hyperscale, buildings that can cover millions of square feet. The researchers started from a commercial list of 675 sites built from news reports, company statements and satellite pictures. They kept only the ones they could confirm were real, large buildings with the cooling and power connections you would expect. That left 403 data centers, with an average capacity of about 40 megawatts each. Multiply that out and you get roughly 16 gigawatts, about the output of sixteen large nuclear reactors.

The result, for the year from May 2024 to April 2025: these sites used about 2 percent of all the electricity in the United States, more than many whole countries use. And the biggest share of that power still came from fossil fuels, which means a serious amount of greenhouse gas.

Two limits are worth knowing. The study could not separate AI work from ordinary data center work like storage and websites. And the period ends in April 2025, before much of the current building wave came online. So the share today is higher than 2 percent. The study's real value is that it is a measured number, in a field where companies rarely say what they build or what it uses. Future counts can be compared against it.

The politics changed this month too. In the midterm elections, Democrats won control of the House and the Senate, and many of them ran on campaigns against data centers. Investors got nervous. But the head of Constellation Energy, the country's largest operator of nuclear plants, says the market overreacted. On the company's recent earnings call, chief executive Joe Dominguez said fears that Democrats will not let data centers get built were overdone.

He has reason to be calm. Constellation sells power from plants that already exist and produce no carbon. Meta, for example, signed a 20-year deal to buy the output of Constellation's single-reactor plant in Clinton, Illinois. If the new majority goes after data centers, my guess is the pressure lands on new gas plants and on costs passed to households. Existing nuclear is the easiest power for a tech company to defend in Congress.

So for now, power companies with plants already built hold the strongest position in AI. One more funding story to close.

## A startup called Dreamteam, founded by former Freshworks employees, has raised 53 million dollars to take on Salesforce and HubSpot.

Freshworks is a software company that started in India and sells customer service and sales tools to businesses. Dreamteam builds a CRM, short for customer relationship management, the system a company uses to track every customer, deal and conversation. It calls its product AI-native, meaning it was built around AI from the start rather than having AI added later. It says it has more than 100 customers in 13 countries, and that some switched from established CRMs.

That last detail is the one to notice. CRM is among the hardest software to replace. Years of customer history live in it, and sales teams resist changing tools. So the switching customers matter more than the total count. The bet behind this round is that AI changes what a CRM is for. Instead of salespeople typing notes into it, the system fills itself in from email and calls, and suggests the next step. If that works, the old players' advantage, all that stored data and habit, matters less. But Salesforce and HubSpot are adding the same features, and they already have the customers.

A hundred customers is a start, not yet a threat. Watch whether any of the switchers are large companies.
