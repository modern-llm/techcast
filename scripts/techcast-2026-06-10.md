# Apple Bets on Google, Anthropic Goes Public, OpenAI Wants to Be Everything
*June 10, 2026*

Apple held its Worldwide Developers Conference today, and the move that will generate the most debate wasn't a new device — it was the revelation that Apple's AI architecture is now built around Google Gemini models. We'll dig into what that means, plus Anthropic's Fable 5 launch, OpenAI filing for its IPO, and the strange economics of an AI industry where a vibe-coding tool is doing half a billion in annual revenue.

## Let's start with Apple, because WWDC 2026 is one of the more consequential developer conferences in recent memory — and not primarily because of the hardware.

The headline from today's keynote is that Apple's new AI architecture is built around Google Gemini models. Not integrated with Gemini as a third-party option — built around it. That's a striking admission for a company that spent decades building everything in-house: its own chips, its own neural engines, its own frameworks. Choosing to anchor the AI strategy to a Google product signals how far behind Apple felt it had fallen in the AI race. It's also a big win for Google, which has been struggling to find distribution for Gemini at scale — having it ship to over a billion Apple devices is the kind of deal you can't buy.

The companion piece to the Gemini partnership is what Apple is calling the Core AI Framework — a new API layer that gives developers access to on-device and cloud AI without managing the infrastructure themselves. The pricing pitch is specifically aimed at small developers and indie makers: Apple says inference through this framework will cost significantly less than going direct to OpenAI or Anthropic. That's a real wedge in a market where API costs are still a meaningful barrier for anyone building a consumer app on a bootstrap budget.

On Siri: after two years of promises and demos that didn't land, Apple seems to have shipped a version that can actually do multi-step reasoning, hold context across a conversation, and work across apps in ways previous Siri couldn't. What was notable in the keynote was how restrained Apple was about it — no superlatives, no five-minute demos of Siri booking a complex international trip. That restraint, for Apple, might be the most encouraging signal. It suggests they've calibrated expectations after getting burned before.

For iOS 27 and macOS 27 Golden Gate, Apple called this a refinement year, and they meant it. The Liquid Glass design language from last year is getting tuned — users now get a slider in Settings to adjust how translucent the effect is, which is a direct response to the people who found last year's version too visually noisy. Mac gets some sidebar and toolbar improvements that address complaints about how the previous redesign handled window chrome. There's a tighter corner radius on windows too. These are small things, but they suggest Apple is actually listening.

Safari got an interesting AI treatment. Apple is using it to solve one of Safari's chronic problems: its thin extension library. The solution is to let users describe the extension they want and have Safari's AI build it on the spot. You want something that clips and organizes recipes from cooking sites? Describe it in plain language. Safari builds it. This is vibe-coding baked directly into the browser, and it sidesteps Apple's historically strict developer requirements by making the user the developer.

And yes, Apple is folding. The rumored foldable device finally got its official reveal. Pricing and full specs are still forthcoming, but the form factor is confirmed. We'll have more on that as the details come in.

The through-line across all of this is Apple trying to catch up on AI while keeping control of the platform experience. The Gemini deal is the most interesting move — it's an admission that they couldn't build their way there alone, but it's also a classic Apple play: use a partner's technology, wrap it in Apple UX, and make it feel native. Whether it works depends entirely on how the integration performs in practice, not in demos.

## While Apple was on stage, Anthropic shipped something significant. Claude Fable 5 is now publicly available.

Anthropic has been talking internally about its Mythos tier of models for a while — their most capable class, the kind they treat with extra caution before release. Today, Claude Fable 5 became the first Mythos-class model the public can actually access. Anthropic also released the system card — their technical document that explains what the model can do, how it was trained, and what guardrails are built in.

The guardrails story is specific. Fable 5 has hard blocks on certain categories of requests, particularly around cybersecurity exploitation and biology — the kinds of prompts that could theoretically help someone build a weapon or compromise infrastructure. This isn't entirely new for Claude models, but the system card language is notably precise about the boundaries, which suggests these constraints are more robust than in previous generations and not just hand-waving.

What does Mythos-class mean in practice? Anthropic's framing is that these aren't just more accurate or faster than their previous generation — they're capable of longer-horizon reasoning, more reliable outputs on hard problems, and more consistent behavior in complex multi-step tasks. Independent benchmarks will tell us more over the coming weeks, but the model is live now and people are already running it through its paces.

One thing worth noting about the timing: Anthropic shipped its biggest public model on the same day as Apple's WWDC. That is not a coincidence. The AI PR calendar in 2026 is fully competitive.

The system card is actually worth reading if you want to understand where the frontier is and where the unresolved problems are. Model releases are demos. System cards are honesty documents. Anthropic has historically been more forthcoming in theirs than most.

## Both major AI labs are now in the IPO queue, and one of them is also rethinking its flagship product from the ground up.

OpenAI this week filed confidentially with the SEC — a Form S-1 submission that formally kicks off the process toward going public. This follows Anthropic's similar filing on June 1st, and now the AI IPO race that everyone has been speculating about for two years is happening simultaneously at both companies. Anthropic's last fundraise put it at the top of global startup valuations. OpenAI's last private round valued the company at around 850 billion dollars. These are extraordinary numbers for companies that are still burning significant capital on compute.

But before OpenAI rings any opening bells, they have a more immediate problem: ChatGPT is due for a fundamental rethink. The Financial Times got access to internal planning documents that reveal how significant the overhaul will be. The internal framing, according to people at the company, is blunt: chat is dead. The conversational back-and-forth interface that made ChatGPT famous in late 2022 is being repositioned as just one mode inside what OpenAI wants to become a superapp — combining coding tools, AI agents, and the chat interface into a single product that can justify a much higher price per user.

The business logic is clear. OpenAI needs to grow revenue per user to justify its valuation ahead of a public listing. ChatGPT subscriptions at twenty dollars a month don't get you to an 850 billion dollar public market cap. A superapp with enterprise coding tools and agent workflows might. The execution risk is equally clear: ChatGPT became popular because it was simple. Every layer of complexity you add is a chance to make it worse.

The confidential filing structure means most financials, executive compensation, and risk factors stay private until they decide to go fully public. So we'll know relatively little until the S-1 drops. But both companies are now in the pipeline, and both are racing to establish their narrative before the other.

The timing pressure here is real. Both companies want to capture public market enthusiasm for AI before the cycle turns. Whether they get priced on vision or on fundamentals once they're public is going to be one of the more interesting financial stories of the next year.

## Two data points from this week paint very different pictures of where value is accumulating in the AI economy.

Lovable, the AI-powered app builder aimed at non-technical founders, says it has crossed 500 million dollars in annualized run-rate revenue and is now seeing a million new projects created every week. Lovable is essentially a vibe-coding platform — you describe what you want to build, the AI writes the code and wires up the infrastructure. The company is explicitly positioning this as a tool for building real businesses, not just prototypes.

Five hundred million in annualized run-rate is a remarkable number for a product category that barely existed two years ago. It says something concrete about demand: people want AI that does the thing they were going to hire a developer to do, and they want it badly enough to pay for it at scale. Annualized run-rate is the most optimistic way to present revenue — it takes this month's number and multiplies by twelve — but even with a skeptical discount, the trajectory is striking.

On the other end of the spectrum: xAI, Elon Musk's AI company, is drawing a pointed comparison from observers this week. The argument circulating is that xAI looks more like a datacenter REIT than a frontier AI lab. REIT stands for Real Estate Investment Trust — companies that own property and lease it. The critique is that xAI's primary business activity has been building and operating massive compute clusters, most visibly the Colossus facility in Memphis, while its research output and product traction haven't kept pace with the infrastructure investment. Grok exists, people use it, but it isn't setting the research agenda the way OpenAI or Anthropic are.

The REIT framing is pointed because REITs are valued on their asset base, not their innovation. If xAI's moat is owning a lot of GPUs, that's a defensible business — but it's a completely different business than being a frontier lab.

Together these two stories tell you where AI value is actually accruing in 2026. It's in infrastructure, and it's in the tools that let non-technical people build things. The hard frontier research is not where the money is moving fastest.

## A couple of technical developments worth putting on your radar.

MiMo-v2.5-Pro-UltraSpeed is making the rounds this week — it's a one-trillion-parameter model that runs at one thousand tokens per second. That speed number is significant because it's roughly ten times faster than where frontier-scale models sat a year ago. Getting to that throughput at that model size changes what's feasible in production: real-time voice, dense agent loops, inline code completion at scale without queuing. The inference speed arms race is now as visible as the capability race.

On the research side, a paper on speculative KV coding is getting attention. KV cache is one of the less glamorous but critically important parts of how large language models run efficiently — it's the data structure that stores the context window so the model doesn't have to recompute from scratch on every token. This paper claims lossless compression of the KV cache by up to four times. If that holds up in practice, the implications are significant: four times the compression means you can fit four times as much context in the same memory budget, or run the same workload on cheaper hardware. Systems-level improvements like this are where the real efficiency gains compound quietly in the background.

And a brief flag: Rich Sutton — the reinforcement learning pioneer behind the widely-read bitter lesson essay about how scaling beats hand-crafted inductive biases — gave a new talk this week on AI creativity and discovery. Sutton is one of the people in this field whose perspective on the long arc is actually worth tracking. The talk is up on YouTube and it's on my list.

The MiMo speed number and the KV compression paper together point at something that doesn't get enough attention: the efficiency frontier is moving as fast as the capability frontier. Models are getting cheaper to run at the same time they're getting more capable. That combination is what drives the cost curve down fast enough to make AI genuinely ubiquitous rather than a premium feature.
