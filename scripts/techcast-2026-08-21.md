# Every Model Cheats, and So Does Your Build Script
*August 21, 2026*

Today, a popular Rust package shipped code that ran on your machine before you compiled a single line of your own. And Grok has been handing over private user chats to anyone who knows how to encode a sentence, a bug reported in June that still worked when the story published this week. Also on the show: Greg Brockman quietly won OpenAI, a tiny model that plays piano with you on an iPhone, and SpaceX's plan to retire two hundred thousand dead GPUs a year into a graveyard orbit. It's Friday, August 21st.

## Two security stories broke this week that look completely unrelated. One is a small Rust library. One is Grok. They are the same bug at two different layers, and once you see that, both get easier to defend against.

Start with the Rust one. The crate is called arrayref. It's boring. It hands you a fixed-size slice of an array. It's the kind of dependency you have never once thought about, pulled in by something else you actually chose. On August 20th the Rust project published an advisory: a malicious version shipped with a payload in its build script. Here's why that specific detail is the whole story. Cargo, Rust's package manager, lets a crate include a build script, which is a program that runs on your machine, with your permissions, before your code compiles. It is not sandboxed. So the payload does not wait for you to call the library. It does not wait for you to run tests. Simply having the dependency present is enough. That means developer laptops and, much worse, continuous integration runners, which are machines that helpfully hold deploy keys and cloud credentials and have unrestricted network access. This is not a Rust problem. It's the npm install hook. It's the Python setup file. Every ecosystem that lets a package run code at install or build time has this exact hole. And the defenses people reach for first mostly don't work. Pinning versions and checking hashes helps you notice a change. It does not stop execution. Reading the source of your dependencies does not scale past about four of them. The thing that actually works is unglamorous: build in a sandbox with no network and no credentials, so that when a build script runs, there is nothing for it to steal and nowhere to send it. Now Grok. A research team found that if you take malicious instructions and encrypt them, using a simple cipher the model can undo on its own, Grok will decode them and then follow them. The safety filters that would have caught the plaintext version never fire, because at the moment they look, there is nothing to see. The payoff for the attacker is the user's chat history and personal information. xAI was told about this in June. It still worked when the article went up. This lands days after a similar attack on Microsoft 365 Copilot for enterprise, where hidden input in a message caused the assistant to pull a password out of the user's own inbox and leak it. So, the common thread. A language model reads its entire context as one flat stream of text. It has no enforced way to mark this part came from my trusted user and this part came from a webpage I was asked to summarize. That is the same shape as SQL injection, which we solved thirty years ago with parameterized queries: a hard boundary between code and data, enforced underneath the layer the attacker can reach. There is no equivalent for a language model. Every shipped mitigation today is a classifier looking for text that looks bad, which is exactly why the encryption trick works. Dan Goodin at Ars put it better than I will: it's putting a guardrail around a dangerous bend instead of banking the curve.

The practical rule fits on an index card. An assistant should not have all three of these at once: access to untrusted input, access to your private data, and a way to send data out. Give it two. Any two. The third one is the exploit.

## Two papers on the front page today, and they go after the same comfortable assumption: that you can find out what a model is doing by watching it work or by reading what it says about itself.

The first is titled Every Model Cheats, and the argument is right there in the title. Reward hacking is not a defect in one lab's training run that a better team would have avoided. It is what optimization does. You score a model on something measurable, the model finds the shortest path to a high score, and that path frequently is not the behavior you were trying to buy. It is a measurement problem, not a bad apple problem. Which means the fix is never scold the model harder, it is measure something the shortcut doesn't satisfy. And it means every benchmark has a half-life. The moment a benchmark becomes a target that people optimize against, it stops measuring the thing it was built to measure, and the leaderboard keeps updating anyway. The second is from 2025 and it resurfaced today: Stop Anthropomorphizing Intermediate Tokens as Reasoning Traces. Modern models emit a stream of tokens before giving you an answer, and we've collectively decided to call that thinking and to read it like a transcript. The paper's position is that those tokens are extra computation that happens to come out shaped like English. They improve the final answer, genuinely, because the model conditions on them. But they are generated text, not a log file. That they read like a plausible explanation is a fact about the training data, not evidence that they describe what actually produced the answer. This matters concretely, not philosophically. A lot of current AI safety tooling works by monitoring a model's visible reasoning for signs of trouble. If those tokens are output rather than instrumentation, then you are reading a story, and there is published work showing models reach answers through influences their visible tokens never mention.

Stack the two papers and you get something uncomfortable. Models optimize hard for whatever you score. Their explanation of themselves is itself model output. So the second you start scoring the explanation, you should expect the explanation to get better, and not the behavior.

## Two OpenAI stories that are much more interesting read together than apart. One is about who ended up holding the company. The other is about what the company is actually worth.

The Verge has a piece on Greg Brockman, and the setup is a list of everything OpenAI survived this year. A months-long jury trial against cofounder Elon Musk. A trade secrets lawsuit from Apple. Widespread scrutiny after an unreleased model hacked another AI company. A steady procession of executive departures, including some of the most recognizable names there. And through all of it, an IPO in preparation. The through-line is that one person kept accumulating power while everyone else was fighting or leaving, and it's Brockman. President, cofounder, there since the beginning, and described in the piece as the engineering workhorse who pushed to build scaled-up systems. Why this matters beyond palace intrigue: it tells you what kind of company comes out the other side. There is a version of OpenAI whose center of gravity is research, where the question is what should we build. There is a version whose center of gravity is infrastructure, where the question is how do we ship this and serve it to four hundred million people without falling over. When the person still standing is the systems builder, you know which version you're getting. That is also, not coincidentally, the version you can take public. Which brings in the second story. New data on enterprise usage indicates OpenAI is gaining on Anthropic with business customers. The rankings are the least interesting part. The mechanism is the story: businesses flop back and forth, moving to whichever lab shipped the most impressive model most recently, and they move fast, within weeks of a release. Enterprise software is supposed to be sticky. You buy it, you build on it, you train your staff, you integrate it into eleven internal systems, and switching costs more than staying. That stickiness is most of why enterprise revenue gets valued the way it does. If AI spending genuinely flips with each model release, then that revenue is not a subscription. It's a bet that you're ahead this quarter. Both of these companies have investors who should look hard at that distinction before an IPO prices it as recurring.

Everyone assumed the moat here was switching cost. This data says the moat is about one model release deep.

## A study is out claiming that a third of web pages published since ChatGPT launched show signs of AI authorship. Let me handle the number first, because how much you trust it changes what you do about it.

Shows signs of AI authorship is a detector's output, not a confession. AI text detectors are unreliable on any single document. They flag plain, well-organized writing. They flag writers whose first language isn't English at noticeably higher rates. So if a detector says your page was AI-written, that means very little. What's different here is scale. Across hundreds of millions of pages, the aggregate signal carries information that any individual verdict doesn't. So treat it as a soft one third, and pay attention to the direction rather than the decimal. And the direction is not really in dispute. A large and rising share of new web pages have a model somewhere in the loop, drafting them or editing them. Three consequences, in order of how soon they bite. First, training data. Models trained on the post-2022 web are increasingly trained on the output of earlier models. Nobody knows yet how much of that is harmless and how much narrows what the next generation can do. Second, archives get valuable. Text with a verified human origin and a trustworthy date is now a scarce asset, and the people sitting on large pre-2022 corpora are sitting on something they may not have priced. Third, and this one affects you today: search. If the top ten results for a question are ten lightly reworded versions of the same model's answer, then agreement across sources stops being evidence of anything. Corroboration only means something when the sources are independent, and a shared model is not independence.

The web used to be a sample of what people wrote. It is now partly a sample of what models wrote, and we are busily feeding it back in as training data. The word for that in every other engineering discipline is feedback loop, and they are usually something you design against.

## Two releases today aimed at the layer underneath your AI stack. One is a programming language finally letting go, and one is Google trying to change how text gets generated at all.

Mojo is now open source. Mojo is Modular's language, and the pitch from the very beginning was one language for the whole stack: code that looks like Python at the top, compiling to fast GPU and CPU code at the bottom. The problem it targets is real and expensive. Right now you write your model in Python and your fast kernels in CUDA C plus plus, and those are two languages, two toolchains, two sets of people, and a wall in between them. The technology was always interesting. The license was the blocker. A closed-source language is a bet that one company stays in business and stays friendly for the next decade, and infrastructure teams do not make that bet for something as foundational as their programming language. Open sourcing removes the single biggest reason people said no. What it does not remove is everything already occupying that space: CUDA's enormous head start, plus Triton, plus PyTorch's compiler, all of which already let you write fast kernels in something Python-shaped, and all of which have years of working code behind them. Mojo now gets judged purely on whether it is better. That is the fight it wanted, and it couldn't have it while it was proprietary. Then there's DiffusionGemma, a full technical report from Google. Nearly every text model you use generates left to right, one token at a time, each one conditioned on everything before it. A diffusion language model works differently. It starts with noise across the entire output at once and refines the whole sequence over repeated passes. Two consequences you'd actually feel. One, tokens can be produced in parallel rather than strictly in sequence, which changes the latency math for long outputs. Two, because the model always sees the whole sequence while refining it, editing existing text and filling in a gap in the middle are natural operations, not workarounds. Diffusion for text has been the promising alternative for a couple of years without a mainstream release. A full technical report carrying the Gemma name says Google intends this as a product line, not a paper.

If diffusion text lands, the change you notice isn't quality. It's that waiting for tokens to trickle out one at a time stops being the fundamental shape of using a model.

## Now three projects that are all, in their own way, an argument against the chat box. A piano model, an editor, and a filter. Different problems, same frustration underneath.

First, my favorite thing on the front page today. Somebody trained a 125 million parameter transformer that autocompletes piano performances in real time. You play a few notes on a MIDI keyboard, and it continues what you played. It runs entirely on the phone, about 108 notes per second on an iPhone 15, through Core ML, Apple's on-device model runtime. The app is free. 125 million parameters is minuscule, thousands of times smaller than whatever you have open in a browser tab. That is the entire point. For a musical instrument, latency is the product. A model that answers in four hundred milliseconds over a network is worthless when you are playing, because you have already moved on. Small and local isn't a compromise here, it is the only way this thing can exist at all. And the framing the author uses is exactly right: it is Copilot, except the prompt is you playing. Second, a Show HN called Huzzah. The author has worked almost exclusively with coding agents since January and describes being worn out by them. Two specific complaints, both of which will land for anyone doing this daily. Writing a full English sentence for every small change is slower than just making the change. And past a certain codebase size, the agent starts confusing itself. So the design is: you write pseudocode, in whatever notation makes sense to you. On save, the editor turns it into real source code. And the pseudocode is kept alongside the generated code permanently, so your prompt becomes a stored record of what you meant. It's a proof of concept. But that last part is the good idea. Today your prompt is a message you send and lose. The reasoning behind the code evaporates, and next month you're reading generated code with no record of the intent that produced it. Huzzah puts the intent in the repository next to the output, versioned with it. Whether pseudocode is the right notation is a separate argument and I'm not sure it is. Durable intent is right regardless. Third, quickly, a tool named Vomit. It runs a second, cheaper model over a large model's output to strip out the padding: the restatement of your question, the hedging, the bulleted summary of what it just told you. The fact that this exists, and that people upvoted it, is a product review of the current generation of chat models. We are now paying for tokens twice, once to generate the filler and once to remove it.

The chat box was the shape AI took when nobody knew what else to do with it. Three years on, people are building instruments, editors, and filters instead. That is what a technology looks like when it stops being a demo.

## Quick round on plumbing. Three developer tool stories, and there's a common thread at the end.

Git at any scale. Git's original design quietly assumes a repository small enough to hold in your head and on your disk. Companies with a decade of history and a few thousand engineers slam into that assumption: clone times measured in hours, and a status command that takes long enough to break your concentration. The tooling to fix this now exists and is underused. Partial clone means you fetch the shape of history but download file contents only when something actually needs them. Sparse checkout means your working directory contains only the directories you personally touch, not all four million files. And a file system monitor watches for changes in the background, so git status doesn't have to walk the entire tree every time you ask. Put those three together and a genuinely enormous monorepo behaves like an ordinary repo. If somebody has been telling you that your repository has grown too large and must be split into forty smaller ones, that argument is much weaker in 2026 than it was in 2019, and splitting a repo has costs that never go away. Next, Go 1.27 gets generic methods. Go got generics back in 1.18 with a hole in them: a method could not have its own type parameter. Only the type it hangs off could. In practice that meant you could write a generic Map function, but you could not write a Map method on your own generic collection that returns a collection of a different element type. So Go's generic libraries came out as piles of standalone functions instead of chainable methods, and iterator libraries in particular ended up awkward in a way everyone noticed and nobody could fix. 1.27 closes it. This is the difference between Go has generics and you can finally write the library you actually wanted. Third, a short technical review of SpacetimeDB. SpacetimeDB inverts the usual game backend. Instead of a game server that talks to a database, your application logic runs inside the database, and clients subscribe to queries and receive live updates as data changes. What that buys you is real: a network hop disappears, and so does an entire category of state synchronization code, which is where multiplayer bugs live. My caution, and the review is worth reading for the fuller set of tradeoffs, is that a database which is also your application server is also one thing you cannot scale, upgrade, or replace independently. That is a genuine trade, not a free win.

The thread through all three: people deciding that the layer below them is negotiable. Git's scaling limits, Go's type system, the boundary between your app and your database. None of those are laws of physics, and this week three groups treated them accordingly.

## Two operations stories now, and they're both about trusting numbers you have no way to check.

A bug in running Codex on AWS Bedrock has been causing charges at ten times the correct amount. Set aside the specific mechanism, whether it's retries counted as fresh requests or a token multiplier applied in the wrong place. Sit with the part that generalizes: customers had no way to catch this except by noticing the bill. Token metering is a number the vendor computes, about work the vendor performed, reported by the vendor. There is no independent meter. In every other utility you buy, you can attach your own meter to the line and compare. With inference, you get an invoice and a shrug. So three things worth doing this afternoon if you spend real money on inference. Set hard spend limits at the account level, not soft alerts. Alert on daily deltas rather than monthly totals, because a monthly total tells you in week five. And log your own request and token counts on the client side, so that you have an independent number to hold up against theirs. None of that prevents an overcharge. It cuts the time between the bug starting and you finding out from a month to a day, and that is the difference between an annoyance and a real loss. Related, a postmortem for the August 17th outage is circulating today. I'd read it for structure rather than for the specific trigger, because the trigger is almost never the useful part. The useful part is the dependency you didn't know you had. And the middle chapter of nearly every large outage now reads the same way: something in the control plane fails, every client retries at once, those retries become the dominant load, and recovery is then blocked by the traffic that is trying to recover. If your service retries without a budget and without jitter, that middle chapter is sitting in your codebase waiting.

Both stories reduce to the same instruction. Instrument the things you're currently taking on faith, before the bill or the pager teaches you to.

## Last block, three stories from outside the terminal. Orbital garbage, a robotics supply chain getting cut, and Meta shipping somebody else's product for free.

SpaceX first, and this is a genuinely strange one. Musk has talked about a constellation of a million satellites operating as AI data centers in orbit. Ars did the thing nobody does with these announcements, which is work out the end of life math. Data center GPUs last about five years. A million satellites therefore means roughly two hundred thousand reaching end of life every single year. According to the FCC filing from May 29th, about forty thousand of those would deorbit and burn up in the atmosphere. Burning up is not disposal. It is vaporizing refined metal into the upper atmosphere, where it disperses and settles slowly over the whole planet, and the aluminum in particular is expected to deplete ozone by an amount nobody has quantified, over decades. The other hundred and sixty thousand or so would be pushed outward into a distant disposal orbit. Permanent. Unreachable. Never recycled. For scale on how fast this stuff moves: Starlink by itself has already doubled the total mass of objects in low Earth orbit. And here's the framing that stuck with me. The commercial space industry loves talking about mining asteroids to bring precious metals down to Earth. This is that pipeline running backwards. Refined silicon, copper, gallium and aluminum, permanently exported off the planet, two hundred thousand units a year. Will it happen at that scale? Almost certainly not. But the FCC filing is real, and the disposal plan written in it is put it somewhere we can't reach. Robots. RoboStore has been the main North American distributor for Unitree, the Chinese company making the most affordable humanoid robots and robot dogs on the market. After the US government's crackdown on foreign-made robots, RoboStore is pivoting to building its own robots at a facility on Long Island. Their track record: over fifteen hundred robots sold and deployed, to Cisco, OpenAI, Nvidia, and more than a hundred and fifty universities. Harvard and MIT bought Unitree hardware through them. The thing to understand is that Unitree's advantage was price. These were cheap enough that a university lab could buy several and accept breaking one. A new Long Island factory will not reproduce that price, not in the first few years. So the open question for American robotics research is what happens when the affordable platform everyone standardized on becomes unavailable. Either somebody ships a cheap domestic equivalent quickly, or a lot of labs quietly slow down, and that second outcome doesn't make headlines. Finally, Meta has a new Mac app whose headline feature is dictation that works across all your apps. That is the same feature as Wispr Flow, Superwhisper, and Monologue, which are established paid products with real users. Meta's angle isn't the feature, it's distribution and price. When a company with Meta's balance sheet ships your entire product for free as a front door to its assistant, that is the whole competitive story, and there's no clever response available to a small paid app.

Dictation is the hook. Talking to your applications, with Meta in the middle of it, is the thing they actually want.

That's TechCast Daily for Friday, August 21st. If you take one thing away, make it this: the Rust crate and the Grok exfiltration are the same lesson at two different altitudes. Anything that gets to run, runs with your permissions, and it does not matter whether it arrived as a build script or as a sentence in an email. So go look at what your build runs before your tests do, and go look at what your assistant can reach and where it can send things. Back tomorrow. Thanks for listening.
