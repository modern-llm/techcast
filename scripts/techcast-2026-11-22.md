# A Riemann Claim, Anthropic's Debut Week, and Two Million Tripwires
*November 22, 2026*

A mathematician at Anthropic says Claude helped prove the Riemann hypothesis, the most famous unsolved problem in mathematics. In the same week, a rival claim appeared from someone else. We'll sort out what has actually been claimed and what still has to be checked. Then Anthropic's first days as a public company, a privacy experiment with two million tripwires, and why Elon Musk's lenders want SpaceX's chips kept on the ground. This is TechCast Daily for Sunday, November 22.

## Start with the claim everyone in mathematics is talking about. Levent Alpöge, a mathematician who works at Anthropic, has posted a document claiming a proof of the Riemann hypothesis, worked out with help from Claude. If it holds up, it is one of the biggest results in the history of the field. That is a big if.

First, what the problem is. Prime numbers, the ones divisible only by one and themselves, look scattered at random. In 1859, Bernhard Riemann suggested they follow a hidden pattern, tied to a function now called the zeta function. The hypothesis says the special points where that function equals zero all sit on a single straight line. If that is true, we know almost exactly how evenly the primes are spread out. Thousands of published results start with the words, assume the Riemann hypothesis is true. It is one of the seven Millennium Prize problems, with a million dollars from the Clay Mathematics Institute for a correct proof, and it has gone unsolved for 167 years. Now the claim. According to The Verge, Alpöge credits Claude with real help, and writes of texting Anthropic's chief executive, Dario Amodei, about the result. Anthropic itself has not commented and is not part of the announcement. The PDF that spread on Hacker News goes further than the headlines. It claims a proof of the generalized Riemann hypothesis, a broader version that covers a whole family of similar functions and includes the original as one case. No independent mathematician has confirmed it yet. Then it got stranger. A second paper appeared almost at once, posted as a hurried announcement, claiming the generalized version under the name Alessandro Masullo. TechCrunch describes it as a competing human solution. So a problem unsolved for a century and a half may have been solved twice in one week, once with an AI and once without. Or neither proof survives. Right now there is no way to tell. Here is why caution is the right default. Checking a claimed proof of a problem this hard usually takes experts months, sometimes years. Famous claims fail all the time. In 2018, the celebrated mathematician Michael Atiyah presented a Riemann proof that experts dismissed within days. AI has its own record here too. Last year, OpenAI executives said GPT-5 had solved several open Erdős problems, puzzles from a famous list left by Paul Erdős, and it turned out the model had mostly found answers already sitting in old papers. The Verge calls OpenAI's forays into the field often inelegant. The other side has a point too. A Hacker News post is titled, with obvious irony, the stochastic parrots have apparently proved the Riemann hypothesis. Stochastic parrot is the old put-down for language models: that they only stitch text together without understanding any of it. And this lands in a week full of similar claims, including Claude reportedly solving two open problems about black holes, and a new processor design called Frostbite, said to be discovered, designed and checked by AI. If Alpöge's proof holds, the bigger question is what Claude actually did. Checking cases, suggesting a known technique, or finding the key idea are very different contributions, and Alpöge's document is the place to look, not the headlines. That difference decides whether this is a story about a mathematician with a good tool, or about a tool doing mathematics.

My take: the timing matters. This arrives days after Anthropic's stock market debut, from an Anthropic employee, so expect sharp questions about hype. But a proof is either right or wrong, and the checking will happen in public. Watch whether established number theorists quickly point to a gap. If weeks pass and they are still working through it, that is the better sign.

## Which brings us to the stock. Last episode we had the final price, 83 dollars a share, above the range. Here is what happened once it started trading.

Anthropic opened on the Nasdaq at 111 dollars, 33 percent above its offering price. The offering raised more than 80 billion dollars in new money, roughly three times the previous record for a stock market debut, set by Saudi Aramco in 2019. A first-day jump that size means demand was far higher than the price assumed. Early buyers got a quick gain, and Anthropic could have raised even more at a higher price. Most popular debuts go that way. The second day was different. The whole Nasdaq fell far enough to wipe out all its gains for 2026, in a broad sell-off of AI stocks, and that weighed on Anthropic's second session. We only have the headline, not the size of Anthropic's move, so we won't guess at a number. What matters is that the debut did not lift the rest of the sector. By the end of the second day, investors were selling AI stocks, not buying them.

Which raises the bigger question about Anthropic as a public company: can it pay its enormous computing bills and keep its safety promises, with shareholders watching every quarter?

## Two pieces this week tried to answer that. One is a conversation on The Verge's Decoder podcast between Nilay Patel and the site's senior AI reporter, Hayden Field. The other is a TechCrunch analysis of where Anthropic's money actually goes.

Start with the bills. Decoder puts Anthropic's computing commitments at roughly 900 billion dollars over the coming years. That is money promised for chips, data centers and cloud capacity, and the 80 billion just raised covers less than a tenth of it. The rest has to come from revenue that keeps growing very fast, or from more borrowing and more stock sales. TechCrunch's point is about who wins either way. Amazon and Google are both large investors in Anthropic, and both sell it the computing power it runs on. Money goes from the cloud companies into Anthropic as investment, then comes back to them as payment for chips and servers. People call this circular financing. The awkward part for new shareholders is that Amazon and Google keep getting paid for the computers even if Anthropic falls behind OpenAI or Google's own models. The people who just bought shares at 111 dollars carry that risk. The cloud companies mostly do not. Decoder's point is about safety. Anthropic is a public benefit corporation, which means its directors are legally allowed to weigh a stated mission alongside profit. It also has a long-term benefit trust, a group that picks some of its board members and is meant to hold the company to that mission. After the Jensen Huang saga at Nvidia, how a company is governed is getting more attention. The tension Field and Patel describe is simple. Anthropic's pitch to big customers rests on being the careful lab. So safety is both the mission and the sales pitch. That works while the two agree. The test comes when they don't, say, a model that would sell well but that the safety team wants held back. One more observation from the conversation: OpenAI seems happy to let Anthropic go first. That makes sense. Anthropic now has to publish its finances every quarter, and OpenAI gets to read them, and to watch how the market prices an AI lab, before making its own move. If Anthropic's numbers look good, OpenAI can sell shares to eager buyers. If they look bad, OpenAI can wait.

My view: the rules on paper are real, but nobody knows how they hold until money is at stake. The first bad quarter will tell us more than any charter.

According to unnamed sources, the Securities and Exchange Commission's investigation of Nvidia is looking at possible accounting problems. We only have the headline, so we don't know which numbers the regulators are questioning. But last episode we walked through how Nvidia has been investing in, and lending to, companies that buy its chips. When a supplier funds its own customers, accountants ask whether those sales are as solid as they look. That would be a natural place for the SEC to look, though no reporting yet says it is. For a company already without its founder as chief executive, an accounting inquiry is the more serious kind of trouble. A personal scandal costs a leader. A problem with the books can force a company to correct past results.

Now, from where the money goes to where your data goes.

## If you build AI apps, this is the story to read in full. Security researcher HD Moore, best known for creating the Metasploit security testing toolkit, ran an experiment on the companies that sit between apps and AI models. The result, in the words of Ars Technica, is an ecosystem that leaks sensitive data like a sieve.

First, the middlemen. Many AI apps don't send your prompt straight to Anthropic or OpenAI. They send it to a router, also called a gateway, such as OpenRouter or Vercel. The router passes each request on to whichever model provider is cheapest or best suited, and there are many smaller providers in that pool. Each one is another company that sees what you typed. Moore's test used canaries, meaning fake secrets that raise an alarm the moment anyone uses them. Two million of them: Amazon Web Services access keys that worked but opened empty accounts, passwords for remote login, unique web addresses, and references to private code repositories. Every one was brand new. Each was placed in exactly one prompt, sent to exactly one provider, and Moore controlled every server those secrets pointed to. So if anyone ever tried a key or visited an address, the record showed exactly which provider it had leaked from. The tripwires went off. Ars describes security breaches, stolen prompts and privacy violations along that chain. A second post on Hacker News, titled Data Processing Incidents on Inference Providers, points the same way, though we only have its title. Inference providers are the companies that actually run the models. Why this matters: people treat a prompt like a private conversation with one company. It isn't. With a router in the middle, it passes through the router, then a provider you've never heard of, then whatever logging that provider runs. For companies, routers are also a contract problem. If your app promises customers their data goes only to one well-known AI company, a router can quietly break that promise. And coding agents make it worse, because they read your files and settings and can pull keys into prompts without asking. Two episodes ago we covered tools for keeping secrets away from coding agents. This experiment is the reason to use them.

The practical steps: never put a live key or password in a prompt. If one has gone through a router, change it. And check whether your router lets you limit requests to providers that promise not to keep your data. OpenRouter has such a setting. Turn it on.

## Speaking of software that sees everything you do, the race to run your computer for you reached the Mac this week.

Google updated Gemini with an agent for the Mac, meaning a version of the assistant that takes actions instead of just answering. It can organize files, fill out forms, and work through tasks with several steps, clicking and typing for you. An agent that can fill out forms can also fill out the wrong one. Before you turn one loose on your files, check what it is allowed to touch. Separately, a TechCrunch report says Apple appears to be building its own agent to run the Mac, to compete with Meta's Muse, Claude, OpenAI's Dots and others. That one is unconfirmed. Here is why the two belong together. Agents from outside companies generally work by reading the screen and driving the mouse and keyboard, using the permissions macOS gives accessibility tools. That works, but it is slow, it breaks when a button moves, and it lets the agent see everything on screen. Apple owns the operating system. Its own agent could work through apps directly and ask permission one task at a time. If Apple ships that, Google's agent becomes the second choice on Apple's own computers.

And an agent that reads your screen is exactly what one messaging app is now trying to block.

## Signal is limiting screenshots in its Android and iPhone apps. The company frames it as a response to what it calls the existential threat of AI surveillance.

The worry is not your friend taking a screenshot. It is the phone itself. Operating systems and AI assistants increasingly capture what is on screen so they can summarize it, search it, or act on it, the way the agents in our last story do. A message that is encrypted end to end, meaning only you and the other person can read it in transit, is exposed again once an AI feature on your phone reads it off the display and sends it to a server. Signal has been here before. It has offered an optional screenshot block on Android for years. Last year it added a block on Windows against Microsoft's Recall feature, which takes regular snapshots of the screen. Signal's president, Meredith Whittaker, has been warning for over a year that AI agents with deep access to your device threaten private messaging. This is that warning turned into a feature, on both phone platforms at once. The report gives few details on how strict the limits are, so we can't say yet what changes for you day to day. There are trade-offs. People screenshot chats to save an address, or to report harassment. And nothing stops someone from photographing the screen with a second phone. The block is aimed at software, not people.

My guess: other privacy-focused apps follow, and the question of which software is allowed to read your screen becomes a major privacy fight.

## Now, where data centers are allowed to go. This week, the answer got narrower from three directions: not abroad, not in many American towns, and, if SpaceX's lenders get their way, not in orbit.

President Trump said on Friday that companies building data centers outside the United States would face a tremendous tax. Speaking after a summit with China's Xi Jinping and Russia's Vladimir Putin, the president said, we're not going to allow our companies to build data centers outside of our country, and compared it to how chip manufacturing moved overseas. We have no detail yet on how such a tax would work or what it would cover. This collides with how the big cloud companies actually operate. Microsoft, Google and Amazon run data centers all over the world, often because governments and customers abroad want their data kept inside their own borders. Europe is the obvious case. OpenAI has a huge project in the United Arab Emirates. A tax on all of that would hit American companies' ability to sell abroad, not just where they build. And at home, as The Verge notes, states and cities are restricting new data center applications, often over electricity prices and water use. So the policy is build here, while the places that would host the buildings are increasingly saying no. Then there's orbit. Elon Musk has talked up data centers in space, satellites packed with AI chips and cooled by the vacuum. That part is harder than it sounds, since a vacuum doesn't carry heat away. Satellites shed heat only through large radiators. SpaceX just took on a 43 billion dollar loan package secured by its advanced AI chips, meaning the lenders can seize and sell those chips if SpaceX doesn't pay. According to the Financial Times, the lenders tried to bar SpaceX from launching that collateral into space. SpaceX says these chips will be used on Earth. The lenders' concern is simple: you can't repossess a chip that's in orbit. It sounds like a joke, but it shows that AI chips now back huge loans, the way houses or aircraft do. That only works if the chips keep their resale value and stay where a lender can reach them.

Put the three together and the message to AI companies is: build in America, in a town that will have you, on the ground. Those towns are getting harder to find.

## Before we go, two research titles from Hacker News. We only have the titles, so treat these as pointers, not findings.

The first is called Language Models Can Rewrite Their Own Weights. Weights are the billions of numbers a model learns in training. They normally stay fixed once a model ships, which is why a chatbot doesn't remember last week's conversation unless it's fed back in. A model that could safely change its own weights would keep learning after release. That would be useful, and it would also make a model much harder to test, because the thing you checked yesterday may not be the thing running today. The second is called LLMs Can Read and Write Executables Byte by Byte From a Hex Dump. An executable is a ready-to-run program, and a hex dump is that program written out as raw bytes, two characters per byte. If models can read and edit programs at that level, with no source code and no compiler, that helps people who take apart old software, like the Jeep engine computer we covered last week. It also helps people who write malware.

Both deserve a careful read before anyone builds on them.
