# TechCast Daily — When the Machine Finds the Counterexample
*July 20, 2026*

An AI just did something no human mathematician managed in eighty-seven years — it broke the Jacobian Conjecture. Meanwhile, a new study says AI advice makes ordinary people worse at their jobs while feeling like geniuses. Hold both of those in your head at once, because that tension is the whole show today.

## Let's start with the big one. The Jacobian Conjecture is one of those problems that sounds innocent and has quietly humiliated the field since 1939. It's from algebraic geometry, and roughly it says: if you have a polynomial map whose Jacobian — its derivative determinant — is a nonzero constant, then the map is invertible, and its inverse is also a polynomial. It's been proven in special cases, resisted in general, and even landed on Steve Smale's famous list of problems for the century.

This week, Claude Fable produced a counterexample. Not a proof of the conjecture — a counterexample, meaning it constructed an explicit case where the conjecture fails, which if it holds up ends the question in the other direction entirely. And here's what makes this different from the usual AI-does-math headline. This wasn't pattern-matching a known result or regurgitating a textbook. A counterexample to a conjecture this old is, by definition, an object that no human had found despite decades of very smart people looking. The model had to generate a specific mathematical structure and then verify it actually violates the conjecture's conditions. Now, the honest caveat: extraordinary claims in mathematics get checked hard, and the community will spend the coming weeks confirming the construction is genuinely valid and not a subtle error that looks like a breakthrough. That verification is the real test, not the announcement.

But even with the asterisk, notice the shift. We've moved from AI that helps mathematicians to AI that produces results mathematicians missed. That's a category change, and it's why this is the lead.

## Now hold that triumph next to a study that landed the same week, because the contrast is the story. A group of researchers looked at what happens when regular people use AI advice to make decisions.

The finding: AI advice made people less accurate, but more confident. Read that twice. The tool didn't just fail to help — it actively degraded the quality of people's judgments while simultaneously making them feel more certain they were right. That combination is the dangerous one. An error you're unsure about, you double-check. An error you're confident about, you ship. The mechanism here is basically automation bias dressed up in fluent language. When a model gives you a smooth, articulate answer, the fluency reads as competence, and you outsource your own scrutiny. The confidence is contagious even when the correctness isn't. And this is exactly the population that matters — not experts who can smell a wrong answer, but everyday users making everyday calls with a very persuasive assistant in their ear.

So the same technology that just cracked an eighty-seven-year-old conjecture also reliably makes your average person worse at their job and happier about it. Both things are true. The lesson isn't that AI is smart or dumb — it's that the fluency and the reliability are two separate dials, and the interface only shows you one of them.

## Let's talk about the physical reality underneath all of this, because demand for AI is now colliding with the limits of the real world in three different ways at once. Start with Moonshot AI.

Moonshot suspended new subscriptions entirely because of demand for its Kimi K3 model. Not a soft rollout, not a waitlist — they stopped selling. When a company turns off the revenue tap, that's not a marketing flex, that's a capacity wall. They physically cannot serve more users without degrading the ones they have. Now zoom out, because Moonshot isn't alone in feeling this squeeze. OpenAI quietly cut the context window on its Codex model from 372,000 tokens down to 272,000 — that's a hundred thousand tokens of working memory, gone. They didn't frame it as a downgrade, but that's what it is for anyone feeding large codebases to the model, and the most likely reason is the same one that stopped Moonshot's signups: serving giant context windows is expensive in compute and memory, and when you're capacity-constrained, shrinking the context is how you fit more users through the same hardware. And then there's the most physical version of this story — power companies in the United States are now using eminent domain to seize private land for data centers.

String those together. Startups turning off signups, incumbents quietly clipping their own products, and utilities invoking the government's power to take your land — all three are the same pressure wave. The bottleneck for AI stopped being algorithms a while ago. It's now transformers, transmission lines, and square footage. When the state is condemning farmland so a data center can exist, the abstraction has fully touched the ground.

## That capacity crunch shows up in your daily developer experience too, and this next thread nails the frustration. Somebody on Hacker News just asked, plainly, what's the deal with all the random weekly quota resets for AI agents lately.

If you've been using coding agents, you've felt this. The limits move, the resets feel arbitrary, and nobody tells you the rules. And it connects directly to what we just talked about — when providers are capacity-constrained, quotas become the release valve. They throttle you not because your work changed but because their supply did. Here's the maddening part, and it's the second half of this segment: developers are now spending real money trying to spend less money. There's a great confession making the rounds titled, roughly, I burned all my tokens researching how to save tokens. Which is exactly as absurd as it sounds — the meta-work of optimizing your token usage now costs enough tokens to matter. That's a sign of a pricing model that's leaking effort. When the unit economics are opaque and the limits shift under you, rational users respond by building elaborate rituals to game a system they can't see. That's wasted human attention, and it's a direct tax the current agent-pricing designs are imposing on their best customers.

The uncomfortable takeaway for the tool builders: if your power users are writing essays about your quota system and burning budget to reverse-engineer it, that's not engagement. That's a design failure with a smile painted on it.

## Let's get out of the software squeeze and into a genuinely different idea about how to compute at all. Researchers just fired up what's being called the biggest probabilistic computer.

Here's the concept, and it's delightfully counterintuitive. Conventional computers treat noise as the enemy — the entire discipline of digital design is about suppressing randomness so a one stays a one. A probabilistic computer does the opposite. It uses controllable noise as a computational resource. The building block is a p-bit, which instead of being pinned to zero or one, fluctuates between them with a tunable probability. Wire enough of these together and the system naturally settles toward low-energy states, which turns out to map beautifully onto exactly the problems classical computers hate: optimization, sampling, and the kind of statistical inference that sits at the heart of machine learning. Think of it as a physics-based shortcut — instead of grinding through a search, you let the hardware's own thermal jitter roll toward the answer. It's often pitched as a room-temperature cousin of quantum computing, without the exotic cooling, because it exploits classical randomness rather than quantum superposition.

Why care today? Because we just spent ten minutes on how AI is starved for conventional compute. If a chip that turns noise into answers can do inference-style workloads more efficiently, this stops being a curiosity and starts being a pressure-release for the exact bottleneck squeezing everyone. Early days, but it's the right kind of weird.

## Quick tooling round, because a few things this week are small but telling about where the craft is heading. First, Claude Code.

Claude Code now runs on Bun, which is itself written in Rust. That's a mouthful, so here's why it's interesting: the AI-agent frontier and the boring-but-brutal world of performance engineering are converging. Bun is the fast JavaScript runtime, Rust is the memory-safe systems language everyone reaches for when milliseconds and reliability both matter, and picking that stack for an AI coding tool signals that agent latency and startup time now matter enough to rebuild the plumbing. On the model-training side, there's a project called LoRA Speedrun — a public, wall-clock leaderboard for fine-tuning techniques. That framing matters. Fine-tuning research is drowning in claims, and a leaderboard that ranks by actual elapsed time rather than theoretical FLOPs cuts through a lot of hand-waving. And for the language-nerds, there's new work on a unified intermediate representation for Julia, which is the unglamorous compiler-internals plumbing that determines whether a scientific-computing language stays fast as it grows.

The through-line: as AI eats more of the workflow, the infrastructure underneath it — runtimes, compilers, honest benchmarks — becomes the competitive edge. Nobody tweets about it, and it's where the real leverage lives.

## Now to the corporate chessboard, where two moves this week tell you how the AI hardware wars are actually going to be fought — in courtrooms and in Tokyo.

First, Apple has a lawsuit that could hang over OpenAI's hardware ambitions. OpenAI has been very publicly working toward a physical device — a piece of consumer hardware to be the front door to its AI — and the question being debated is whether an Apple legal challenge could slow or complicate that, right as OpenAI is also eyeing public markets. It's a reminder that the hard part of shipping hardware often isn't the engineering, it's the intellectual-property gauntlet an incumbent like Apple can throw in your path. Second move: Jensen Huang, Nvidia's CEO, left Japan after a visit that produced deals spanning the entire Japanese tech ecosystem. That's the tell. Huang isn't selling chips one customer at a time anymore — he's wiring whole national tech economies into Nvidia's platform. When the most valuable company's leader does a country-wide sweep and comes home with agreements across the board, he's not making sales, he's setting the default. Countries that standardize on your stack today are locked in for a decade.

Two very different plays, same board. Apple uses law to defend its turf, Nvidia uses partnerships to expand its gravity well, and OpenAI is trying to squeeze a hardware business out between them. The AI race at the top is less about models now and more about who controls the ground everyone else has to build on.

## Two closing stories, and they're a palate cleanser — proof that ambitious engineering doesn't only happen inside trillion-dollar companies. First, from India.

Skyroot Aerospace's Vikram-1 rocket reached orbit on its debut flight — India's first fully commercial, privately developed satellite launcher. It lifted off from the Sriharikota spaceport, pushed through a last-minute technical delay, and delivered on target into a 280-mile-high orbit. It's a modest rocket by design, about 72 feet tall, carrying up to 770 pounds to low-Earth orbit — which actually puts it a notch above Rocket Lab's Electron, the most successful small-satellite launcher in the world. The significance isn't the size. It's that India now has a private company that can independently reach orbit, breaking what was effectively a government monopoly on space access there. First orbital attempts fail all the time; nailing it on debut is genuinely rare.

A new commercial launch provider entering the market is exactly the kind of competition that drags the cost of getting to space down for everyone. Watch this one.

## And to send you off — my favorite story of the week, because it's a masterclass in what one determined engineer can do against an absurd status quo. A guy bought an abandoned eight-lane bowling center in the rural Midwest.

His scorekeeping system was installed in 2008 and cost six figures. If you wanted to forklift-replace it with a modern equivalent, quotes ran eighty to a hundred twenty thousand dollars — for a one-to-one swap, with every feature billed as a separate line item, no service contract included. Replacement parts alone were four thousand dollars per pair of lanes. For context, the man bought the entire building for a hundred and five thousand. So the scoring computer costs about as much as the bowling alley. Instead of paying it, he rebuilt the whole thing himself using ESP32 microcontrollers — those two-dollar wifi chips — for about sixteen hundred dollars total. And remember what these systems actually do: camera-based pin detection, calculating ball speed and trajectory, driving the pinsetter and the ball return and the animations. He replicated a hundred-twenty-thousand-dollar object detection and control system with a handful of hobbyist chips and, in his words, learned that hardware is not so hard.

That's the note I want to end on. On one side of today's show, we've got AI cracking eighty-seven-year-old math and utilities seizing land to feed data centers. On the other, one SRE with a leaky roof and sixteen hundred dollars in parts humbling a six-figure incumbent system. Both are the frontier. The big one gets the headlines, but the small one is where most of us actually get to play.
