# Check the Proof: Machine-Verified Math, Copied Models, and the New Price of Code
*September 11, 2026*

OpenAI just attached a machine-checked proof to a Navier-Stokes result, so you can check the math yourself instead of trusting a press release. Meanwhile Anthropic says three Chinese labs have been copying its models, Cognition's new coding model is posting frontier-level numbers, and Shopify is walking away from the framework it championed for six years. This is TechCast Daily for Friday, September 11th, 2026. Let's get into it.

## Let's start with math, because this one is genuinely unusual. OpenAI put out a release on the Navier-Stokes equations, and the detail that caught everyone's attention is that it shipped with a formal proof written in Lean 4.

Quick context. The Navier-Stokes equations describe how fluids move: air over a wing, water in a pipe, blood through an artery. Engineers solve them numerically every day. But nobody has proven, in three dimensions, whether smooth solutions always stay smooth, or whether a flow can blow up, meaning some quantity like velocity shoots to infinity in a finite amount of time. That question is one of the Clay Institute's seven Millennium Prize problems. It carries a million dollar prize, and only one of the seven has ever been solved. Now the important caveat. The headline doesn't tell you exactly what was proven. It could be the full problem, a special case, a simplified model equation, or a construction of one specific blowup scenario. Those are wildly different claims, so read the theorem statement before you celebrate or scoff. Here's why the Lean part matters either way. Lean is a proof assistant: a programming language where a proof is code, and a small, heavily scrutinized checker verifies every logical step. If the file compiles, the argument is correct relative to its definitions. No referee has to squint at page eighty. That is a real change in how AI science claims get judged. Last fall, an OpenAI executive's claim that its model had solved open Erdős problems turned out to mean the model had found solutions already sitting in published papers. A Lean proof removes that whole category of argument. One weak spot remains. Lean checks the proof, not whether you formalized the right question. If a definition is subtly off, you can have a flawless proof of the wrong theorem. The good news is that experts now audit a page of definitions instead of a hundred pages of reasoning. And formalizing this kind of analysis in Lean has historically been slow, painful work, so the fact that this file exists at all tells you how far AI-assisted formalization has come.

My take: the Lean file matters more than the announcement. It turns 'trust us' into 'check it yourself,' and every lab making science claims should be held to that bar from now on.

## From proofs anyone can check to accusations outsiders mostly can't. Anthropic published its latest report on detecting and countering AI misuse yesterday, and the part getting the most attention names three Chinese companies: Alibaba, Moonshot AI, and DeepSeek.

The accusation is distillation. That means training your own model on a stronger model's answers. You send huge volumes of prompts, save the responses, and use them as training data, so your model learns to imitate the other one. Inside a single company that's a normal technique. It's how a lot of small, cheap models get made. The problem is doing it to someone else's model, which every major lab's terms of service forbid. Anthropic describes these campaigns as persistent and says they've escalated in recent months as competition has intensified. The fight itself isn't new. Early last year, OpenAI said it had evidence DeepSeek used its outputs. What stands out here is the list. Moonshot and DeepSeek are fast-moving startups, but Alibaba is one of the largest companies in China and already builds strong models of its own under the Qwen name. Why this matters goes well beyond hurt feelings. First, economics. Frontier labs spend enormous sums on training, and if a competitor can capture a real slice of that capability for the price of API calls, the lead shrinks. Second, policy. US export controls limit which chips Chinese labs can buy, and distillation lets you benefit from compute you never had to own. Anthropic has been one of the loudest supporters of those controls, so expect this report to show up in that debate. Third, safety. A distilled copy can pick up capabilities without inheriting the safeguards around them. Now the fair counterpoints. Outsiders can't independently check the account data behind these attributions. And plenty of people will find it rich that AI labs, which trained on the open web without asking, are now complaining about copying. The two situations aren't identical. One is reading public pages. The other is breaking a contract you agreed to in order to get API access. But the optics are what they are.

This lands in Washington long before it lands in a courtroom. Proving distillation to a judge is hard. Explaining it to a senator worried about China takes about thirty seconds.

## Staying on security, but this one needs action today, not a policy debate. If you self-host your Git server, check your version right now.

Forgejo, the community-run fork of Gitea and the software behind Codeberg, has a critical remote code execution vulnerability in version 16.0.3 and everything before it. Remote code execution means an attacker can run their own commands on your server. A Git server is one of the worst places for that. It holds your source code, and often deploy keys, build pipeline secrets, and webhooks that reach straight into production. So, what to do. Upgrade to a release newer than 16.0.3 today. If you can't, take the instance off the public internet or put it behind a VPN until you can. Read the Forgejo advisory to see which feature is affected and whether an attacker needs an account first. And if your instance was publicly reachable, rotate the secrets stored in it, because you can't prove nobody got in before you patched.

Self-hosting buys you control. The price is that the patch window is your job, not GitHub's.

## Now to coding agents. Cognition, the company behind the Devin agent, which bought Windsurf last year, has launched its own model called SWE-2. It's being pitched as a rival to Anthropic's Fable 5.1 and OpenAI's GPT-Astra.

The headline number is 92.8 on Terminal-Bench 2.1. Terminal-Bench drops an agent into a real command line and gives it jobs like compiling a stubborn project, configuring a server, or repairing a broken environment. Automated tests check whether the job actually got done, which makes it much harder to game than a multiple-choice benchmark. A score near 93 tells you two things. SWE-2 is strong, and the benchmark is running out of room. When the top models cluster in the nineties, the remaining failures tend to be ambiguous or flaky tasks, and the gaps between models stop meaning much. That's why these benchmarks keep getting new versions. The bigger story is strategy. For the past few years, coding tool companies like Cursor, Windsurf, and Cognition built on models from Anthropic and OpenAI. They paid those labs for every token, then watched the same labs launch competing tools. Owning your model fixes both problems at once: better margins, and nobody upstream who can raise your prices or cut you off. Cursor made the same move with its own Composer model last year. The companies once dismissed as thin wrappers around someone else's model are becoming model companies. The caveats: these are vendor-reported numbers, the 'rivaling' framing comes from launch coverage rather than independent testing, and a model tuned hard for coding benchmarks can still stumble on a messy real codebase. Speed and price per finished task will matter as much as the score.

A benchmark gets a model onto your shortlist. Your own repo decides whether it stays. Point it at a week of real tickets before you switch anything.

## Which raises a practical question: where should these agents actually run? Two stories this week point in opposite directions.

First, OpenAI launched an Agents API. Public details are still thin, but the direction fits OpenAI's path so far. The Assistants API came first, then the Responses API and the Agents SDK in 2025, and each step moved more of the agent loop onto OpenAI's side. The agent loop is the cycle where the model calls a tool, reads the result, and decides what to do next. An API built around agents points toward OpenAI running that whole loop for you, including the state and the tool calls. The upside is less plumbing to build and maintain. The downside is lock-in. Your agent's memory and history live on OpenAI's servers, and when something goes wrong at step forty, your debugging options are whatever OpenAI chooses to expose. The second story goes the other way. A piece titled 'Nine coding harnesses versus your laptop' compares the tools that run coding agents on your own machine. A harness is the program wrapped around the model. It reads your files, runs commands, decides what goes into the model's context, and asks permission before doing anything risky. Claude Code, OpenAI's Codex command-line tool, Google's Gemini command-line tool, and open-source options like OpenCode and Aider all fall into this category. The reason to compare them on a laptop is the part people forget: the model runs in a data center, but the harness runs on your computer. It holds conversation state in memory, spawns processes, watches files, and runs your test suite. Run three or four agents in parallel and your laptop becomes the limit, not the API.

The sensible split: hosted agents for work that should keep running while you sleep, local harnesses for work where you want to watch every command. Most teams will end up with both, so pick deliberately instead of by default.

## Let's follow the money, and the megawatts, because three stories today are really one story about the cost of compute.

Start with Nvidia. Jensen Huang says the company will grow about 70 percent next year. For a company of Nvidia's size, that's an absurd number. Most giant companies celebrate growth in the low teens. The demand is coming from everywhere at once: cloud providers, AI labs, governments buying national compute, and ordinary enterprises. Huang also took on the criticism that has followed Nvidia for about a year, that its deals are circular. Nvidia has invested in companies like OpenAI and CoreWeave, which then buy enormous amounts of Nvidia hardware. The worry is that part of Nvidia's revenue is its own money coming back around. Huang insists that isn't what's happening. The honest test is a number, not a quote: how much of next year's revenue comes from customers Nvidia helped fund. That's the figure critics want disclosed. Next, where the power comes from. Google has agreed to buy half the electricity from one of Finland's nuclear power plants. Google already runs a large data center in Hamina, Finland, inside a converted paper mill cooled with seawater from the Gulf of Finland. The country offers cold air, a stable grid, and a lot of carbon-free power. Nuclear matters to data centers because it runs around the clock, unlike solar and wind. This follows Microsoft's deal to restart a reactor at Three Mile Island, Amazon's deal with the Susquehanna plant in Pennsylvania, and Google's own bet on small modular reactors from Kairos Power. The difference is timing. This is an existing plant producing power now, not a reactor promised for the 2030s. Expect a local debate over whether one foreign company contracting that much clean power raises prices for Finnish households and industry. Finally, a technical write-up on compute-efficient pretraining and scaling to trillion-parameter models. Trillion-parameter models aren't exotic anymore. Moonshot's Kimi K2, the same Moonshot from the distillation story, has a trillion parameters. What makes that affordable is a design called mixture of experts. The model contains many specialized sub-networks and switches on only a few of them for each word, so Kimi K2 uses about 32 billion parameters per token instead of all trillion. At that scale, the hard problems are keeping training stable, spreading work evenly so some experts don't sit idle, and cutting the communication overhead between thousands of GPUs. Every bit of efficiency gained there means fewer chips and fewer megawatts per model.

But don't expect efficiency to slow Nvidia down. When training gets cheaper, companies train more models. That's been true every year of this boom, and it's the real reason Huang can say 70 percent with a straight face.

## Two big engineering organizations made public bets on programming languages this week, and I think they're reacting to the same underlying change.

Microsoft now treats Rust as a tier-1 language internally. At a big company, tier-1 generally means fully supported. It works in the standard build system, it has internal libraries and security tooling, and there are people whose job is to fix it when it breaks. In practice, a team can choose Rust without asking anyone's permission. The motivation goes back years. Microsoft's security team reported that roughly 70 percent of the vulnerabilities it patched were memory-safety bugs, things like reading past the end of a buffer or using memory after it's been freed. Rust's compiler rejects those bugs in ordinary Rust code before the program ever runs. Microsoft already ships Rust in parts of Windows and Azure. Tier-1 status turns that from a collection of projects into a default option. Meanwhile, Shopify is moving its mobile apps from React Native back to native code: Swift on iOS and Kotlin on Android. That's a notable reversal. React Native lets you write one JavaScript codebase that runs on both platforms. Back in 2020, Shopify publicly declared React Native the future of mobile at the company and rebuilt its flagship apps on it. It was the framework's most prominent big-company champion. Here's my read, and to be clear, this is my interpretation, not Shopify's stated reasoning. The main argument for cross-platform frameworks was always cost: one team, one codebase. Coding agents happen to be very good at exactly the work that building twice requires, like porting a feature from Swift to Kotlin with the same structure and the same tests. When that work gets cheap, the savings from a shared codebase shrink, but the downsides stay. You wait for the framework to support new iOS and Android features, you debug through an extra layer, and performance is close to native but not quite. The Rust decision follows the same logic. The biggest objection to Rust was always the cost of learning it and rewriting old code. If agents handle a big share of that translation, the objection weakens and the safety benefit remains.

The pattern to watch: decisions made years ago to save engineering hours are being re-made now that engineering hours cost less. Shopify won't be the last company to reverse one.

## Time for some under-the-hood reading: two pieces on where time quietly disappears, and one proposal to win some of it back.

First, a reminder that Python sets and dictionaries can take quadratic time. Everyone learns that dictionary lookups are constant time. That's the average case. A hash table places each key in a slot based on its hash. If many keys land in the same neighborhood, every insert and lookup has to step past the collisions, and inserting n items ends up taking time proportional to n squared. Quadratic means doubling the input quadruples the time, and ten times the input takes a hundred times as long. There are a few known routes there in Python. Strings are protected by hash randomization, which has been on by default since Python 3.3. But integers mostly hash to themselves, so someone who controls integer keys can choose values that collide on purpose. Deleted entries also leave placeholder markers that lookups still have to step over until the table gets rebuilt. The practical lesson: if an attacker can choose your keys, a dictionary is a potential denial-of-service. Second, a deep dive on what actually happens when a GPU writes memory. The reason to care is that in AI workloads, arithmetic is rarely the bottleneck. Moving bytes is. A single write can involve the GPU's own address translation, whether the memory is pinned, meaning locked in place so the operating system can't move it, and sometimes a trip across the PCI Express link into the CPU's memory. If your training run is slower than the spec sheet promises, that path is often where the time went. Third, Java. JEP 544, a JDK Enhancement Proposal, adds ahead-of-time code compilation. It's the next step in Project Leyden, OpenJDK's long effort to fix Java's slow startup and warmup. Normally the Java virtual machine starts by interpreting bytecode, watches which methods run hot, then compiles those to machine code while your service is already taking traffic. Peak speed is excellent, but the first seconds or minutes are slow, which hurts when containers scale up and down all day. Earlier Leyden work in JDK 24 and 25 let you cache class loading and profiling data from a training run. JEP 544 goes further and stores the compiled machine code itself, so a fresh process starts with its hot methods already compiled. Unlike GraalVM native images, which compile everything ahead of time but restrict dynamic features like reflection, this approach keeps normal Java behavior intact.

The common thread: 'constant time' and 'fast startup' describe the typical case, and production is exactly where the unusual cases show up.

## On the startup side, two companies show where the money goes once AI and robotics have to produce actual revenue.

Maven Robotics came out of stealth with a 100 million dollar Series A and, importantly, robots already deployed with customers. A typical Series A is a fraction of that size. The pitch, going by the headline, is aggressive: Maven wants to win robot deployment contracts that competitors think are already theirs. There isn't much public detail yet on Maven's robots or its customers, but the shape of the bet is clear. Robotics has no shortage of companies with impressive demo videos. The hard part is deployment: fitting into a real facility, meeting safety requirements, and keeping machines running at a guaranteed uptime. Launching with deployments instead of a sizzle reel is the strongest signal a robotics startup can send right now. Pocket FM is much further along. The Indian audio series app, known for serialized shows that run for hundreds of episodes, has doubled its revenue run rate to 500 million dollars. Run rate means current revenue projected over a full year. AI now produces 99 percent of its new content and 93 percent of its total library, and the company says production is about 80 times cheaper. Those two percentages aren't a contradiction: the library still includes older shows made by people. Eighty times cheaper changes the whole business model. You can launch far more shows, see which ones hook listeners, and cancel the rest without losing much.

Pocket FM is the clearest evidence so far that people will pay for AI-made entertainment. My guess is it works best in formats sold on plot hooks and cliffhangers, and worst where the writing itself is the product.

## We'll close with two essays that climbed Hacker News on the same day: 'Creativity is the new moat' and 'AI Is Breaking This Thing We Call Trust.' Read together, they describe one problem.

A moat, in business terms, is whatever stops competitors from copying you. The case in that first title is simple. If AI makes execution cheap, the code, the copy, even the audio, as Pocket FM just showed, then execution stops protecting anyone, and the scarce skill becomes knowing what's worth making. I agree with half of that. Taste and judgment matter more when building is cheap. But cheap building also means good ideas get cloned faster than ever. Today's distillation story is a literal example: capability that cost a fortune to build, allegedly copied for the price of API calls. So creativity gets you a head start, not a wall. The second essay covers the other side of cheap production. When anyone can generate a convincing email, voice message, product review, or bug report, the default assumption that something is genuine stops holding. The cost of checking shifts to whoever receives it. Open-source maintainers already live this. The curl project has complained publicly about floods of AI-generated security reports that sound plausible and are simply wrong. Voice-clone scams do the same thing to families and finance departments. The encouraging part is that the answer showed up at the top of today's episode. That Lean proof is what trust looks like when it has to scale: not a reputation, not a confident tone, but an artifact anyone can check. Signed content, reproducible results, and verifiable proofs are about to matter a lot more than they used to.

So here's the thread through the whole show: when making things gets cheap, the value moves to two places. Deciding what's worth making, and proving that what you made is real.
