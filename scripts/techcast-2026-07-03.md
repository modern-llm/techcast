# The Government Wants a Piece: OpenAI's 5% Offer and the Great AI Land Grab
*July 03, 2026*

Sam Altman just offered the United States government a five percent stake in OpenAI — and that's somehow the low end of what was on the table. Today we untangle what it means when Washington becomes a shareholder in the AI race, why Google's power bill jumped thirty-seven percent in a single year, and how a single transformer layer might be quietly embarrassing a lot of expensive training runs. Grab your coffee. Let's get into it.

## We start with the biggest story of the day, and it's a strange one. OpenAI is reportedly in active talks with the Trump administration about the U.S. government taking an equity stake in the company. According to the Financial Times, Altman has proposed handing over around five percent of OpenAI, potentially into a U.S. sovereign wealth fund.

Here's the shape of it. The talks are early-stage, but the direction is clear: Altman's pitch is that giving the public a financial stake is the best way to share the upside of AI. And notably, five percent is actually lower than what some, including Senator Bernie Sanders, had floated as a target — so this is being framed as OpenAI's counter-offer, not a capitulation. Trump reportedly likes the idea. And OpenAI isn't the only one being approached — sources say the administration has floated similar arrangements with Google and Meta. Think about what that actually means. A government that regulates these companies, sets their antitrust exposure, controls their export licenses for chips, and shapes AI policy would also own a slice of the equity. That's a profound alignment of incentives — and depending on where you sit, either a smart way to socialize the gains of a transformative technology, or a conflict of interest so large it doesn't have a name yet.

The obvious tension: once the U.S. Treasury owns five percent of OpenAI, is the government a regulator or a business partner? You can't fully be both. Keep an eye on whether this stays a wealth-fund abstraction or turns into a board seat.

## And the equity story doesn't stand alone — it's part of a broader repositioning across the whole industry this week. Two moves in particular tell you where the money and the power are flowing.

First, Microsoft just launched its own dedicated AI deployment company, backed by a two and a half billion dollar commitment. That puts them alongside Amazon, OpenAI, and Anthropic, all of whom have spun up dedicated groups whose entire job is getting AI models out of the lab and into production, at scale, inside real organizations. The signal here is that the frontier of competition has shifted. A year ago the race was about who had the smartest model. Now everyone assumes the models are good enough — the fight is about deployment, integration, and who can actually make this stuff stick inside a Fortune 500 workflow. Second, and this is the one I'd underline: Anthropic is reportedly in talks with Samsung about a new custom AI chip. That comes barely a week after OpenAI announced its own custom silicon partnership with Broadcom. Everyone is trying to escape the Nvidia tax.

Put these together and the theme is vertical integration. Custom chips, dedicated deployment arms, government equity — the AI labs are no longer just software companies. They're becoming industrial conglomerates, and they're building the whole stack from the sand up.

## If you want the physical receipt for all that ambition, look at the power meter. Google just reported that its annual electricity consumption rose thirty-seven percent in 2025 — the single largest jump in the company's history.

Let that number sink in. Thirty-seven percent in one year, on top of twenty-seven percent the year before. Google's total electricity use is now more than two hundred and fifty percent higher than it was in 2019. The company attributes it to the AI data center buildout, plus cloud and YouTube growth. And here's the honest line buried in their sustainability report — they admit the path to their climate goals will not be linear, because, and I'm quoting, their AI infrastructure buildout is currently accelerating faster than the grid is decarbonizing. That's a remarkable admission. It means the demand for compute is outrunning the supply of clean power. Google is papering over the emissions gap by buying enormous amounts of clean energy credits, which keeps their reported operational carbon down, but the underlying draw on the grid is exploding.

This is the part of the AI story that doesn't fit in a demo. Every clever model you use is backed by megawatts, and the grid is the real bottleneck. When Anthropic and OpenAI design custom chips, efficiency-per-watt isn't a nice-to-have — it's the whole ballgame.

## Which is a perfect segue, because the research world is pushing hard in the opposite direction — doing more with dramatically less. Two papers making the rounds this week both land on the same uncomfortable question: are we massively overspending on training?

The first is a paper asking whether one layer is enough. The finding: a single transformer layer can match the performance of full-parameter reinforcement learning training on certain tasks. Read that again. Instead of updating every weight across a deep network during expensive RL fine-tuning, you can get comparable results by training just one layer. If that holds up and generalizes, it's a direct assault on the assumption that better behavior requires touching the entire model. The second paper is about asymmetric quantization — a compression technique that claims near-lossless retrieval quality with a ninety-seven percent reduction in storage. Ninety-seven percent. That's the difference between a vector database that costs a fortune to host and one that fits comfortably on modest hardware. Both papers point at the same insight: a lot of the size and cost in modern AI is waste, not signal. The information you actually need is far more compressible than the raw parameter count suggests.

Here's why I care about these beyond the math. If training and retrieval get an order of magnitude cheaper, the moat around the big labs shrinks. Efficiency research is quietly the most democratizing force in AI — it hands capability to people who don't have a two-and-a-half-billion-dollar deployment budget.

## And that democratizing thread runs straight into a piece that's more manifesto than product — an argument for what the author calls the right to local intelligence.

The core claim is simple and, I think, increasingly urgent. As AI becomes the interface to everything — your email, your files, your search, your thinking — the question of where that intelligence runs becomes a question of power. If every query has to round-trip through a corporate cloud, then a handful of companies see everything you ask, and they can meter, censor, or price your access to your own cognition. The right to local intelligence is the argument that you should be able to run capable models on hardware you own, offline, private, under your control — the way you own the calculator on your desk, not rent it by the keystroke. And the efficiency research we just discussed is exactly what makes this plausible. Quantization down to a few percent of the storage, tiny fine-tunes on a single layer — these are the technical preconditions for intelligence that fits on your laptop instead of a data center.

This is the philosophical fault line of the decade. On one side, the sovereign-wealth-fund, custom-chip, mega-cloud model of AI as centralized utility. On the other, local, private, owned. The technology is genuinely pulling in both directions at once.

## Now to a legal ruling that draws a hard line about what AI is and isn't allowed to be. Japan's top court just ruled that an AI cannot be listed as an inventor on a patent application.

This is part of a global pattern. The same conclusion has now been reached in the United States, the United Kingdom, the European Patent Office, and Australia — and now Japan's Supreme Court joins them. The reasoning is consistent: patent law was written around the idea of a natural person as the inventor. An AI system has no legal personhood, so it can't hold that role, full stop. What makes this interesting isn't the AI-can't-invent headline — it's the gap it exposes. AI systems are already generating genuinely novel designs, molecules, and mechanisms. So if the machine did the inventing but can't be named, who gets the patent? The person who prompted it? The company that trained it? The court is essentially saying: figure that out among the humans, because the law only recognizes people.

The practical takeaway for anyone building with generative tools: the human-in-the-loop isn't just good practice, it's now a legal requirement for owning what comes out. If you want the IP, a person has to be able to claim meaningful inventive contribution.

## Let's talk security, because there's a story here that should make cryptographers uneasy. There are fresh allegations that the NSA is trying to weaken the standardization of ML-KEM — the post-quantum key encapsulation mechanism that's supposed to protect us from future quantum computers.

Some background. ML-KEM, formerly known as Kyber, is the flagship post-quantum encryption standard — the algorithm the world is racing to deploy so that encrypted traffic captured today can't be decrypted years from now by a quantum machine. The concern being raised is about the standardization process itself: choices around parameters and design that, critics argue, could reduce the security margin. And the NSA carries a very specific historical baggage here. This is the same agency credibly linked to the Dual EC DRBG affair, a random number generator standard that was later understood to contain a backdoor. So when the NSA's fingerprints appear on decisions that shrink a security margin, the community doesn't extend the benefit of the doubt — and shouldn't. Whether this particular case is malice or legitimate engineering tradeoff is exactly what's being fought over.

The lesson from history is that you don't get to trust a process just because it's official. Post-quantum crypto is foundational plumbing for the next thirty years of the internet — this is precisely the kind of thing that deserves loud, adversarial public scrutiny, not quiet deference.

## Shifting into developer tools, and there's a clear center of gravity right now: MCP, the Model Context Protocol. It's the standard that lets AI agents plug into external tools and data, and an ecosystem is forming around it fast. A YC-backed startup called Manufact just launched to serve it.

Manufact — previously known as mcp-use — describes itself as a vertical cloud for MCP apps and servers. The analogy they use is sharp: Manufact is to mcp-use what Vercel is to Next.js. In other words, they built the open-source SDK, it got popular, and now they're building the managed cloud platform on top — where dev teams can ship, iterate, test, monitor, and prepare MCP servers for production and store submission. What's telling is the origin story they tell. They started building agent frameworks, and then, in their words, the harness revolution hit — Claude Code, Codex, and others shipped agent harnesses that made standalone agent frameworks largely redundant overnight. So they pivoted from building agents to building the infrastructure those agents connect to. That's a smart read of where the durable value sits: not in the agent loop, which is commoditizing, but in the plumbing that connects agents to the real world.

The pattern to notice: whenever a protocol gets hot, the picks-and-shovels cloud businesses arrive right behind it. MCP is barely more than a year old and it already has a Vercel. That tells you how fast this layer is maturing.

## And on the MCP theme, two more releases that make agents dramatically more useful by giving them eyes. First, a Safari MCP server for web developers.

The Safari MCP server lets an AI agent drive and inspect a real Safari browser — reading the DOM, interacting with pages, and debugging in the actual rendering engine, not a simulation. For web developers, this is the difference between an agent that can guess about your bug and one that can actually open the page, see what's broken, and verify the fix. Pair that with the second release: a project letting any large language model watch a video. Historically, video understanding was locked to a few specific multimodal models. This approach opens it up, so an ordinary text model can reason over video content through the right pipeline. Both of these are about closing the perception gap. An agent that can only read text is blind to most of what a human developer or user actually experiences. Giving it a real browser and real video comprehension is what turns a chatbot into something that can genuinely do the work.

The through-line across all the MCP news is sensory. The frontier this quarter isn't smarter reasoning, it's better inputs — real browsers, real video, real tools. We're bolting hands and eyes onto the models.

## A quick but genuinely useful engineering insight now, from a piece arguing that Postgres transactions are a distributed systems superpower.

The argument is that developers reach for complex distributed systems machinery — queues, sagas, custom coordination logic — to solve problems that a plain database transaction already solves. When you wrap a set of operations in a Postgres transaction, you get atomicity and isolation for free: either everything commits or nothing does, and concurrent work is safely serialized. The author's point is that a surprising amount of what people build bespoke distributed coordination for can instead lean on the boring, battle-tested guarantees your database already provides. The superpower framing is a bit cheeky, but the underlying advice is sound: don't build a distributed consensus problem when a single transactional boundary will do. Reach for the simple tool first.

This is the kind of wisdom that saves teams from themselves. The most impressive architecture is often the one you didn't build because the database already had your back.

## And staying in the data layer, a couple of milestone releases worth flagging for anyone who works close to storage and infrastructure. Let's do a quick lightning round.

First, LMDB — the Lightning Memory-Mapped Database Manager — hit version one point zero. LMDB is a tiny, blazingly fast embedded key-value store built on memory-mapped files, and it quietly powers a huge amount of software you use, often as a hidden dependency inside other systems. A one point zero after all these years is a statement of maturity and stability for a genuinely foundational piece of code. Next, Podman shipped version six. Podman is the daemonless, rootless container engine that's become the leading drop-in alternative to Docker, especially in security-conscious and enterprise Linux environments — a major version bump signals continued momentum against Docker's incumbency. And Immich reached version three. Immich is the self-hosted photo and video backup platform that people run to escape Google Photos — own your memories, on your own hardware. Given everything we said earlier about the right to local intelligence, Immich is that same own-your-stuff philosophy applied to your personal media.

Three unglamorous, deeply useful releases. LMDB for speed, Podman for control, Immich for ownership. This is the infrastructure that quietly holds the fancy stuff up.

## Two more developer curiosities before we wrap, and the first is the kind of project that makes you tilt your head. It's called crustc — the entirety of the Rust compiler, translated into C.

To appreciate why this matters, you need the bootstrapping problem. The Rust compiler is written in Rust — which means to build Rust from scratch, you already need a working Rust compiler. That's a chicken-and-egg dependency that has long bothered people who care about being able to build a toolchain from a minimal, trusted base. Translating rustc into C attacks that directly, because C compilers are the universal substrate — nearly every platform can bootstrap from C. So a C translation of the Rust compiler is a potential path to building Rust on machines and from foundations where you don't already have Rust. It's also just a staggering feat of engineering to mechanically render a compiler that large into another language and have it work.

This is trust-and-provenance work. In an era where everyone worries about supply-chain integrity, being able to bootstrap a language from a minimal C base is a real security property, not just a party trick.

## And finally, a tool that uses AI to clean up AI-era codebases — a command-line tool for detecting non-exact code duplication using embedding models. It pairs nicely with a benchmark also making the rounds, CursorBench three point one.

Traditional duplicate-detection tools look for copy-pasted code — literal matching text. This tool goes further by using embeddings to find semantic duplication: two functions that do the same thing in different words, with renamed variables or restructured logic that a text matcher would completely miss. That's exactly the kind of near-duplicate mess that piles up when a whole team is generating code with AI assistants — subtly different implementations of the same idea scattered across the repo. Catching that lets you consolidate and keep the codebase coherent. And CursorBench, now at three point one, is part of the same maturing story — an evolving benchmark for measuring how well AI coding tools actually perform. As these assistants become standard, we badly need rigorous, versioned ways to measure whether one is genuinely better than another, rather than going on vibes.

There's a nice symmetry here. AI helps us write far more code, far faster — and now we need AI-powered tools to detect the redundancy and measure the quality of what it produced. The tooling is racing to keep up with its own output.
