# Nvidia's Board Investigates Its Own CEO
*November 09, 2026*

Good morning, it's Monday, November ninth, and this is TechCast Daily. Nvidia's board has opened an independent investigation into its own chief executive, and Jensen Huang has apologized to the whole company in writing. Also today: a bank pulls a popular coding tool from its traders, Apple scrambles to make Siri do real work, and a Microsoft research group shuts down.

## We start with Nvidia, because on Sunday the company announced something boards almost never do: an investigation into its own founder. If you followed the recent reporting that Chinese intelligence had targeted Nvidia's chief executive, this is the next step, and it moved fast.

Here is what the board said. In a statement posted Sunday, it said it is starting an independent investigation in light of recent reporting that Jensen Huang has been the target of a foreign intelligence effort. A committee made up only of independent directors, meaning board members who do not work for Nvidia, will oversee it. They will be assisted by outside independent counsel, meaning lawyers who are not on Nvidia's payroll. The statement also says the board prioritizes United States national and economic security.

The person at the center is Alice Huang, an executive at a company called Megaspeed. Reporting says she was recruited by China's Ministry of State Security, the country's main spy agency, to gather information on Nvidia. The board's investigation is about Jensen Huang's relationship with her.

Now, what we do not know. We do not know what, if anything, was passed along. We do not know how long the relationship lasted, or what investigators have found so far. The statement we have does not say how long this will take or whether the results will be public. So treat everything beyond the statement itself as unconfirmed.

Why it matters: Nvidia makes the chips that every large AI lab wants, and the United States restricts which of those chips can be sold to China. That makes Nvidia's internal information, such as shipment plans, product roadmaps and how the company handles export rules, valuable to a foreign government. And a chief executive who can be pressured is a security risk, whatever the details turn out to be. Spy agencies have long used personal relationships as leverage. The board does not have to prove that happened here to have a duty to look.

The speed matters. Boards usually wait for facts before announcing a probe. This one announced first, probably because the story was already public. Next, the memo Huang sent to staff.

## The same day, Huang sent a memo to Nvidia employees, and The Verge published the full text. It is direct, and not about chips.

He opens by writing, quote, I owe my family and all of you an apology. I was unfaithful to my wife. My actions hurt her and our children. There is no excuse for what I did. I am deeply sorry. End quote. He goes on to say his family has given him love and support his whole life, and that they deserve honesty and loyalty.

That is as far as our copy of the memo goes. The rest was cut off, so we cannot tell you whether he addresses the investigation, the intelligence reporting, or anything he may have shared. The reports are plain: Huang admitted that he had an affair.

Two things are worth separating. The first is personal: a chief executive apologizing to his family and his staff in a company-wide message. The second is institutional, and it is the one the board is paid to care about. An affair is a private matter until someone with an agenda knows about it. The question for the investigators is whether anyone used it, and whether Huang knew he was being worked. His memo does not answer that in the part we have.

For Nvidia, there is a practical problem too. The company is run by its founder, who is the public face of the AI boom. The independent directors now have to investigate the person who usually sets the agenda for them.

Watch for two things: whether the board publishes findings, and whether lawmakers ask for them. Both would tell us how seriously this is being treated.

## Now to AI coding tools, starting with a headline from Hacker News: Goldman Sachs is pulling Claude Code from its bankers and traders.

What we can say is why a bank would hesitate. Bankers and traders handle confidential client deals, trading positions and information that regulators care about. The headline says bankers and traders, not engineers. That hints at a split between software teams, who may keep it, and staff in client-facing and trading roles, where the data is more sensitive. That is our reading of the headline, not a fact.

If you work in a regulated industry, expect this question in your own company soon: which AI tools may touch which data.

## Staying with Claude Code, here is a security item you can act on today. A post says a feature called Don't Ask Again exposed passwords in 1,122 public files on GitHub.

We only have the headline, so the following is our inference, not the author's findings. When Claude Code wants to run a command, it asks permission. One of the choices is Don't Ask Again, which saves a rule so the same command runs without a prompt in future. If the command you approved had a password typed into it, the saved rule would contain that password. Those rules live in a settings file inside your project folder. And project folders get uploaded to GitHub.

That would explain how a count like 1,122 files could happen. Nobody broke in. People approved a command, the tool wrote it down, and the file went public along with the rest of the project.

So here is what to do. First, search your own repositories for Claude settings files and read them for anything that looks like a password, a token or a key. Second, if you find one, change that secret now. Deleting the file is not enough, because GitHub keeps history and automated scanners collect public secrets within minutes. Third, going forward, pass secrets through environment variables, which are named settings held outside your files, rather than typing them into commands.

If you use the tool, ten minutes of searching today beats a leaked-password emergency later.

## Now a complaint that comes around after almost every model release. A Hacker News post asks whether Opus 5.5, one of Anthropic's models, has been nerfed, which is gamer slang for quietly made weaker.

The title adds a specific claim: its thinking patterns changed on November third. There is no article text, so we cannot tell you what the author measured, how many examples they have, or whether anyone else sees the same thing.

Here is how to read a post like this. Models can seem worse for several reasons. The company may have changed the model, or the settings around it, such as how much it is allowed to think before answering. Or nothing changed at all, and the user's tasks got harder, or their prompts drifted, or ordinary variation happened to look like a trend. Without saved outputs from before and after, it is very hard to tell these apart by feel.

The detail worth noticing is the date. A specific day is testable. If someone kept logs from before November third and after, they can compare, and that is much stronger than saying it feels dumber. We have not seen that evidence here, and we have not seen a response from Anthropic. Treat it as a question worth watching, not a finding.

We will flag it if evidence appears.

## One more from the Claude Code corner: a post titled Goodbye Claude, hello Pi.

That is the whole headline, and we have no text, so we do not know who wrote it or why they switched. The title suggests Pi is another coding tool, but we cannot confirm that, so we will not describe it.

What the title does tell us is the shape of the story. Someone left one coding tool for another and thought it worth writing down. These posts are common, and they hint at something real. Coding assistants are easy to swap. They work on the same files, in the same terminal, with the same change history. Switching costs an afternoon, not a migration. That is why companies in this market compete so hard on price, limits and reliability, and why a change in usage limits or model behavior can send people to a rival.

Switching is cheap, so any slip in quality or price costs a vendor users quickly.

## Apple now. Bloomberg's Mark Gurman reports that the company is racing to make Siri far more capable, and that the pressure is coming from the top.

The detail: chief executive John Ternus is pressing Craig Federighi, Apple's AI chief, to speed up the Siri roadmap and deliver a powerful agent that can compete with OpenAI's Dots and Meta's Muse. Those are the rivals named; we have no details on either.

Gurman's account is candid about where Apple stands. Federighi delivered a working version of Siri AI on an incredibly short timeline. But the company has no illusions that it is keeping pace with its rivals. That is an unusual thing to say about a company that rarely admits it is behind.

Two significant improvements are expected in the coming months. The first would give Siri fine-grained control over Apple's own apps. The examples given are rotating and cropping an image in Photos, and organizing tabs in Safari. In plain terms, you would tell Siri what you want done inside an app, and it would do the steps for you. The description of the second improvement was cut off in our source, so we will not guess at it.

Why it matters: Apple's edge is that it controls the phone, the apps and the privacy rules. Control of its own apps is the part of the agent problem Apple can solve better than anyone, because it needs nobody's permission. The hard part is accuracy. A Siri that crops the wrong photo, or closes the wrong tabs, is worse than a Siri that says it cannot help.

The test is not the roadmap. It is the first week real users hand Siri a task with five steps.

Code in a beta is not a promise, though. Apple removes unfinished features all the time.

Read it as a direction, not a launch date.

## Next, a benchmark story. A project called MaxxBench has a pointed subtitle: Nice DeepSWE score. But is it true?

A benchmark is a standard test used to compare AI models, and DeepSWE, going by its name, is a test of software engineering. We have only the title, so we do not know which model or company the score belongs to, or what MaxxBench found.

The title suggests what the project is for: checking whether a published score holds up. That matters because scores are the main way AI companies compare themselves, and they are easy to inflate by accident or on purpose. A model may have seen the test questions during training. The test may be run with extra help that ordinary users do not get. Or the setup may differ from the one used for competitors.

We have no evidence about any of that here. The useful takeaway is the pattern: as benchmark numbers drive buying decisions and investment, at least one project now exists to re-check them. If you are choosing a model for real work, the better test is your own tasks run on your own code.

Treat any single score as a claim, not a fact.

## A short one on AI agents: a post argues that agent memory needs its OpenTelemetry moment.

Headline only again. OpenTelemetry is an open standard for collecting records of what software is doing, such as logs, timing and error traces, in one common format. Before it, every monitoring tool had its own format, and engineers had to rewrite their setup to switch. Once most tools agreed on one format, you could change vendors without redoing the work.

So the title is making a comparison. Agent memory is whatever an AI agent keeps between sessions, such as notes about you, past decisions and project facts. Today each product stores that in its own way, and you often cannot see it, edit it or move it. The argument, as we read the title, is that there should be a shared format for how memory is stored and inspected.

We cannot tell you what the author proposes. But the problem is real. If your assistant remembers wrong facts about you, you need a way to look at what it remembers and fix it. And if you change tools, you would like your history to come with you.

Standards only matter if rival vendors adopt them.

## Now one that sounds too good to be true: Llama-modchip, which claims to run a 284 billion parameter DeepSeek model on 64 gigabytes of memory, with no graphics card.

Headline only, so here is the arithmetic and our guesses. Parameters are the numbers a model learns in training. A model with 284 billion of them, stored at four bits each, a common squeezed format, needs about 142 gigabytes just to hold its weights. That is more than twice 64. So the whole model probably does not sit in memory.

There are two likely explanations. One is that the model is a mixture of experts, a design where each word only uses a small slice of the model, so only part of it needs to be ready at any moment. The other is that the rest is read from the drive as needed, which works but is slow. It could be both. Neither is confirmed; we are working from the title.

Why it matters: if it holds up, running a very large open model on an ordinary desktop, with no graphics card, is a real change for privacy and cost. Your data stays on your machine. The catch is speed. The headline says nothing about how many words per second it produces, and that is the number that decides whether it is usable or a party trick. Until someone posts that figure, hold your excitement.

Ask for the speed number first.
