# The Government Becomes the Gatekeeper
*June 27, 2026*

Today, the most powerful AI models on Earth aren't being held back by their makers. They're being held back by Washington. Anthropic and OpenAI are both shipping their flagship models on the government's timeline now, not their own. We'll trace how AI quietly became export-controlled technology, why OpenAI is putting a chip called Jalapeño between itself and Nvidia, and why the AI boom just made your next MacBook hundreds of dollars more expensive.

## Let's start with the story that reframes everything else this week. For the first time, the U.S. government is sitting inside the release pipeline of frontier AI models, deciding who gets access and when. This didn't come from one announcement. It assembled itself across half a dozen reports over the past few days, and once you line them up, the through-line is unmistakable.

Here's the sequence. Anthropic's Mythos 5, its most capable model, got pulled back from foreign markets and then tangled in a licensing fight with the Trump administration. And this wasn't a quiet bureaucratic delay. At one point the NSA actually lost access to the tool during the dispute. Sit with that for a second. One of the most secretive, technically sophisticated agencies in the U.S. government had woven a commercial AI model so deeply into its work that a contract fight knocked out its access. That tells you these models aren't toys or chatbots anymore. They're infrastructure, the kind that intelligence agencies now depend on. After a two-week negotiation, led on Anthropic's side by co-founder Tom Brown, Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick sent a letter, dated June 26th, revising the license so Mythos 5 can come back, but only for a select group of trusted U.S. organizations. Read that carefully: not the public, not foreign allies, a vetted list. And the public-facing version of that model class, the one branded Fable 5, is still in limbo, with no rollout timeline at all. Now hold that up next to OpenAI, same week, almost the same script. OpenAI is delaying its next big model, GPT-5.6, codenamed Sol, at the government's request over security concerns. Instead of a broad public launch, it ships as a limited preview to a small group of enterprise customers. And here's the part that should make you sit up: during that preview period, the administration itself reportedly approves access on a case-by-case basis, customer by customer. Sam Altman confirmed the shape of this in an internal company Q&A on Wednesday. So you have the government, in two separate cases, deciding not just whether a model ships, but to whom. And there's a competitive wrinkle worth flagging. Reporting suggests OpenAI got a more favorable deal than Anthropic did, keeping more control over its own rollout. When the government is the gatekeeper, the terms you negotiate with it become a competitive advantage in their own right.

Step back and the picture is clear. Frontier AI is now being treated like a dual-use export, somewhere between encryption and munitions. The government has decided these models are strategic assets, and the lever it's pulling is access itself. That's a profound shift in who actually controls the frontier. A lab's release schedule used to be a product decision, about readiness, safety testing, and market timing. Now it's partly a policy decision made in Washington. And once that precedent is set, it doesn't go back in the box. Every future frontier model now ships with an implicit question attached: who gets to say yes?

## And if you're wondering why Washington suddenly cares so much about who can touch these models, this next story is the answer, in concrete, slightly alarming numbers.

Anthropic has accused Alibaba of running the largest campaign it has ever measured to illicitly extract Claude's capabilities. The evidence came in a June 10th letter to Senators Tim Scott and Elizabeth Warren, sent one day ahead of a Senate committee hearing pointedly titled AI and the American Dream. In that letter, Anthropic laid out what it called new, confidential evidence. Between April 22nd and June 5th, operators it links to Alibaba and Alibaba's Qwen AI lab allegedly generated more than 28.8 million exchanges with Claude, across nearly 25,000 fraudulent accounts. Those numbers deserve a beat. Twenty-five thousand fake accounts is not a careless researcher poking at an API. That's an organized, industrial-scale operation built specifically to stay under the radar. And the targeting wasn't random. The campaign went straight for Claude's crown jewels, the capabilities Anthropic considers most valuable: agentic reasoning, software engineering, and long-horizon tasks, meaning the model's ability to plan and execute complex work over many steps. Those are exactly the abilities that are hardest to build and most expensive to train. The technique at work here is essentially distillation. You use a frontier model's own outputs as a teacher, generating millions of high-quality question-and-answer pairs, and then you train a competing model on that data. In effect, you let someone else spend the billions to reach the frontier, and you copy the answer key. Anthropic says this violated Claude's terms of service and access restrictions, and it wants Alibaba punished.

Now connect this to our first story, because it's one narrative, not two. Mythos gets restricted from foreign markets. China races to close the capability gap. And the single fastest way to close that gap is to learn directly from the very model you can no longer legally buy. That is precisely the dynamic the export controls are trying to interrupt, and this is the clearest public evidence yet of why the government decided model access is a national-security question rather than a commercial one. Whether you think the controls will actually work is a fair debate. But the logic behind them just got a lot easier to see.

## Now to the hardware layer, where a very different kind of independence is being fought over. The biggest names in AI are all quietly trying to escape the exact same company, and this week two moves landed that show how serious that effort has become.

First, the headline: OpenAI just unveiled its first custom chip, built with Broadcom and named Jalapeño. The key word is inference. This chip is purpose-built for running models at scale in data centers, serving answers to users, rather than for training new models from scratch. Both companies frame it as the first generation of a long-term silicon project, with chips that get refined over years. And OpenAI is walking into a crowded room. Google has been running its own TPUs for years. Apple, and even SpaceX, are designing their own chips too. The common motive is what the industry calls single-supplier risk, and that single supplier is Nvidia, which has owned the AI chip market so completely that, until recently, there simply was no Plan B. Now the second move, which got less attention but may matter just as much: Qualcomm is acquiring Modular, the company founded by Chris Lattner, the engineer behind the LLVM compiler infrastructure and Apple's Swift language. Modular builds the software layer that lets AI code run across different kinds of chips without being permanently chained to Nvidia's CUDA platform. And that's the piece most people underrate. Nvidia's real moat was never just that its chips are fast. It's CUDA, the software ecosystem that nearly every AI developer on Earth has built on top of. Switching chips usually means rewriting your stack, and that pain is what keeps customers locked in. So the smart strategy is to attack from both ends at once. Custom inference chips like Jalapeño chip away at the hardware dependence, while a portable software layer like Modular's chips away at the CUDA lock-in.

And starting with inference is exactly right, strategically. Training a model is a one-time, bursty cost. Inference is the bill that never stops, every single query, forever, for as long as the product lives. If you can shave the cost of inference with your own silicon, that compounds into real money fast. None of this dethrones Nvidia tomorrow, and I want to be honest about that. Nvidia is still the center of gravity. But the era of total dependence, the era where there was genuinely no alternative, is the thing that's quietly ending. And competition at the chip layer is ultimately good news for everyone building on top.

## All of that hardware maneuvering is really about one anxious question sitting underneath the entire industry: does the math on AI actually work? This week served up the bear case from several directions at once, along with one genuinely bright spot.

The bear case has three legs, and they reinforce each other. First, the blunt argument that current large language model costs simply aren't sustainable. The frontier labs are burning staggering amounts of cash to serve queries, and the prices users pay don't yet cover the true cost of running these models at scale, especially now that everyone is racing toward the most expensive reasoning modes, where the model thinks for longer and burns far more compute per answer. The unit economics, for the most demanding uses, can be underwater. Second leg: what one writer memorably called the unbearable cheapness of open-weight models. Open models, the kind you can download and run yourself, have gotten good enough and cheap enough that they set a brutally low price floor for the whole market. Think about what that does to pricing power. If a free, downloadable model handles eighty percent of what most people need, it becomes very hard for anyone to charge premium prices for the remaining twenty percent. The floor keeps dropping out from under the paid products. Third leg, the efficiency wildcard: Databricks' former AI chief says he can cut AI's power bill by a factor of a thousand. His system, called Un-0, reportedly replicates the behavior of conventional AI systems at a tiny fraction of the energy. Now, a thousand-x claim deserves heavy skepticism until it's independently verified. But even if the real number is far smaller, the direction is the threat. If efficiency keeps improving this fast, then today's enormous, multi-billion-dollar compute buildouts could end up looking wildly overbuilt, like laying ten-lane highways for traffic that learns to teleport. And now the bright spot, cutting the other way. Among consumers who actually pay for AI, Anthropic's Claude is steadily winning share away from ChatGPT. ChatGPT still dominates overall, by a wide margin. But the paying segment, the people voting with their wallets, is increasingly choosing Claude.

Put it all together and you can feel the squeeze. The frontier labs are spending as if maximum capability is the only thing that matters, while open weights commoditize the floor underneath them and efficiency breakthroughs threaten to make today's massive capital spending look like a historic over-investment. The companies that win this won't necessarily be the ones with the single biggest model. They'll be the ones whose costs and prices actually meet somewhere in the middle. And Claude's quiet momentum among paying users is a useful hint: willingness to pay tracks genuine usefulness in daily work, not benchmark scores or launch-day spectacle. Revenue follows the product people actually reach for on a Tuesday.

## Here's where all of that abstract AI spending lands on your kitchen table. If you've been eyeing a new Mac, you just got more expensive news, and the reason traces straight back to everything we've been talking about.

Apple raised prices across much of its lineup, and these aren't trivial bumps. The entry-level MacBook Neo went from 599 to 699 dollars. The iMac that was 1,299 is now 1,499. An M5 MacBook Pro that cost 1,699 is now 1,999. And at the very top end, the M3 Ultra Mac Studio with 96 gigabytes of memory took a 1,300-dollar increase, all the way to 5,299. The iPad line is up between 100 and 200 dollars, with smaller increases on the Apple TV and HomePod. iPhones, at least for now, are untouched. So what's driving it? In Tim Cook's own words to the Wall Street Journal, it's the soaring price of memory. Cook said Apple has been trying to shield its customers from the increases being passed to it, but the situation has become, quote, unsustainable. That's the same word the LLM-cost critics used, and it's not a coincidence. Here's the connection. The AI data-center buildout is devouring memory, specifically high-bandwidth memory and DRAM. Every giant GPU cluster needs enormous quantities of it, and that demand has bid up memory prices across the entire industry. Memory is a commodity with finite production capacity, so when hyperscalers buy it by the warehouse to feed AI, there's simply less to go around, and the price rises for everyone, including the chips inside your laptop. And there's a strategic tell buried in the same week's reporting. Apple is reportedly skipping its high-end M6 Mac chips entirely, jumping instead to an AI-focused M7 line, with M7 Pro, Max, and Ultra variants. They're effectively reorganizing their entire chip roadmap around AI workloads.

So your more expensive MacBook is, in a very literal sense, collateral damage of the AI boom. The same memory that fills the data centers is the memory in your laptop, and there's only so much of it being made. This is the moment the AI buildout stops being an abstraction in a tech headline and starts showing up on your receipt. And Apple skipping a whole chip generation to pivot toward AI silicon tells you exactly where it believes the next several years will be won. The AI race isn't just a story about chatbots and frontier labs anymore. It's quietly reaching into the price of nearly every device you own.

## Let's end on something that points the very same technology at the opposite end of human history. After all the talk of restrictions, lawsuits, and price hikes, here's AI doing something purely, almost gloriously additive.

For the first time ever, researchers have read an entire Herculaneum scroll. These are the carbonized papyrus rolls buried and charred by the eruption of Vesuvius in 79 AD, in the same disaster that destroyed Pompeii. For centuries they've been an agonizing tease, because they're so fragile that physically unrolling one destroys it. They survived two thousand years only by being turned into lumps of carbon, and that very preservation made them unreadable. The breakthrough comes from the Scroll Prize, an open research effort that combines two things. First, high-resolution CT scanning, the same imaging idea as a medical scan, to capture the internal structure of a scroll while it stays rolled and sealed. Second, machine-learning models trained to detect the faint, ghostly traces of ancient ink hidden inside those rolled-up layers, ink that's nearly invisible even in the scan. The software then virtually unwraps the scroll, layer by layer, and reconstructs the text, without anyone ever physically touching it. Going from a handful of scattered words, which is where this project was not long ago, to a complete, readable scroll is an enormous leap. There are hundreds of these scrolls still waiting, an entire ancient library that's been locked shut since antiquity.

There's a lovely symmetry in closing here. The same broad family of machine-learning techniques that governments are now treating as a strategic weapon, that companies are fighting and suing each other over, is also quietly recovering literature that's been unreadable for nearly two thousand years. Same fundamental tools, wildly different stakes. And it's a good reminder to carry out of today's episode: the technology itself is neutral. What we choose to point it at is entirely up to us.
