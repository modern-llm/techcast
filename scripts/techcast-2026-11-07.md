# Spies, Valuations, and Agents That Never Sleep: TechCast Daily for November 07, 2026
*November 07, 2026*

Welcome to TechCast Daily for Saturday, November seventh, twenty twenty-six. The biggest story today: China's intelligence service reportedly recruited an AI executive to spy on Nvidia's Jensen Huang, and now Congress wants him to explain. Also, OpenAI's valuation climbs past a trillion dollars, while new data says company spending on AI has stalled even as usage climbs. Plus acquisitions, agent infrastructure, and a few security stories you may want to act on. Let's get into it.

## Start with a spy story that has now reached Capitol Hill.

The New York Times reports that China's Ministry of State Security, the country's main intelligence agency, recruited an executive named Alice Huang to collect information on Nvidia's chief executive, Jensen Huang. She works at Megaspeed, a company that buys Nvidia's chips. According to the Times, the agency wanted to learn about American AI chips and, in its words, potentially influence the company to push for its most advanced chips to be made available to China. The Times also says she has relationships with executives at the highest levels of the AI chip industry, including leaders at Gigabyte, the hardware maker. This follows a report last week that Jensen Huang was seen as the target of a Chinese spy plot. Now Congress is moving. The chairs of the House committees on China, foreign affairs and intelligence, John Moolenaar, Brian Mast and Rick Crawford, wrote to Huang this week. They asked him to appear before a joint, closed-door briefing on November eighteenth. Their letter says the recent reports raise significant national security concerns about China's access to sensitive American technology and information.

Two points. First, Huang is being asked to explain, but in these reports he is the target, not the suspect. Second, the access route is what matters. Megaspeed is a customer, and customers talk to chip makers about supply. If a foreign agency can sit inside that relationship, export rules are only as strong as the vetting of who is buying. The briefing is closed, so we may never hear what was said. What we can watch is whether Nvidia's tone on China sales changes afterward.

## Next, two stories about money that point in opposite directions. First, the headline number.

Bloomberg reports that OpenAI has raised another eleven billion dollars at a valuation of one point two five trillion dollars. That would make it one of the most valuable companies in the world. In March, it raised one hundred twenty-two billion dollars at a valuation of eight hundred fifty-two billion. The new round has not closed, but is expected to in the coming weeks, and OpenAI is still pursuing more investment that could bring this round to twenty billion. Here is the part worth noticing. OpenAI first hoped to raise as much as thirty billion dollars at a valuation of one point four trillion. That was before tech stocks dropped. The Verge's summary, which is cut off in our copy, also ties the lower figure to the midterm election results. So the number is enormous, but it is a cut from the plan. Now the other side. Ramp, which sells corporate cards and sees what its customers spend, says AI usage keeps climbing, but AI spending has stalled. Two things are behind it, according to the report: price cuts from the model makers, and smarter cost controls among the biggest spenders. In plain terms, companies are using more AI and paying about the same, because each unit costs less and finance teams have learned to watch the bill. The timing matters, because Anthropic is nearing its IPO, its first sale of shares to the public. Investors have priced these companies on the assumption that customer spending will keep growing at a breakneck pace. If usage grows while revenue per customer flattens, that assumption gets harder to defend.

Earlier this week we heard that AI bills were becoming a problem for buyers. This is data from many companies pointing the same way. A valuation is what investors will pay for future spending. When spending stalls while usage climbs, that story gets a crack in it. And OpenAI taking less than it wanted suggests investors are already looking at it.

## A quick win for publishers now.

Meta has indefinitely paused an update to its commercial terms after weeks of backlash. The update would have required Meta Business account holders to allow extensive AI training on external websites linked from Facebook. Legal experts told Nieman Lab the vague wording could let Meta ignore robots.txt, the file where a website tells crawlers what they may not copy, and crawl vast amounts of content outside Facebook for AI training. Almost every news publisher has a Facebook page. So Nieman Lab warned that Meta could avoid millions of dollars in licensing fees. An earlier attempt by Meta to calm the criticism failed. Now a spokesperson has told Reuters: we're pausing the changes as we continue to engage with stakeholders and take our time to get this right. In the meantime, the existing terms remain in effect. Ars Technica's headline caught the mood: Does Meta think we can't read?

Notice the word pause, not cancel. The terms were not withdrawn, and the goal, more free training data, has not changed. If you run a Meta Business account, read any future terms update line by line, especially anything about content on your own website.

## Two acquisitions now, both about the plumbing around AI agents. An agent here means an AI that takes actions for you, not just answers questions.

First, Dropbox is buying Tigris Data for three hundred fifty million dollars. Tigris sells object storage, the kind of storage that holds files and data in bulk. Dropbox says the deal supports DBX Agent Cloud, a new storage platform aimed at developers building AI models and agents. That is a notable move for a company known for syncing your documents. It wants to be where AI builders keep their data. Second, Palo Alto Networks is in talks to buy Clutch, a startup focused on securing AI agents, for about five hundred million dollars. Clutch is backed by Lightspeed and SignalFire. Its last valuation was eighty-five million dollars, according to a source. If the deal lands at that price, the buyer pays nearly six times what investors last valued the company at. We have no details on how Clutch's product works beyond that focus, so we will not guess. And these are talks, not a signed deal.

Both deals follow one logic. Agents need somewhere to keep their data, and someone to control what they are allowed to do. Established companies would rather buy those pieces than build them. The prices say they are in a hurry.

## Three infrastructure items from Hacker News now, on where AI agents actually run. Only the first has a full post behind it.

Rivet launched on Hacker News. It is a Y Combinator company from the winter 2023 batch, and its pitch is an orchestrator, a system that starts and manages many agents at once. Instead of giving each agent a heavyweight sandbox, an isolated computer of its own, each agent gets a lightweight actor. An actor is a small, long-lived process with its own memory. According to the launch post, each actor has in-memory state for context, a WebSocket server so several people can work with one agent at once, durable SQLite storage backed by S3, and hibernation when idle. The founders say it is a similar model to Cloudflare Durable Objects, but open source and self-hostable. They came from building infrastructure for multiplayer games, and say actors let them serve tens of millions of monthly users. Second, Replit rebuilt its sandbox engine with microVMs. We only have the headline. A microVM is a very small virtual machine, which isolates each workload more strongly than a container while still starting quickly. Third, GitHub has a new Git storage architecture. Again, headline only. Git is the tool that tracks changes to code, and GitHub stores a huge number of repositories. We covered OpenAI's post on scaling Git earlier this week.

Two large players writing about Git storage in one week suggests the load on version control is changing, though we do not have their reasons. The common thread across all three: an agent that works for minutes or hours needs its own state, not just a one-shot request. How cheaply you can keep ten thousand of them parked is becoming a product question.

## Now a startup with a clear answer to AI slop in mathematics.

Axiom Math is betting on verification. The problem it describes: AI is flooding mathematics with proofs that nobody has checked. Axiom's answer is formal verification, where a proof is written in a language that a computer can check step by step, so correctness does not depend on trusting a human or a model. The company says its AI agents have just formally verified the Stacks Project, one of the field's biggest libraries. The Stacks Project is an online reference work in algebraic geometry, a branch of pure mathematics. We do not have details on how long this took, or how much of it was done by hand.

Here is why it matters, and this part is my view. A model can write a proof that sounds right and is wrong. A verifier cannot be charmed. If agents can check a library of this size, the same method could carry over to software, where proving code correct uses similar tools. The useful AI trend may be less about generating more, and more about checking what was generated.

## Now three security and privacy items. The first has real reporting. The other two are headlines only.

Security researchers found hundreds of servers running Artex, an AI-driven penetration testing tool, exposed to the open internet, so anyone could use it. TechCrunch reported it. A penetration testing tool is software that probes systems for weaknesses, the kind security teams use to test their own defenses. Left open like this, it is a ready-made attack tool for anyone who finds the server. The owners almost certainly did not intend that. Second, a Hacker News post titled: ChatGPT silently passes data between connectors and the bounty wants an ID card. Connectors are how ChatGPT reaches other services, such as your files or email. The headline suggests a researcher found that data moves between connectors without telling the user, and that the bug bounty program asked for identification before it would deal with the report. We cannot verify either claim from a headline, so treat them as claims. Third: Claude Code sends your email address to websites. Claude Code is Anthropic's coding agent. We have only the title, so we do not know which websites, or in what circumstances. It is worth reading the original post before drawing a conclusion.

The pattern is familiar. Tools that act for you pass along more than you expect, and tools built to attack are only safe if their owners lock them down. If you use ChatGPT connectors, check which ones are switched on, and turn off any you do not need.

## A short one on land and power.

Google has acquired Crusoe's data center site in southeast Wyoming. That is the whole headline, and we have no price, size or reason for the sale. Crusoe builds data centers for AI. So a data center builder has sold a site to one of the biggest users of data centers.

What we can say is that sites with land and power are the scarce part of the build-out, and big buyers are collecting them. Whether this is Google expanding or Crusoe raising cash, we will wait for more.

First, a post titled: AI is dramatically accelerating AI research. We only have the title. The claim is that AI tools are making researchers who build AI faster. We cannot judge the evidence from here. Second: a Jev that can ask Opus for help gives Opus accuracy at 4x lower cost. We do not know what a Jev is. If that holds up, it fits the cost theme from earlier: spend on the big model only when you have to. Third, Anthropic published a piece titled: AI for scientific discovery, how researchers use Claude. Headline only. It presumably describes scientists using Claude in their work, but we do not have examples. Fourth, Google released Gemini Omni 1.2 Flash. Flash is the name Google gives its faster, cheaper models. That is all we have: no benchmarks, no pricing, no word on what changed in 1.2.

Notice how many of these are about cost. A smaller model that asks for help, a Flash model, a stalled spending line. The race is moving from who has the smartest model to who can deliver the answer cheaply.
