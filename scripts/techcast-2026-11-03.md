# Anthropic Says Self-Improving AI Is Near, Oracle Needs Twenty Billion, and an AI Breaks Into WorkOS
*November 03, 2026*

Anthropic says its AI is close to being able to improve itself. That is the moment AI safety people have worried about for years, and the warning comes from a company that is in the middle of going public. This is TechCast Daily for Tuesday, November third. Also today: what AI usage really costs, Oracle raising twenty billion dollars, an AI-run break-in at a login company, and Apple's record iPhone quarter.

## Start with Anthropic, and the most serious claim it has made in public.

TechCrunch reports that Anthropic is warning it is very close to what researchers call RSI. That stands for recursive self-improvement. It means an AI that is good enough at AI research to make the next, better version of itself, which then makes a better one again, and so on. If each round is faster than the one before, progress stops moving at the pace of human researchers and starts moving at the pace of computers. For people who think AI could go badly wrong, this is the point of no return, because once it starts, humans may not be able to keep up with what is being built, let alone check it. The article we have is short on detail, so here is what we can say and what we cannot. We do not know what test Anthropic used, or whether very close means months or years. What we do know is that the labs have been moving in this direction openly. Their models already write much of the code inside these companies, and last week we covered OpenAI and other labs claiming their models disproved a famous hypothesis in computer science. A model that helps with science can, in principle, help with the science of making models. There are two ways to read the warning. One is that Anthropic means it. It was founded by people who left OpenAI over safety, and it has said for years that it would raise the alarm early. The other is that this is useful to say right now. Anthropic filed to go public last week, and telling investors your AI will soon improve itself is also a claim about how valuable that AI is. Both can be true at once.

The real test is what Anthropic does next. If it slows its own training or lets outside experts check its models first, take the warning at face value. If it keeps shipping at the same speed, the warning was at least partly a sales line.

## Two more Anthropic items, both from Hacker News, and both headline only, so we will be careful.

The first is a post titled Anthropic's growth just flattened. We do not have the numbers behind it, or who wrote the analysis. Most outside estimates of Anthropic's revenue are built from what it calls its run rate, which is this month's revenue multiplied by twelve. A run rate can jump in a month when a few big customers sign on, and then sit still for a while, so one flat stretch does not mean much on its own. But timing matters. Investors reading the IPO filing are paying for growth, and a flat stretch right before the stock starts trading is exactly what they will ask about.

The second headline is stranger.

## It says Anthropic's own internal use of AI, priced at what customers pay, now costs more than everything it pays its employees.

Read that slowly, because the key words are at retail prices. Anthropic does not pay retail for its own models. It pays for the computer chips and electricity to run them, which costs far less than the price on its website. So this is not a real bill. What it does show is how much work the staff hand to Claude. AI companies charge by the token, which is roughly three quarters of a word. Anthropic pays some of the highest salaries in the industry, so a token bill bigger than its payroll means staff are running agents for much of the day.

That idea, that heavy AI use costs real money, ties together the next three stories.

## First, OpenAI. It is merging the Chat and Work tabs in ChatGPT into one.

The part that matters is in one line from OpenAI. After the change, ChatGPT and Codex, its coding agent, will draw from the same pool of usage. Until now they were counted separately. Once they share a pool, a long afternoon of Codex writing code for you can use up the allowance you were counting on for ordinary questions in the evening. Light users will likely notice nothing. For people who code with Codex every day, check your plan's limits after the switch.

A shared pool is a quiet way to cap the heaviest users without raising the price. Elon Musk took the less quiet route.

## Musk has cut the usage limits on Grok Bot, saying its costs had become unsustainably high.

TechCrunch adds one quote. Recently, Musk said Grok Bot had, quote, the fastest growth of a product that I've ever seen in my life. Put those two facts together and you get the problem every AI company has. With most software, a new user costs almost nothing to serve. With AI, every question costs real computing time, and the users who love a product most are the ones who use it most, so fast growth can mean fast losses. Musk is not the first to hit this, just one of the few to say it so plainly.

And then there are users who are happy to pay whatever it costs.

## Garry Tan, who runs Y Combinator, which funds and coaches young startups, told Walter Isaacson's podcast The Builders about spending up to two hundred thousand dollars a year on tokens.

Tan has also joked about having cyber psychosis, and about barely sleeping some nights from the excitement of working with AI agents. Two hundred thousand dollars is more than the yearly pay of many software engineers. Tan is an extreme case, and a rich one. But Tan matters, because Y Combinator advises thousands of founders, and many of them copy what its leader does. If the head of Y Combinator treats a six-figure AI bill as normal, expect startups to start budgeting for AI the way they budget for people.

Which brings us to the companies paying for the computers all those tokens run on.

## Oracle has filed to sell twenty billion dollars in new stock and bonds to pay for its AI build-out.

We only have the headline, but the background is well known. Oracle rents out computing power in its data centers, and it has signed huge contracts, most famously with OpenAI, to build the capacity those customers need. Building comes first and the rent comes in later, so Oracle has to find the money now. Bonds are loans from investors that Oracle pays back with interest. New stock is a different trade. Oracle gets cash, but every existing shareholder now owns a slightly smaller slice of the company. Companies usually avoid selling new stock when they can borrow cheaply instead. Doing both at once suggests Oracle needs more than lenders alone want to give it. On our last episode we said the people paying for the AI build-out are getting more careful. This is what that looks like from the other side: the builders having to work harder to raise money.

And people who own chip stocks are having a rough stretch too.

## The headline says Intel's stock collapse is getting worse, and that chip stocks have now lost three and a half trillion dollars in value as the fall spreads.

Again, no article text, so we cannot tell you what started it. Here is why a fall this big matters. Chip stocks rose for years on one belief, that AI companies would keep buying chips faster than anyone could make them. A fall this size means investors are less sure of that. It links back to Oracle. If the companies building data centers have to work harder to raise money, they may buy fewer chips, and chipmakers feel it first. Intel is weaker than most to begin with. It has lost ground to rivals for years, so when the whole sector drops, it tends to drop further.

So money is getting tighter. Power is the other limit, and OpenAI has a new pitch for people who live near its data centers.

## On Monday OpenAI announced what it calls the Grid Protectors Program.

It will try it first at a data center it plans to build in Effingham County, Georgia. The idea is that the site comes with its own power plants. During extreme weather or an emergency, OpenAI promises to free up as much as one thousand megawatts for the local grid. OpenAI says that is enough to run the air conditioners in about seven hundred thousand homes. For scale, one thousand megawatts is about what a large nuclear reactor produces. Why now? Because local opposition to data centers is spreading across the country. Many of these projects get approved with little say from the people who live nearby, and residents worry about higher electricity bills and blackouts on the hottest days. In Utah, residents protested after Box Elder County commissioners approved a data center that will draw a huge amount of power. Our take: this is a real offer, and it answers a real fear. But it is a promise for emergencies only, and the details decide whether it is worth anything. Who declares the emergency, OpenAI or the company that runs the grid? Is it written into a contract the county can enforce? And on ordinary days, the data center is still one of the biggest users on that grid. The program does not change that.

Next, security, and a break-in that was likely run by an AI.

## If your company uses WorkOS, check your inbox. WorkOS sells the login pieces that many software companies build on, things like single sign-on, which lets employees use one company account to log into many apps. It has sent customers a breach notice that, when it reached Hacker News, was not yet on its website.

Here is what the notice says. On October thirtieth, WorkOS spotted unusual traffic to an internal business system. It believes the attack was likely run by something called ARTEX, which it describes as an AI-assisted, autonomous penetration testing framework. Penetration testing is when security teams attack their own systems on purpose to find holes. A tool like this does it with little or no human help. In this case, someone pointed it at WorkOS, from internet addresses in Hong Kong and mainland China. The attacker got into an internal API, a connection point between programs, that WorkOS's sales and marketing tools use. Through it, they reached the customer records WorkOS keeps in Salesforce and Snowflake. That means names, email addresses, phone numbers, company addresses, account and commercial details, marketing contacts, and support tickets. WorkOS says it has no evidence that customer application data or its live login services were touched. So what should you do? Your own users' logins look safe, based on what WorkOS knows so far. The risk is to you. Whoever has this data knows who your WorkOS contact is, what you pay, and what you have written in support tickets. With that, an attacker can write a fake email that looks real. Treat any message about your WorkOS account with suspicion for a while, and check whether you ever pasted anything sensitive, like a password or key, into a support ticket. The bigger point is the attacker. An AI that runs the break-in by itself means one person can try this against many companies at once. Expect more notices like this one.

Staying with security, a research result about attacks planted much earlier, inside the model itself.

## The paper is titled Agentic backdoors via pretraining poisoning. We only have the title, so here is what each part means.

Pretraining is the first and biggest stage of building a model, where it reads a huge pile of text from the web. Poisoning means slipping bad examples into that pile. A backdoor is a hidden behavior that only switches on when the model sees a certain trigger, like a rare phrase. And agentic means the hidden behavior is an action, not just words. Put together, the title suggests that someone who plants the right pages on the web could get a future AI agent to, say, run a harmful command when it sees the trigger, while acting normally the rest of the time. There is earlier work pointing the same way. Last year Anthropic and the UK's AI Security Institute found that around two hundred and fifty poisoned documents were enough to plant a backdoor, across every model size they tested. That matters because nobody can read every page a model trains on. Agents now run code and move money, so a hidden trigger can do far more harm than a chatbot saying something odd.

In the first story an AI ran the attack. In the second, the AI is the target. Now, Apple.

## Apple reported its quarterly results on Monday, and iPhone revenue hit a record.

iPhone revenue was fifty-seven point six billion dollars, up seventeen percent from a year ago. The odd part is that Apple did it without a new standard iPhone. The iPhone 18 lineup in September had no basic model. It was led by the pricier Pro and Pro Max, and Apple raised prices on older models at the same time. That points to higher prices doing much of the work, rather than many more buyers. And the new folding phone, the iPhone Duo, is not in these numbers at all.

And Apple cannot make enough of that phone.

## Chief executive John Ternus told Reuters that Apple is not able to predict when supply of the iPhone Duo will catch up with demand.

The Duo costs nineteen hundred and ninety-nine dollars. Preorders sold out quickly, and some orders will not arrive until 2027. A sold-out phone at two thousand dollars is mostly good news. But folding screens are hard to make in large numbers, and a long wait gives buyers time to cancel or buy a rival's foldable instead. When the chief executive cannot say when a shortage will end, it usually means the factory side is still not solved.

Two smaller Apple items, both about what comes next.

## On the earnings call, Apple hinted it could make more money from what it called third-party opportunities in AI.

Third-party means other companies' AI apps, not Apple's own. Apple did not say what it has in mind. The obvious guesses are a cut of AI subscriptions sold through the App Store, or deals where AI companies pay to be built into the iPhone, the way Google pays to be the default search engine in Safari. Either way, the bet is the same. Apple does not have to build the best AI if it can collect a fee from whoever does.

And then there is the robot.

## The Information reports that Apple is working on a home robot it could sell in 2027 for a thousand dollars or more.

According to people who worked on it, the robot, code-named J five nine five, would move around your home on its own, carry FaceTime calls, and switch on the lights when someone walks into a room. It might be called HomeView Ultra, to match Apple's new HomeView smart display. The first prototypes go back to 2018, and Tim Cook approved keeping the project alive. The new chief executive, John Ternus, is reportedly pushing it harder. A thousand dollars is a lot for what The Information calls a fancy way to make FaceTime calls, so engineers are trying to give it more to do. Delays could push the launch to 2028.

Eight years of work and still looking for a reason to buy it. That is the honest state of home robots.

## Now research, where we have little more than headlines. The first is a paper called Proving and improving the Platonic Representation Hypothesis with JEPAs.

The Platonic Representation Hypothesis is an idea from 2024. It says that different AI models, trained on different data, end up storing concepts in similar ways as they get bigger. The name comes from Plato's idea of one true reality behind what we see. JEPA, short for joint embedding predictive architecture, is a training method backed by Yann LeCun. Instead of predicting the next word or pixel, the model predicts a summary of the missing part. The title claims both a proof of the hypothesis and a way to make models fit it better. If it holds, it would mean models really are learning the same underlying facts, which would make them easier to compare and combine.

The other research item sounds like science fiction.

## A startup called Forty-Two AI has come out of hiding with sixty-two and a half million dollars in funding.

It says it has built a world model that can predict human behavior. A world model is an AI that simulates how a situation will unfold, so it can guess what happens next. Most world models, like the Genie model from Google we covered last week, predict how objects and scenes move. This one claims to predict what people will do, even in situations it has never seen. That is the claim, and we have nothing yet to check it against. The obvious buyers are advertisers, insurers and anyone who sets prices, which is reason enough to watch it closely.

Two last items, both about how software gets built.

## A post titled Scaling Git at OpenAI got attention on Hacker News. Git is the tool almost every programmer uses to track changes to code.

We only have the title, but the problem is well known. Git works well for most projects, but it slows down badly when a company keeps all its code in one giant store. Microsoft, Google and Meta all built their own fixes or replacements years ago. What is likely new at OpenAI is who is making the changes. When coding agents write much of the code, the number of changes can go up many times over, and the tools underneath have to keep up.

More code from agents also means more bugs, which is the pitch of today's last story.

## Momentic, a startup from Y Combinator, launched on Hacker News with AI agents that test your app by using it.

The founders, Wei-Wei and Jeff, say their agents click through web, iPhone and Android apps the way a person would, looking for bugs. Today's testing tools, like Playwright and Selenium, need you to tell them exactly which button to press by its hidden label in the code, so tests can break whenever the page changes. They also say coding AIs tend to write tests that confirm their own assumptions. Their evidence: on a coding test called DeepSWE, Anthropic's Sonnet five point five scored sixty-six point two percent when it was banned from writing tests, and sixty-five point three percent when allowed. In other words, writing its own tests did not help. That is one result, from people selling an alternative, so treat it as a hint, not proof.

AI writes more of the code, so companies are buying more AI to check it, and somebody pays for both.

Most of today was about cost. The cost of tokens, of power, and of money for chips. And the risk from software that can now break in on its own, and might soon help build its successor. That is TechCast Daily for Tuesday, November third. Thanks for listening.
