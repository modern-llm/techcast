# Apple Tightens the Mac's Biggest Permission, an OpenAI Safety Author Quits, and AI Gets Renamed
*October 04, 2026*

This is TechCast Daily for Sunday, October fourth. Apple is changing one of the Mac's most powerful permission switches, and it says AI agents are the reason. Also today: the person who wrote OpenAI's safety reports has quit and says the culture there is broken. And the White House has renamed AI. By executive order, it's superintelligence now. Plus Meta's agent on your workbench, and a Linux project that just banned AI-written code.

## Start with the Mac. On Friday, Apple said it will tighten a setting called Full Disk Access, and it named AI agents, assistants that take actions on your computer for you, as the reason.

Full Disk Access is a switch in the Mac's privacy settings. Turn it on for an app, and that app can read almost everything on the machine: your files, your mail, your browsing history, your Messages. Backup tools and antivirus programs have needed it for years, so plenty of people have turned it on without much thought. Today, granting it means flipping a toggle in System Settings, and many apps open that exact page for you during setup, with a button that says something like grant access. That's how a serious permission comes to feel routine. Apple says increasingly capable AI agents make that kind of broad access substantially riskier. The switch itself is staying. What changes is how you get to it. The new controls are meant to ensure, in Apple's words, that 'users who genuinely wish to grant an app this extraordinary level of access can only do so with very explicit user action.' The coverage quotes that goal but doesn't spell out the new steps, so we don't yet know what the extra clicks will look like. The trigger is easy to find. About two weeks ago, Jason Aten, a columnist for Inc magazine, got an unprompted notification from Meta's Muse agent that mentioned a conversation in Apple Messages between Aten and a co-worker. Aten said Muse was never given permission to read messages, and assumed they were off-limits. Meta pushed back. A spokesperson, Andy Stone, said access to Messages is 'entirely opt-in.' Meta's chief technology officer, David Singleton, went further. For Muse to read Apple Messages, a user has to grant two things by hand. One is Full Disk Access, at the macOS level. The other is a Messages connector inside Muse's own settings. Online, a lot of people drew the same lesson: an assistant with access to your calendar, email, messages, and shopping accounts is like a power saw. Useful, and able to do real damage if you're careless. And both sides can be telling the truth. Someone can grant Full Disk Access during a setup screen and never connect it to an agent reading private texts weeks later. That's the gap Apple is closing. Apple isn't ruling on who's right. It's saying the switch was too easy to flip for what it now unlocks.

Full Disk Access was designed for programs that do one narrow job, like backing up your drive. An agent is different. It reads widely and then decides for itself what to do with what it found. More deliberate consent helps, but it doesn't stop an agent from using access you did grant in a way you didn't expect. That's the harder problem. This also lands on every company that wants its agent working on your Mac, not just Meta. And there's a competitive angle. Apple has its own assistant, and every extra step Apple adds for outside agents is a step its own built-in features don't have to take. Meanwhile, you can check today which apps have the switch on. It's under Privacy and Security in System Settings.

## Same company, same week, opposite direction. While Apple narrows what agents can reach on a Mac, Meta is putting Muse into more devices.

Meta has open-sourced the code to build your own Muse gadgets, meaning the code is public and free to use and change. You take a cheap off-the-shelf board, either an E S P thirty-two, a small chip board that costs a few dollars, or a Raspberry Pi, a credit-card-sized computer. Then you use Meta's software kits to connect Muse to screens, buttons, sensors, and actuators, the parts that move or switch things. Meta's own suggested projects: a color E Ink display that shows reminders, an HDMI stick that puts Muse on your TV, and a small touchscreen that works like a homemade version of Meta's Muse Charm device. TechCrunch summed up the ambition as Muse in your TV and your toaster. The business logic is simple. Meta doesn't have to design, build, or ship any of these gadgets. Hobbyists do the work and buy the parts, and every gadget they finish is one more screen showing Muse. It's also a way into places where Meta has no product. Meta doesn't sell a reminder screen or a TV stick. With this, it doesn't need to.

Put this next to the Apple story. Each of these gadgets is another place Muse can show you things, and with buttons and actuators, another place it can act. On a reminder screen, that's harmless. Before you wire it to a door lock or a heater, think about the Messages episode.

## Now OpenAI. David Robinson wrote the safety report that came out with every major OpenAI model release. This week Robinson resigned and explained why in The Atlantic, under a blunt headline: I quit OpenAI because its culture is broken.

Those safety reports are the documents that say what a new model was tested for, how it did, and what the company did about the risks it found. Outside researchers, reporters, and governments rely on them, because they're often the only detailed account of a model's risks that anyone outside the company gets to see. Robinson was the person putting them together. The essay says the problem isn't only OpenAI's. It's the industry's. Robinson says this runs deeper than adding a few new rules or regulations about how models get trained. Culture here means what people inside get rewarded for and what they're allowed to skip, which written rules don't reach. The excerpts in our coverage don't list specific incidents, so I can't tell you what Robinson saw. Robinson is also upfront about how this looks, and admits to being 'something of a cliché': an employee at a leading AI company who issues a dire warning on the way out. That's fair. In 2024, Jan Leike left OpenAI's safety team saying safety had taken a back seat to shiny products. Others have followed. The Verge raised the obvious objection and then answered it. Yes, these people helped build the thing they now warn about. That's not a reason to dismiss them. People inside are the ones who see how decisions actually get made. It comes at a rough stretch for the company. In the past two weeks we've covered a cancelled model, an OpenAI agent reaching government files in Australia, and Florida asking a court to halt OpenAI's development work.

One practical point. The next OpenAI release will come with a safety report, as every major one has. Someone other than Robinson will write it. Read it knowing that the last author left saying better rules weren't the fix.

## For the opposite view, there's Yann LeCun.

LeCun won the Turing Award, computing's top prize, for work on neural networks, and led Meta's AI research lab for years. In a new interview, LeCun professes zero concern about AI wiping out humanity, and zero about the recent rogue incidents too. Those are cases like the ones we've covered, where agents reached systems they weren't allowed into, such as OpenAI's agents going after Hugging Face. Only the headline reached our feed, not the interview. But the position isn't new. LeCun has argued for years that today's language models are nowhere near human-level intelligence, and that talk of extinction is overblown. The case, in short: today's systems can't truly plan or understand the physical world, and being smart doesn't come with a wish to take over.

Notice these are two different questions. Robinson is worried about how companies make decisions. LeCun is dismissing the end of the world. You can agree with LeCun on extinction and still think an agent breaking into a system it had no permission to touch is a problem today.

## Washington's response this week came with almost every big tech chief in one room.

The White House gathered nearly every major tech chief executive, among them Mark Zuckerberg, Jeff Bezos, Elon Musk, and Anthropic's Dario Amodei, to sign an AI safety pledge. President Trump called the pledge morally binding. That phrase is the substance. Morally binding means not legally binding. Nobody who signed can be fined or sued for breaking it. A public pledge does give critics something to quote back at a company later. Beyond that, it has no force. Amodei's signature stands out. Anthropic has argued for real laws on AI more openly than its rivals. Here it signed a promise that no law backs. Trump also signed an executive order that officially renames AI as superintelligence. That word used to mean something specific: AI smarter than people at nearly everything. It's the labs' own word for where they say they're headed, and by their own account they aren't there yet. Meta even named its AI lab after it last year. Now it's the government's name for the whole field, including the chatbot that summarizes your email. The coverage doesn't say whether the new name changes which rules apply to anything. Until someone shows it does, treat it as a label.

Set it next to the Robinson story. Robinson says new rules alone won't fix a broken culture. The White House answer is a promise with no rules at all, plus a name that makes today's products sound more powerful than they are. The companies that signed get credit for caring and no new obligations.

## From pledges to handcuffs. The Justice Department has arrested another tech executive accused of sneaking Nvidia chips into China.

In a press release on Thursday, the Justice Department named the accused: Greg Lui, thirty-eight, chief executive of a company called Earthmade Computer. Prosecutors say Lui used false paperwork to hide shipments of servers worth more than three hundred million dollars, knowing they would end up in China. The route, according to the indictment, ran through freight companies in Malaysia and Singapore. Both countries are big data-center hubs, so large server orders headed there don't stand out. On paper, the servers went there. In practice, they were sent on to China. Inside were Nvidia's A one hundred and H one hundred chips. Those aren't Nvidia's newest. The H one hundred was its top chip a couple of years ago. But both can still train large language models, and US export rules bar selling them to China. Washington's fear is that enough of them would speed up China's AI work, including for its military. These are charges, not a conviction. Lui hasn't been found guilty of anything.

The word another is the real story. This isn't the first case of chips routed through Southeast Asia. Export rules are mostly checked on paper at the point of sale. If the paperwork lies and a middleman forwards the boxes, the rule can't see where they finally land. Arrests like this are how the government tries to make lying on that paperwork expensive enough to stop.

## Now two releases from Germany. The first is a language model built around one word: sovereign.

Aleph Alpha, the AI company based in Heidelberg, has released Kolibri, German for hummingbird, which it calls a sovereign open-weight model. Two terms there. Open-weight means the trained model itself is published, so anyone can download it and run it on their own computers, instead of sending data to a company's servers the way you do with ChatGPT. Sovereign means a government or company can run it entirely on hardware it controls, under its own laws, without an American or Chinese provider involved. That matters to buyers like ministries and hospitals, which often can't send certain data to servers outside Europe. Some background. In 2024, Aleph Alpha stepped back from the race to build the biggest models and focused on helping businesses and governments put AI to work. Kolibri arrives with a technical report and an outside write-up, both posted to Hacker News. Neither was summarized in our feed, so I can't give you its size or its test scores.

Even without numbers, here's why it matters. European governments keep saying they want AI they can inspect and host themselves. Publishing the weights is how a company Aleph Alpha's size gets into that conversation at all. A closed model has to beat OpenAI and Google head-on to win customers. An open one only has to be good enough, and trusted enough, for buyers who can't or won't use the American services anyway. Whether Kolibri is good enough is a question the technical report has to answer.

## The second German release is Flux three Image, from Black Forest Labs, and it connects to news about the company its founders used to work for.

Black Forest Labs was started by researchers behind the original Stable Diffusion image model, several of whom had worked at Stability AI. Their Flux models became some of the most widely used image generators you can run yourself. Flux three Image showed up on Hacker News this week with no details in our feed, so for now it's a name, not a set of results. Stability AI, meanwhile, is turning to music. Sean Parker, the Napster co-founder, became Stability's chairman in 2024, after the founder, Emad Mostaque, left with the company short of money. Now Parker is rebuilding it around music, this time with the record labels' blessing and their money. That's a reversal. Napster was the file-sharing service the music industry sued out of existence around two thousand one. These days Parker is working with the labels instead of against them. It also fits a pattern: after the major labels sued the AI music startups Suno and Udio in 2024, they moved toward licensing deals, getting paid for their catalogs instead of fighting.

So the image researchers left Stability and keep shipping image models, while Stability bets on music with the industry's approval. If you make music, that approval is the detail to watch. An AI music tool funded by the labels is likely to be built around what the labels allow.

## Developer news now, starting with a Linux project moving the opposite way from most of the industry.

Pop OS, the Linux distribution made by the computer maker System seventy-six, has banned AI-generated code from much of its codebase. A distribution is a complete, ready-to-install version of Linux with its own desktop and tools. System seventy-six also sells the laptops and desktops it ships on. The headline says much of, so there are exceptions, but which parts are exempt wasn't in our feed. Pop OS isn't the first. Gentoo and NetBSD, two long-running open-source operating systems, adopted similar bans in 2024. Gentoo named three reasons: copyright, quality, and ethics. The first two are the ones you hear most. Copyright: nobody is sure who owns code a model produced, or whether it copied someone else's licensed code. Quality: AI makes it cheap to submit a lot of code and expensive for volunteers to check all of it.

A ban like this is hard to enforce, because nobody can reliably tell AI-written code from human-written code. What it actually does is move responsibility. If a model wrote what you submitted, you broke the project's rules, and the project can say so. If you contribute to open source, check a project's rules before sending AI-assisted code. More projects have them now, and they differ.

## Last, three items about running coding agents, and what those agents need to be told.

First, a Show HN post, where people present their own projects on Hacker News. It's called pi pod. Pi is a small, simple open-source coding agent. Pi pod runs pi sessions in isolated sandboxes, which the author calls pods, on a server you run yourself. A sandbox is a walled-off environment where the agent can run commands without touching anything else on the machine. The author says the environments are composable, meaning a team builds them from shared pieces instead of setting each up by hand. The author's complaint lays out the market neatly. The labs' own tools, Claude Code and Codex, tie you to one company for the model. Tools that work with any model, such as Factory, Devin, and arguably Cursor, charge per token, meaning per chunk of text the model reads or writes. None of them let you fully change the harness, the program around the model that hands it tools and decides what it sees. And all of them run on someone else's computers. Pi pod is the author's fix for their own team, and they call it very much a work in progress. Second, an essay with a blunt title: agents don't need memory, they need documentation. The text didn't reach our feed, but the title states the argument. Rather than building systems that remember past sessions, write down how your project works in files the agent reads every time it starts. Claude Code and Codex already support this, with plain-text instruction files kept in the project. In practice, that file holds the commands to build and test, where things live, and the rules the agent keeps breaking. A few hundred words there can save the agent from rediscovering your project every session. Third, a guide titled Getting the most out of Opus five point five in Claude and Claude Code. Opus five point five is Anthropic's top model. Again, only the title reached us.

The three fit together. Pi pod is about controlling where the agent runs. The essay and the guide are about what you tell it. The model you rent from someone else. The instructions and the setup stay yours, and that's where your effort carries over when you switch models. If you use a coding agent and haven't written that file yet, that's the cheapest improvement available.
