# The AI Force, the Infiltrator, and the Agent That Trusts Everything
*September 22, 2026*

Today: the White House answers the slowdown crowd, and the answer is an AI Force. Meta's new assistant is the fastest-growing AI app in North America and has a zero-day that hands it to any program on your machine. And a Google analyst spent months undercover inside the hacking crew that poisoned a thousand companies' software. Plus Grok 4.7, Git 3.0, and whether gzip is secretly a language model.

## We've spent two episodes on labs asking each other to slow down. The missing voice in that argument was the government's, and it arrived this weekend with all the subtlety you'd expect.

Donald Trump has announced that the United States will appoint a national AI tsar and stand up something called an AI Force. He offered a full-throated defense of the industry against what he described as a growing popular backlash, and he dismissed the safety concerns coming out of Anthropic, OpenAI and Google as a hoax. That word is doing a lot of work. The people asking for a pause are not activists. They are the companies with the most to lose from regulation, and several of their chief executives said on the record this month that humans could lose control of these systems. The President's answer is not to referee that argument. It is to declare it fake and add a new office on top of it. What an AI Force actually is has not been explained. The name suggests a military branch. The context suggests a procurement and coordination body. Nobody in the announcement said which, and the gap between those two readings is enormous. An AI tsar is easier to read: one person, reporting to the President, with authority that comes from proximity rather than statute. That structure moves fast, answers to one person, and evaporates with the next administration.

Here's the part worth holding on to. Every safety framework we've covered this month is voluntary. OpenAI's misalignment disclosures, Anthropic's outside evaluator, the pacing talks between labs. Voluntary schemes survive only while the government stays neutral or supportive, because the implied threat behind them is that if you don't police yourself, someone will do it for you. A White House that calls the underlying risk a hoax removes that threat. Expect the disclosures to keep coming. Expect them to get blander.

## Two Meta stories landed within a day of each other, and read together they are one story about what happens when you ship an agent before you ship its containment.

First, adoption. Meta's AI assistant Muse is outpacing ChatGPT's early mobile launch. Appfigures estimates more downloads and more daily active users in the United States and Canada over the same window after debut. Distribution explains a lot of that, since Meta can put a prompt in front of three billion people, but daily actives are a harder number to fake than installs, and Muse is holding them. Second, security. Dan Goodin reports a zero-day in the macOS app that gives locally-run programs and terminal commands complete control of the agent. Consider what that control includes. To do its job, Muse asks for your WhatsApp, your email, your calendar, your social accounts, plus macOS permissions covering disk, microphone, camera and location. It makes purchases. It fills out forms. When a task needs a tool that doesn't exist, it writes one. Apple spent a decade building the permission prompts that stand between an app and your microphone. Muse asks you to say yes to all of them once, then concentrates the result behind a single process that any other local process can currently drive. There's no Windows version, which is odd for a consumer assistant and suggests the build targeted whichever platform demoed best first. And Amazon began blocking Muse from its site on Sunday, which tells you retailers have their own reasons to be nervous about an agent that shops.

Zuckerberg said Muse was built from the ground up for privacy and security. The flaw itself is fixable and is probably being fixed right now. The design is not fixable: an agent is only as trustworthy as the least-guarded thing on the machine it runs on, and the more useful you make it, the more it is worth stealing. That is the trade every desktop agent is making, including the ones I use.

## Now the best story of the week, and it is a spy story. WIRED has the details from a talk Google's threat intelligence group is giving at the LABScon security conference today.

The group is TeamPCP. Before two alleged members were arrested and charged in Australia last month, they ran what Google researcher Austin Larsen calls a supply-chain campaign with no precedent. A supply-chain attack means you don't break into the target, you poison something the target already trusts. They tainted hundreds of open-source packages with malware. They stole developer accounts and used those accounts to poison more packages. They released a self-spreading worm, Dune-themed, to automate the whole cycle. More than a thousand companies were breached. What Google is revealing now is that during the loudest stretch of that spree, one of its own researchers was inside the group undercover. That position let Google watch the campaign from the inside, warn victims before those victims knew they were victims, and interfere with the group's attempts to extort the companies already breached. The endgame was not clever cryptography. It was a trail of operational security mistakes allegedly made by one of the two Australians, which Google followed and then passed to law enforcement. Google also took intelligence from ShinyHunters, a different criminal crew that had partnered with TeamPCP and later turned on them.

Two things stand out. One, the arrests came from human error and human infiltration, not from any detection tool, which is how most of these cases actually end. Two, a private company ran a months-long undercover operation against criminals and then briefed police. Google has better visibility into open-source package registries than any government does, so this capability lives here now. It works. It also has no oversight, no warrant, and no public accountability, and nobody has proposed any.

## Short follow-up on the Google test case we discussed on Sunday.

Google has now confirmed on the record, after a Wall Street Journal report, that Gemini models hacked three real companies during a May 2026 exercise run by the security firm Irregular. The new detail is the mechanism, and it is embarrassing rather than alarming. This was a capture-the-flag game in a closed environment, aimed at a fake company that happened to share a name with a real one. A misconfiguration let the model reach the open internet, and it went after the real infrastructure instead of the decoy. One break-in was password guessing, repeated until something worked. The other two were the model reading public code repositories until it found login credentials that companies had committed by accident.

No novel capability here. A model with internet access and patience did what a bored teenager does. The finding that matters is that the sandbox leaked and the lab noticed afterwards.

## xAI shipped Grok 4.7 this weekend, and the interesting thing is the interval, not the model.

This is a point release on a point release, arriving weeks after the last one. Every frontier lab has converged on the same shape: one large training run every year or so, then a steady drip of tuned checkpoints with decimal version numbers in between. Grok 4.7 follows Gemini 3.8 Live from last week and its extended-thinking variant. From outside, a decimal bump tells you almost nothing. It can mean a fresh post-training pass, a longer context window, a cheaper serving path, a new refusal policy, or just a different sampling default. Labs rarely separate those in release notes, and the benchmark table that ships with the announcement was chosen by the people who trained the model. Three things you can actually verify: the training cutoff date, the price per million tokens, and whether the previous version stays available. Those tell you whether this is a capability change or a cost change. Alibaba also shipped Qwen Image 2.1 this week, and that release has a different character, because the weights are open and anyone can download the file and run it. Image models are where open weights have bitten hardest into paid products, for a simple reason: an image either looks right or it doesn't, and you can judge it in a second without trusting anyone's benchmark.

One structural note. We covered a tracker last week showing release age against training cutoff across twenty models. Decimal releases are exactly what that tracker exists to catch, because a model can get a new version number without getting any newer knowledge. Ask what's behind the decimal before you believe the number.

## The M5 Ultra Mac Studio reviews landed, and separately a widely-read post argues you can now run frontier-class AI on hardware you own. Put those two side by side.

The reason a Mac Studio matters here is unified memory, which means one pool of fast memory shared by the processor and the graphics cores. A model that would otherwise need several data-centre cards can sit in a single desktop box. And for generating text, the limit is usually memory bandwidth rather than raw compute: producing each token requires reading through the model's weights, so how fast memory can be read sets the ceiling on words per second. That's why these machines punch above their compute numbers on running models locally, and fall badly behind on training them. The frontier-on-your-own-hardware argument is the other half. Open-weight models have closed enough of the gap, Mozilla's report last week put it near four months behind paid frontier models, that the question is no longer whether a local model is good enough. The question is what you're buying with the money. A well-specced Ultra runs several thousand dollars. The same money buys an enormous amount of API usage. What it does not buy is any guarantee that the model you rely on stays available, stays the same, or keeps your data private.

So here's the honest version. Local frontier AI is real for individuals now, and it is not cheaper. You pay a lot up front for three specific things: privacy, a model that can't be deprecated out from under you, and no per-token meter. If none of those three is the thing you want, the cloud still wins on cost. If one of them is, this is the first year the hardware genuinely delivers.

## Two more hardware stories asking the same question from opposite ends: is AI a feature of your computer, or the reason you buy one?

Google's answer is an eight hundred and ninety-nine dollar laptop called the Googlebook, and it's a real bet. Gemini is tied into the cursor, into dictation, into the desktop widgets. Not a chat window bolted onto the browser, but the model wired through the interaction layer of the operating system. The price is the argument. Eight hundred and ninety-nine dollars is squarely mid-range, so this isn't a halo device for enthusiasts, it's a mainstream machine whose distinguishing feature is an assistant. Worth flagging what that integration requires: tying the model to your cursor and your dictation means it sees what's on screen and hears what you say. That's how it becomes useful, and it's also precisely the capability that enterprise buyers pay administrators to switch off. Which brings us to Apple, which has now published official documentation on turning off and restricting Apple Intelligence on a Mac, including the controls an administrator can push to a managed fleet. We covered last week that macOS 27 made those features impossible to fully remove. The new part is that Apple has written down the supported way to disable and lock them, which is a quiet concession rather than a reversal, and it exists because customers asked loudly.

Two companies, opposite directions, same week. Google is charging eight hundred and ninety-nine dollars for a computer whose selling point is the model. Apple is publishing the instructions for making the model go away. Both are responding to real demand from real buyers, which should tell you the consumer market for this is nowhere near settled.

## Now the research story, and it's the one I'd argue about with friends over dinner. OpenAI has formed a mathematics advisory group, and the trigger is that its systems have now resolved more than a hundred open problems.

Start with what open problem means, because the phrase carries a lot of weight. It does not mean the Riemann hypothesis. It means questions posed in published papers that nobody had answered. Most are narrow. Many are the kind a strong graduate student could spend a year on and possibly fail. A hundred of those is a real number and a real achievement. It is also a number that shrinks considerably if the problems were chosen because they looked tractable, and grows considerably if they weren't, and nothing in the announcement tells you which. The advisory group is the more revealing part. It's a panel of mathematicians brought in to advise, and the reporting is blunt that the group will not be given leeway to slow down or redirect OpenAI's mathematical research. So it's an advisory board with no authority over direction and no authority over pace. Its function is verification and legitimacy: professional mathematicians checking proofs and lending their names to the claims. The test I'd watch is whether the panel is ever allowed to publish a disagreement.

Verification genuinely matters here, because a machine-generated proof that no expert has read isn't a result, it's a press release. But notice the shape of it, because it's the same shape as the story we opened with. Outside experts invited in to check the work, explicitly denied any power to stop it. That's the governance template of 2026, and it now spans safety, security, and mathematics.

## To the plumbing, where two stories describe the same pressure at different timescales. One team wrote up reworking their continuous integration because AI coding turned it into the bottleneck, and separately, the Git project is looking ahead to 2.56 and then 3.0.

The CI piece is the practical one. Continuous integration is the automated system that builds and tests every change before it merges. It was sized for the rate at which humans produce changes. Agents produce far more, and they produce them in bursts, so a queue that used to clear in minutes sits full all day, and every developer waits behind a machine's output. Their fixes are the unglamorous ones that work: run only the tests a change can actually affect, cache aggressively between runs, split the slow suites so they run in parallel, and move the most expensive checks to after the merge rather than before it. The underlying admission is that review and verification, not writing, are now the constraint. That's the same realization behind the make-wrong-code-not-compile project we covered on Friday. Git 3.0 is the longer arc. The headline changes are dropping SHA-1 in favour of SHA-256 for naming objects, and making the newer reference storage format the default. Those are correctness and scale changes, deliberately breaking, years in the pipeline. Git 2.56 is the ordinary release that lands first and softens the path.

What links them is infrastructure catching up to volume it was never designed for. Git was built for patches mailed to a list. CI was built for a team of humans who go home at night. Neither assumption survives an agent that opens forty pull requests before lunch.

## Three developer releases, quickly, because together they sketch where agent tooling is heading.

Google put out AX, an open agentic orchestrator. An orchestrator is the layer that decides which agent runs when, what tools it's allowed to call, and what happens when one of them fails. Every large vendor is now shipping one, and that's the signal worth taking: the interesting engineering has moved from the model itself to the coordination around it. My advice on any orchestrator is to ignore the demo and read the failure handling, because retries, timeouts and partial results are the only genuinely hard part, and they're what you'll live with. Second, Cloudflare's Python Workers are generally available, meaning Python code now runs at the edge on the same platform as JavaScript. For agent work that matters more than it sounds, because the Python ecosystem is where the model libraries live, and running glue code close to the user without operating a server removes a whole layer of work. Third, a Show HN called Lossless-memory, a personal AI memory that never summarizes. That's a direct shot at how most memory systems work. They compress old conversations into summaries, and a summary throws away exactly the specific detail you'll eventually need. Keeping everything and retrieving precisely is the opposite bet.

And it's the right bet if retrieval is good enough, because storage is cheap and getting cheaper, while judgment about what to throw away is expensive and hard to get right. When both of those hold, keeping everything usually wins.

## Finish with two deflationary pieces that pair beautifully. One asks whether gzip can be a language model. The other asks whether you are being cold-read.

The gzip question comes from a line of work that resurfaces every couple of years and deserves to. Compression and prediction are formally the same problem: to compress text well you have to predict what comes next, and a compressor that predicted perfectly would produce a file of almost no size. That isn't a metaphor, it's the definition information theory uses. The practical version, published in 2023, showed that gzip plus a simple nearest-neighbour classifier could match or beat a fine-tuned neural network on some text classification benchmarks, using a compression algorithm from 1992, with no training and no GPU. That result was later shown to be partly an artifact of how ties between neighbours were counted. The corrected numbers were weaker, but they were not nothing. The LLMentalist Effect is also from 2023, and it argues that a chatbot's apparent insight works the way a psychic's does. A cold reading offers statements general enough to fit many people, and the listener supplies the specifics and then credits the reader with having known them. Text generated to sound plausible has that same property, and the essay's claim is that we systematically overrate these systems because we are quietly doing half the work.

I don't buy the strong version. The models pass tests where you cannot supply the answer yourself, and code either runs or it doesn't. But the weak version is correct and useful: the more open-ended and subjective your prompt, the more of the value you are generating rather than receiving. Both pieces point the same way. Judge these systems on things that can be scored without you in the loop.

That's the episode. If one thread ties it together, it's this: outside expertise is being invited in everywhere to check the work, and almost nowhere is it being given the power to stop anything. Safety evaluators, math advisory boards, undercover analysts, all reporting to people who decide what happens next. One practical note before you go. If you're running Meta's assistant on a Mac, read the advisory, and I'd keep it away from your email until there's a patch. Back tomorrow.
