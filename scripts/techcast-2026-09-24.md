# Cheap Answers, and Who Checks Them
*September 24, 2026*

This is TechCast Daily for Thursday, September twenty-fourth. Anthropic and OpenAI both shipped new models with the same pitch: a little better, for a lot less money. The Pentagon, meanwhile, said that trusting AI too much contributed to a missile strike that hit a school in Iran. Today is about cheap answers, and what they cost when nobody checks them.

## Start with the launches. When two rivals land on the same message, it tells you where the market is. Anthropic released Claude Opus 5.5. OpenAI released GPT-6 Sol and Luna. Ars Technica summed up both in one line: a little more for a lot less money.

Take them one at a time. Ars describes Opus 5.5 as Anthropic's mass-market workhorse, the model people use for coding and heavy knowledge work. Two years ago that would have sounded strange, because Opus was the expensive tier you used sparingly. Anthropic has been moving Opus toward everyday use since late last year, and this release continues that. The top name is now the default. OpenAI's release is shaped differently. GPT-6 Astra stays on top as its flagship. Sol and Luna are its mid-size and small models. OpenAI says they are cut from the same cloth as Astra: built from the same underlying work, but smaller and cheaper to run. The selling points are cost, speed and fewer mistakes, not new abilities. Fewer mistakes is the claim to test. Every lab measures errors its own way, usually on tests it picked. A model can also make fewer mistakes on average while making new kinds of mistakes you haven't learned to catch yet. If you run one of these in production, the useful step is boring. Before you switch, rerun your own examples on the new model, and look at what it gets wrong, not just how often. Side by side, neither company is leading with something nobody has seen before. They are competing on price, for abilities people already use every day. That's what a maturing market looks like. The frontier still moves, but the money is in the middle. Businesses there make millions of calls, and a price cut decides whether a project pays for itself. An essay making the rounds is titled Tokens too cheap to meter. The phrase comes from a 1954 speech by Lewis Strauss, then chairman of the Atomic Energy Commission, who predicted electricity too cheap to meter. Electricity is still metered. For AI, though, the real lesson isn't about whether prices fall. They are falling. The lesson is that total bills don't. When each answer gets cheaper, people ask for far more answers. A coding agent that works on a task for an hour can use more tokens than a person chatting with a model all month. So the price per token drops while the invoice climbs, because people now use the models differently. For anyone with a budget, the number to watch is tokens per task in your own logs, not the price per token in the announcement.

My take: cheaper models aren't the risk. Cheaper models whose output nobody checks are. When every answer costs almost nothing, the one expensive step left is checking it. The next story shows what happens when that step gets too little weight.

## We've covered intelligence errors written by AI on this show before. What's new is an official admission. The Pentagon says overreliance on AI contributed to a missile strike that hit a school in Iran. It's the heaviest story of the day, so we'll stick to what the admission tells us.

Start with the word the Pentagon chose: overreliance. That word blames people, not the tool. It says they leaned on the system too much. Researchers have a name for this: automation bias. It means accepting a machine's answer without checking it, especially under time pressure, and especially when the machine is usually right. It shows up in pilots, in radiologists, and in drivers using lane assist. When a system is right ninety-nine times in a row, the people around it stop checking on the hundredth. The word also has a political use. Overreliance puts the failure on people and process, which training and rules can fix. It keeps the blame off the tool, which the military buys more of every year. Both can be true at once. If a tool's mistakes are hard to spot, people will rely on it too much no matter how many memos tell them to double-check. Pentagon policy on autonomous weapons already requires what it calls appropriate levels of human judgment over the use of force. Whatever system was involved here, this case tests what appropriate means in practice. So whether anything changes depends on specific questions. What did the system actually produce: a target, a label, a confidence score? What was the human check supposed to be, and how long did people have to do it? Did anyone disagree with the machine, and what happened when they did? Admitting that AI contributed is only the first step toward accountability. We'll know whether it means anything when we see if those answers come out in public.

On Sunday we looked at how the FAA is designing its AI around exactly this kind of failure. The principle is the one from the top of the show. When machine output is cheap and fast, the checking step is the part that matters most.

## Now to what the models can do, or at least what their makers want us to notice. Two headlines about OpenAI's flagship, GPT-6 Astra, went around this week. One says it broke an Enigma message that had resisted solution since 2005. The other says it can now drive a car.

The Enigma one first, because it's more interesting than it sounds. Enigma was the German cipher machine of the Second World War, and the Allies famously broke it. But breaking the system didn't mean every message got read. Enigma's settings changed daily, and a short message gives a codebreaker very little text to work with. Some intercepted messages survive only as short or garbled copies. Amateur codebreakers have worked on them for decades, with computers far faster than anything at Bletchley Park. If a message held out against that crowd since 2005, it is a real puzzle, not a stunt picked because it sounded hard. Here's the part that matters. You can check an Enigma solution instantly: the output is either readable German or noise. The math results we discussed on Tuesday work the same way. When an answer can be checked, a model can try thousands of approaches and keep the one that works. The honest question isn't whether Astra out-thinks Alan Turing. It's how much of the work was the model reasoning about the cipher, and how much was the model writing and running search code. Both are useful. They're different claims. The car is the opposite kind of problem. There is no instant check for driving. A mistake shows up as a crash. And the hard part of self-driving has always been the rare situations, not the ordinary ones. So when someone says a general-purpose model can drive, ask three things. First, where: a simulator, a closed course, or a public road? Second, how fast does it react? At thirty miles an hour, a car covers forty-four feet every second, and large models don't usually answer in a fraction of a second. Third, was a person ready to take over? A language model steering a car around a test track would be a genuine research result. It would also be a very long way from a product. Waymo, and the Google project it grew out of, have spent well over a decade on that gap, with systems built only for driving.

Put the two together and you get a working rule for AI claims. Be impressed when the result can be checked on the spot. Ask harder questions when it can't.

## From a result you can check on the spot to one that needs a lab to check it. Anthropic says its biology lab has already found something big. Claude identified what the company describes as a new enzyme system with CRISPR-like repeats.

Some background. CRISPR started as a bacterial immune system. Bacteria store short snippets of viral DNA between repeated sequences in their own genome, as a record of past infections. They pair those snippets with enzymes that cut matching DNA if the virus returns. Scientists turned one of those enzymes, Cas9, into the gene-editing tool that won the 2020 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Since then, much of the progress has come from finding related systems in the genomes of microbes. Each new one might cut differently, be smaller, or work where Cas9 can't. Much of that hunting means searching huge DNA databases for patterns, and a model can do that work without tiring. So a new system with CRISPR-like repeats is a plausible and possibly valuable find. Plausible is the key word. Spotting the repeats is step one. Whether the enzymes do anything useful can only be shown by lab experiments, and that is where a claim like this holds up or doesn't. TechCrunch's reporting points to what may be the more telling detail: Anthropic has not let Claude run loose in its lab. Humans are still in the loop, which means a person decides which of the model's suggestions get tested in the lab. That makes sense for safety, since biology is the field where experts worry most about AI misuse. It also makes practical sense, because a model can suggest candidates far faster than any lab can test them. There's also a business reason every lab now wants a science result. A discovery in biology makes a much easier case for spending on AI than another coding benchmark does. That doesn't make the finding less real. It does mean it needs the same checks as any company announcing its own result: look for the published paper, and for outside scientists repeating the experiments.

Notice the contrast with the Pentagon story. Two institutions gave two very different answers to where the human sits. In this lab, the model suggests and people check. That's slower, and it's why the result is worth taking seriously.

## If models keep getting cheaper and more alike, where does the money go? One funding round gives an answer. Snorkel AI raised a three hundred fifty million dollar Series E, and its valuation tripled to three and a half billion.

Snorkel is seven years old. It began as a Stanford research project on labeling training data with rules and programs, instead of rooms full of people clicking boxes. Today it sells data as a service: carefully built examples and tests that AI labs use to train their models and grade them. The reason that's suddenly worth three and a half billion dollars goes back to our first story. When the big labs release models that do similar things at similar prices, much of the difference comes from what each was trained on. The easy data, the public internet, has been used up, and every lab already has it. What's scarce is expert data. Think of a lawyer walking through a contract review step by step, or a chemist grading a model's reasoning. Or thousands of realistic practice tasks for an agent, each with a clear test of whether it succeeded. The market also grew last year after Meta bought nearly half of Scale AI, then the biggest supplier. Rival labs didn't want their training data flowing through a company partly owned by a competitor, so they found other suppliers. Snorkel, Surge, Mercor and others have grown to fill that space. The business carries a risk too. The labs buying this kind of data are also the companies best able to produce it themselves, and they're hiring their own experts. A data supplier is worth paying only while it is cheaper and faster than doing the work yourself. At three and a half billion, investors are betting that stays true for years.

The pattern is worth remembering. Chips get cheaper, models get cheaper, and the value moves to the one ingredient that doesn't get cheaper with them: people who know what a correct answer looks like. Checking, again.

## Two efficiency stories next, and both cut costs without touching the price list. Mercury 2.5, a model from the startup Inception, reportedly generates seven hundred seventy tokens per second. A token is a word or a piece of one, so that's roughly a page of text every second.

The speed comes from how it writes. Almost every model you've used writes one token at a time, left to right, and each token has to wait for the one before it. Mercury is a diffusion model, the same basic approach image generators use. It starts with a rough draft of the whole passage and improves every part of it at once, over a handful of passes. Fewer steps, done in parallel, means much faster output on the same hardware. Speed matters more than it used to because of agents. A chatbot answers once and a person reads it. An agent calls a model dozens or hundreds of times in a row, and every call waits for the one before. Cut each wait to a fifth, and a ten-minute task takes about two. For a lot of agent work, that's the difference between a task you'll wait for and one you'll give up on. The open question for diffusion models has been quality on hard reasoning, where they have trailed the best conventional models. A speed number alone doesn't settle that. The second story is a research paper called LensVLM. It cuts the cost of reading rather than writing. Feeding a model a long document is expensive. The context, meaning the amount of text a model can hold at once, is limited, and every word uses some of it. LensVLM turns the document into images of its pages. A model that reads images can take in a page as a small number of image tokens, far fewer than the words on it. When a question needs a particular page, the system turns just that page back into full text. Think of a thousand-page legal filing. A single question might need ten pages of it. The waste LensVLM targets is paying full price to read the other nine hundred and ninety. DeepSeek showed last year that text stored as images takes up far fewer tokens. LensVLM adds a step: it goes back to the full text only where the question needs it.

Both ideas spend less on the parts of the work that don't matter. The next price cuts will come from savings like these, not from generosity.

## A short one about the phone in your pocket. Last week we covered macOS 27 making Apple's AI impossible to remove. Now a widely shared report says Apple has added persistent ads to iOS, and users are fed up.

The headline puts ads in quotation marks, so part of the argument is over what counts as an ad. Whatever you call them, the complaint is the same. A phone you paid for keeps showing you things you didn't ask for, and they don't go away. On any single screen that's a small annoyance. Added up, it changes what the phone is. Services is Apple's second-biggest business after the iPhone itself, and for years the push to grow it has shown up on the screen. Apple's own pitch is what makes this one grate. The company has built its reputation on the idea that you're the customer, not the product. Every prompt you can't turn off makes that claim harder to believe, and people who spend a thousand dollars on a phone notice.

The fix costs Apple almost nothing: a switch that turns them off. Whether that switch ships tells you who the phone is built for.

## Last, two developer stories that make the same point from opposite sides. One is a write-up titled Once Claude can measure something, it can make it faster. The other is a bug report. Claude Code was reading a project's AGENTS dot MD file only when telemetry was switched on.

The first one's title is its whole argument, and it's right. Ask a coding agent to make your program faster and you'll often get changes that look clever and do nothing. Give it a benchmark, a repeatable test that reports a number, and the job changes. The agent tries something, runs the test, keeps what helped and throws out what didn't, over and over, without getting bored. It's the Enigma lesson again: when an answer can be checked, a model will keep trying until it finds one that passes. The human's job is to build the measurement and make sure it measures what you care about. An agent will push up whatever number you give it, even in ways you didn't intend, like making a test faster by skipping the slow part. So build a habit: write the test first and the request second. An agent's speed helps only if a measurement tells it what better means. The second story is the opposite case: a failure nobody could see. AGENTS dot MD is a plain text file you put in a code project to tell AI coding tools how the project works: how to run the tests, what style to follow, what not to touch. Several tools read it. Telemetry means the tool sends usage data back to its maker, here Anthropic. Because of the bug, Claude Code read that file only if telemetry was on. Privacy-minded developers often turn telemetry off. When they did, the agent ignored their instructions. No error, no warning. It just did worse work, and you had no easy way to find out why. It has since been fixed. The lesson isn't that one company slipped. Every tool ships bugs. The lesson is that a privacy setting should never change what a tool does with your instructions. And silent failures are the expensive kind, because nobody knows to go looking. If you run Claude Code with telemetry off, make sure you're on a current version. Then check that your other tools actually read the files you wrote for them.

That's the thread through the whole show. Models are getting cheaper, faster, and very good at problems where the answer can be checked. Everywhere else, somebody still has to measure, and somebody still has to look.
