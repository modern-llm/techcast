# A Million Satellites, a Factoring Rumor, and a 1Password Breach
*November 26, 2026*

Welcome to TechCast Daily for Thursday, November 26th, and happy Thanksgiving to those of you in the United States. On Wednesday, the FCC gave SpaceX permission to put up to a million satellites in orbit as flying data centers, about fifty times everything up there today. Meanwhile, on the ground, AI data centers are getting harder to build. Also today: a breach at 1Password, a rumor that would break internet encryption if it were true, and OpenAI giving heavy users less for the same subscription.

## Last week we mentioned SpaceX's lenders wanting data centers in orbit. Now the regulator has said yes, in what Ars Technica calls an extraordinary decision.

On Wednesday, the Federal Communications Commission granted SpaceX conditional authorization for a constellation of up to one million satellites, called Starmind, designed to work as data centers in orbit. Instead of just relaying internet traffic the way Starlink does, these satellites would do the AI computing themselves. The pitch for computing in space is constant sunlight for power and no neighbors objecting to a new building. If the whole constellation were built, the number of satellites in orbit would grow roughly fifty-fold. FCC Chairman Brendan Carr called it a bold new frontier for the computing infrastructure America needs. Two words in that decision matter more than the million. The first is conditional. We don't know the conditions yet, and for a constellation this size, that's where the real fights will be: space debris, collisions, interference with other operators, and the night sky that astronomers depend on. The second is authorization. Permission to launch up to a million satellites is not a plan to launch a million satellites. It's a ceiling, and a ceiling this high mostly tells you what SpaceX wants investors to picture. Now compare that with the ground. TechCrunch reports that Nvidia has made clear it won't backstop an AI data center until it's nearly ready to run. Backstop here means putting Nvidia's own money or promises behind a project, the kind of support that has helped smaller companies borrow to build. Those companies are called neoclouds: firms like CoreWeave that exist mostly to rent out Nvidia chips. Nvidia's message lands just as neoclouds report construction delays and more expensive debt. The lesson for anyone pitching a data center is blunt. Land, power, and a finished building matter more than a pitch deck. TechCrunch's example is Anita Verma-Lallian, who has been selling exactly that.

Put the two side by side and the picture is odd. On Earth, the money now wants proof the building exists before it commits. In orbit, the regulator just approved a million machines that don't exist yet. One of those two standards is going to have to move.

## Security now, starting with a company whose entire product is trust.

1Password, the password manager, says a hacker stole customer names and email addresses after getting into its backend systems, meaning the servers the company runs, not the apps on your devices. Passwords and everything else stored in customer vaults were not exposed. The company hasn't said how many people are affected. Here's how it happened. 1Password started investigating on November 19th, after an attacker took over an employee's corporate GitHub account. GitHub is where companies keep and manage their code. With that one account, the attacker got into 1Password's source code and pushed malicious code to backend services, code written to download customer data. The Verge's report lists more data taken beyond names and emails, but the summary we have cuts off before saying what. Two takes. First, the vault design did its job. 1Password encrypts your vault on your own device, with a key the company never holds, so even a hacker running code on its servers walked away with names and emails, not passwords. That's the argument for end-to-end encryption, made by a real incident. Second, the worrying part is the way in. A single employee's account was enough to change code running in production, meaning the live systems serving customers. The question to put to 1Password is what stopped that same access from reaching the apps that ship to your phone and laptop, because that's where you type your master password.

What to do today: expect phishing, meaning fake emails dressed up as 1Password, now that attackers hold a list of confirmed customers. 1Password will not email you asking for your master password or your secret key. If a message does, it isn't them.

## Next, two security headlines from Hacker News. Titles only, but both worth knowing about.

The first is called Nfs2root, described as a twelve-year-old pre-auth remote root in the Linux kernel's NFS server. In plain words: NFS, the Network File System, is the long-standing way Linux machines share folders over a network, and Linux has an NFS server built into the kernel, the core of the operating system. Pre-auth means before anyone logs in. Remote root means full control of the machine, from across the network. So the claim is that anyone who can reach your NFS server can take over the machine without a password, and that the hole has sat there for about twelve years. We don't have the write-up, so we can't tell you which versions are affected or whether a fix is out.

If you run an NFS server, check two things today: that it's reachable only from networks you trust, and that your Linux distribution's next kernel update gets installed promptly.

## The second headline would be far bigger, if it's true. Right now, it's a rumor stacked on a title.

A Hacker News post is headlined: rumors swirling that labs might have cracked factoring in P. A second post says Google has released papers, under the name Cogentic, on integer factorization, discrete logarithms, and more. We have no details beyond those titles, including what Cogentic is. So here's why the words alone would matter. Factoring means taking a huge number and finding the two primes that multiply to make it. Multiplying is easy. Going backwards is believed to be hopelessly slow, and that belief is what keeps RSA encryption safe. RSA is the scheme behind much of web security and digital signatures. In P means solvable in polynomial time, which in practice means fast on an ordinary computer, no quantum machine needed. Discrete logarithms are the hard problem behind much of the rest: the key exchange your browser does when it connects, and the elliptic-curve cryptography in phones and cryptocurrencies. A fast method for both would break most of the encryption the internet uses today. Now the skepticism. After the Riemann hypothesis claims we covered recently, the word labs most likely means AI labs, and big claims are now arriving faster than anyone can check them. We don't know whether Google's papers claim a break at all, or something much narrower. And a real result would be easy to prove without anyone reading a paper. There are published challenge numbers, RSA keys of known size that nobody has ever factored. Factor the one called RSA-2048 in public, and the debate is over in an afternoon.

Until someone factors one of those numbers, treat this as noise. The useful reminder is that the move to post-quantum encryption, built on different math, is already underway. A scare like this is a good moment to check that your own systems can switch.

## Now OpenAI, and a price increase that leaves the price alone.

TechCrunch reports that OpenAI is merging the Chat and Work sides of ChatGPT, and with them, moving to a single usage meter across the whole product. OpenAI says heavy users may hit their limits sooner. A Hacker News headline puts numbers on it: on GPT 6.2, the version called Astra hits limits forty percent faster, and the version called Sol twenty-five percent faster. Those numbers come from a headline, so we don't know how they were measured. Astra taking the bigger cut would fit a familiar pattern, where the most capable model is the most expensive to serve. Here's why one meter matters. One meter means one pool. An agent grinding through a big job in the afternoon can use up what you'd have spent on quick questions in the evening. And reaching a limit forty percent faster means getting about thirty percent less done before you're cut off, on the same plan, at the same price.

This is where flat monthly pricing was always headed. Models now do longer work, each task costs more to run, and the price stays fixed, so the allowance shrinks. If you rely on ChatGPT for your job, watch which tasks drain the meter for a week before assuming your plan still fits.

## Astra, the model whose allowance just shrank, also has a new claim to fame.

A former Googler built a benchmark out of an old office hobby. Back in 2015, a small group at Google spent hours every week on code golf, which means solving a puzzle with the shortest program possible. People fought over single bytes, and there always seemed to be another Googler with a shorter answer. Once a problem closed, every solution was shared, and the author says reading the winners was always astonishing. Eleven years later, they collected those records and set frontier models against them. The headline result: GPT 6.2 Astra is the first model to beat the Googlers' records. And the models found genuinely clever approaches. On two problems, called Surviving Queens and The Rolling Dice, models used complex numbers, the kind with an imaginary part, which Python and Ruby support out of the box, to write shorter solutions. Another problem asks for the first n positive numbers you can make by multiplying powers of minus two, minus three and minus five. The post's account of what the models did there is cut off in the excerpt we have. The author says the idea came from an OpenAI post describing researchers watching a model solve open math problems one after another in a Slack thread.

Code golf is a better test than it sounds. Every answer can be checked, the human record is known, and strong people set it with real effort. If those solutions stayed mostly inside Google, a model is also less likely to have memorized them. Still, the shortest program is almost never the one you'd want to maintain. This measures cleverness, not good engineering. Hold on to that point about tests nobody could have memorized. It comes back later.

## Next, what happens to your chats when an AI company goes broke.

Character.AI, the app where people chat with AI personas, filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in October, after years of scandals and legal challenges, including from Disney. Chapter 11 lets a company reorganize or sell itself off in pieces under a court's supervision. Disney ended up buying some of the company's assets and hiring its chief executive. Now a coalition of 42 state attorneys general is trying to stop what they say comes next. Their accusation is that the company plans to auction the private chat histories of more than 21 million U.S. accounts, more than 2.1 million of them registered to minors, with no safeguards for the people whose conversations are being sold. Think about what those chats contain. People used Character.AI for role play, for company, and for some, as something close to a diary. In a bankruptcy, data is an asset like office furniture, and the process exists to get creditors as much money as possible. A privacy policy written while the company was healthy protects less than people assume once a court is selling the assets. We've seen this fight before. States pushed back last year when 23andMe's genetic data went up for sale in its bankruptcy.

The attorneys general are right to push. The rule the industry needs is easy to state: when a chat company dies, the chat logs get deleted, not sold. If you or your kids used Character.AI, deleting the account now is worth doing, though whether deletion holds up in a bankruptcy sale is part of what this fight will decide.

## A short follow-up on Meta's AI agent Muse, whose privacy questions we covered last week.

What's new: Amazon and Meta are reportedly in talks on a deal that would end a two-month blockade of Muse. The report we have gives no terms. But the fact that it takes a negotiation between two of the biggest companies in tech tells you something. An agent that runs your errands only works where the store lets it in, and stores have figured out that letting it in is something to bargain over.

For anyone building an agent, that's the hard part. The model is the easy bit. The access is the business.

## Staying with who is in control: Anthropic's chief executive is pushing back on rivals.

Dario Amodei called accusations from rivals that Amodei favors surrendering human control to AI absurd. TechCrunch sets the denial against a real trend: the leading AI companies are increasingly automating their own research and the development of their next models. We only have the summary, not the full exchange, so I won't put words in anyone's mouth about who said what.

My take: the accusation and the denial are both about intentions, and intentions are cheap. The question worth putting to every lab is concrete. What share of the work on the next model is now done by the current model, and which of those steps still has a person who reads the result and can say no? A lab that answered that in numbers would settle this argument.

## Back to tests nobody could have memorized, this time in physics.

About six months ago, Suvrat Raju, a professor of theoretical physics at the International Centre for Theoretical Sciences in Bengaluru, India, gave a frontier AI model a problem Raju had already solved but never published. That was the point. Models are trained on most of the internet, so if a problem has been discussed online, a model may simply remember the answer. An unpublished problem is a fair test. The model got it wrong, but it did better than Raju expected, well enough to convince Raju the experiment should be repeated and done properly. With a colleague, Raju set out to build a benchmark for theoretical physics, a set of test problems for AI. The piece, from the newsletter Understanding AI, argues that AI may soon change what it means to be a theoretical physicist. The excerpt we have stops before any benchmark results, so treat that as the writer's argument, not a finding.

What I like here is the method. Code golf records kept inside one company, a solution sitting in a physicist's drawer: the most trustworthy AI tests now come from work that never reached the internet. Expect more experts to quietly hold a few problems back for exactly this reason.

## For developers, four stories about agents doing the patient work people tend to skip.

The one with real detail is a Show HN from a professor of algorithm engineering. In that field, you improve an algorithm in a cycle: design it, analyze it, implement it, test it on real-world data, then use what you saw to design the next version. The professor's team packaged that cycle as a skill for an AI coding agent, meaning a ready-made set of instructions, and runs it in an endless loop. It profiles the code to see where time goes, writes down a guess about what would make it faster, implements the change, and measures. If the code got faster and passes tests agreed with the user up front, the change stays. If not, it's thrown away. Everything is tracked in version control, and the output includes a PDF report and separate patches, so you can merge only the ones you trust. They report their KaHIP graph partitioning toolkit ran 3.9 times faster afterward, and their VieCut minimum cut solver 8.9 times faster. Those are the team's own codebases, which they know well. The part that matters is the throw-it-away rule. A lot of agent work keeps whatever compiles. This keeps only what a measurement supports. The weak point is the tests. An agent hunting for speed will find any shortcut the tests don't forbid, which is why agreeing on them first matters so much. Three more titles point the same way. One developer ported Rockbox, the open-source replacement software for music players, to an undocumented nine-dollar MP3 player from AliExpress called the YP3, and the title credits language models with the help. Undocumented means no manual and no data sheet, so somebody had to work out how the hardware behaves. Another post says the author checked Clojure's core library with Lean, a language for writing proofs that a computer checks line by line, and found bugs nobody had reported. And one post is titled, simply, multiagent coding is the future. That title is all we have.

The common thread: agents are good at grind. Profiling, poking at unknown hardware, checking every case. The people getting the most out of them set up the measurement first.

## Last, two quick titles for people who tune or run models themselves.

One is titled Predicting LoRA size for any dataset. LoRA, short for low-rank adaptation, is the cheap way to specialize a model: instead of retraining all of it, you train a small add-on on your own data. The hard part has been guessing how big that add-on should be. Too small and it can't learn what's in your data. Too big and you waste memory and training time. A reliable way to predict the right size from the dataset itself would save a lot of trial and error. The other is titled Bare Metal Qwen. Qwen is Alibaba's family of open models, and bare metal usually means running straight on the hardware, with little or no operating system in between. That's our reading of a title, nothing more. Both are pointers, not results.

If either holds up, it's another small step toward models you can shape and run without renting a data center.
