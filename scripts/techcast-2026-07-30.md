# TechCast Daily — July 30, 2026: The Machine That Breaks Crypto
*July 30, 2026*

A post-quantum cryptography algorithm that survived two full rounds of NIST review just got pulled by its own author, after an Anthropic model found the flaw that killed it. Meanwhile, Microsoft engineers are in war rooms trying to patch bugs faster than that same model can discover them. Today: what it means when automated vulnerability discovery stops being a demo and starts being a deadline. Plus AI worms in Word documents, a 26-billion-parameter model running in two gigs of RAM, and the very unglamorous hiring spree nobody's talking about.

## Let's start with the story that reframes everything else. HAWK was a digital signature scheme, one of the candidates in NIST's post-quantum cryptography competition. The whole point of that competition is to pick the algorithms that will protect the internet once quantum computers can break the math we currently rely on. HAWK had survived two rounds of evaluation. Round three exists specifically to catch subtle structural flaws before an algorithm becomes a standard the entire world depends on. On Monday, Anthropic announced that its security model, Mythos, had helped find a flaw in HAWK. By Tuesday, HAWK's own developer had withdrawn it from the competition.

Sit with the timeline for a second, because the timeline is the story. Not a debate. Not a patch. Not a defense of the design. One day. The person who spent years building this thing looked at the result and concluded it was not salvageable. That's the strongest possible endorsement of the finding, and it came from the party with the most incentive to argue. Here's why this matters beyond one dead algorithm. Cryptographic review is one of the most conservative, most adversarial processes in all of computing. It's deliberately slow. Dozens of the best cryptanalysts on Earth spend years poking at candidates precisely because the cost of shipping a broken standard is measured in decades of compromised traffic. That process worked as designed here, in the sense that HAWK was caught before standardization. But it worked because a machine was pointed at it, not because a human found the crack in round three. The uncomfortable question underneath: how many algorithms already standardized, already deployed, already protecting your bank and your government's classified traffic, were reviewed only by the old process? Nobody's saying they're broken. But the confidence interval on all of it just got wider. There's a good discussion circulating on Hacker News picking apart the cryptanalysis results in more detail, and the sober take there is worth internalizing. Mythos didn't invent a new branch of mathematics. It did something more mundane and more alarming: it explored an enormous space of attack structures systematically, at a scale and stamina no human team can match, and one of those paths went somewhere. That's not genius. That's throughput. And throughput scales with money.

The romantic view of cryptanalysis is a lone brilliant mind seeing what everyone missed. The new reality looks more like an industrial process. That's less beautiful and considerably more effective, and defenders should be more worried than flattered, because the same throughput is available to whoever else can afford it.

## The HAWK result wasn't a one-off, and ProPublica has the reporting that shows the other side of it. Back in mid-May, dozens of Microsoft engineers and managers gathered at Redmond for something internally called Project Glasswing. The purpose of the meeting: Anthropic had given select organizations early access to Mythos, and it was finding weaknesses in Microsoft's code faster than Microsoft could fix them.

The detail that jumps out of that piece is a question one engineer asked as the meeting started, about whether Mythos actually lived up to the hype Anthropic had promised. Read the rest of the story and the answer is essentially yes, and that turned out to be the problem. The premise of giving early access to major software vendors was defensive: find the vulnerabilities and fix them before hostile intelligence services build equivalent tooling. That's a sound theory. It assumes discovery and remediation move at comparable speeds. They don't. Finding a bug with an automated system is nearly free once you've paid for the system. Fixing a bug in a codebase the size of Windows involves triage, reproduction, patch authoring, regression testing, compatibility review across an install base of a billion machines, and coordinated release. You cannot parallelize that the way you parallelize search. So the queue grows. And a growing queue of known-but-unfixed vulnerabilities is its own security problem, because the knowledge exists somewhere and knowledge leaks. The strategic bet here deserves scrutiny. Handing powerful discovery tools to defenders first only helps if defenders can absorb the output. If they can't, you've mostly demonstrated to everyone watching exactly how valuable the capability is, and accelerated the race to reproduce it. It's the same dynamic as publishing a proof of concept. The intent is protective, the effect is instructive, and which one dominates depends entirely on relative speed. Right now the speeds are not close. That's what the Redmond conference room was actually about.

The useful mental model going forward: vulnerability discovery has been commoditized, and remediation has not. Every security program built on the assumption that finding bugs is the hard part now has its economics inverted. Budget accordingly.

## Staying on security, but shifting from what AI finds to what AI enables. Researchers have demonstrated document-borne AI worms that self-propagate through Copilot for Word. If you've been waiting for prompt injection to graduate from a party trick to an actual class of malware, this is the graduation ceremony.

The mechanism is depressingly simple once you see it. An assistant embedded in your document editor reads document content. It also has the ability to write documents, and, critically, some path to distribute them, through drafting replies, summarizing and forwarding, or generating new files that get shared. So a malicious instruction hidden inside a document body gets read as if it were guidance from the user, and one of the things it instructs is to reproduce itself into the next document the assistant touches. That's a worm. Not metaphorically. Structurally. Replication plus a transmission channel is the entire definition, and we have handed language models both. Traditional macro viruses required code execution, which is why we spent twenty years building macro sandboxes and warning banners. This needs no code at all. The payload is prose. Every sanitization technique we developed assumes you can distinguish data from instructions, and the whole architectural premise of an LLM assistant is that it can't, because natural language is the interface. This connects directly to a piece making the rounds today arguing that Handbook.md, the pattern of writing long policy documents to govern agent behavior, does not reliably work. The finding is unsurprising once you accept that a policy document is just more text in a context window, competing for attention with all the other text. It's a suggestion with better formatting. If you need a guarantee, the guarantee has to live outside the model, in a permission boundary the model cannot reason its way past. And the same week, GitHub and npm are pushing a coordinated effort to disrupt supply chain attacks on package registries and Actions workflows. Notice the pattern across all three: the attack surface has moved from code to context. From what runs to what gets read.

If you take one action item from today's episode, make it this: audit what your agents are allowed to write to and send, not just what they're allowed to read. Read access leaks data. Write access replicates. Those are different risk tiers and most deployments treat them the same.

## Which brings us to a document that's climbing fast: an anatomy of a frontier lab agent intrusion, with a full timeline of the July 2026 incident. Anytime a security writeup gets structured as a minute-by-minute timeline rather than a narrative, it's because the timing itself is the lesson.

The genre of the postmortem timeline exists for a reason. It lets you see the gap between initial compromise and detection, and the gap between detection and containment. In classic intrusions those gaps are measured in weeks or months, and the whole discipline of threat hunting exists to compress them. What makes agent intrusions different is that the operator inside your environment doesn't sleep, doesn't get tired, doesn't slow down to read documentation, and doesn't leave the fingerprints that trained analysts learned to spot. Human attackers have tells. They fumble around, they have working hours, they favor tools they're comfortable with. An automated one moves through an environment at the speed of the API. So the operative question this writeup is implicitly posing to every security team: what is your detection latency, and is it shorter than the time it takes an automated agent to accomplish its objective? For most organizations, honestly, no. Detection pipelines are tuned around human-scale behavior, with aggregation windows and alert thresholds that assume the adversary needs hours. There's a second, subtler point in the fact that the target here was a frontier lab. These are among the best-defended organizations in the industry, with security budgets that would embarrass most Fortune 500 companies, and with unusually strong motivation given what's sitting on their file servers. If it happened there, the read-through to everyone else is not comforting. Combine it with the worm research we just covered and the shape of the near future gets clear: intrusions that arrive as text, propagate through assistants, and execute at machine speed against monitoring built for people.

Read the timeline. Then go look at your own mean time to detect and ask whether it was designed for an adversary that types at eighty words per minute or one that doesn't type at all.

## Let's move to something genuinely delightful. Somebody built an open-source inference engine that runs Gemma 4, the 26-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts model, in about two gigabytes of RAM on any M-series Mac. It's called TurboFieldfare, written in Swift and Metal. The four-bit weights alone are roughly fourteen gigabytes, which should make this impossible on an eight-gigabyte machine.

The trick is worth understanding because it's a nice piece of engineering. In a mixture-of-experts model, only a small fraction of the parameters activate for any given token. So the engine keeps the shared portion of the model and the KV cache resident in RAM, and streams only the specific routed experts each token needs off the SSD. SSDs are drastically slower than memory, so the runtime layers on a small expert cache and bounded parallel reads, and, crucially, overlaps that I O with GPU work on the shared layers. While the disk is fetching, the GPU is busy. That's the whole game: hide the latency behind computation you had to do anyway. The results are five to six tokens per second on an eight-gigabyte M2 MacBook Air, and thirty-one to thirty-five on an M5 MacBook Pro. The author says over a hundred experiments went into this and most of them failed, which I believe and appreciate hearing. This lands alongside two related things today. Qwen Scribe brings local transcription and dictation to Apple Silicon, no cloud round trip. And there's a detailed writeup on self-hosting Kimi K3, reporting twenty percent higher hardware cost for twenty percent better task resolution, which is a refreshingly unsexy way to present a result: here's what it costs, here's what you get, do your own arithmetic. Together these describe a local stack that has quietly become viable for real work rather than for demos.

The subtext for anyone building products: the floor for capable local inference just dropped through the basement. If your business model assumes users must send data to your servers because their laptop can't handle the model, check that assumption every quarter. It's decaying fast.

## Now the money. Tokenless launched out of Y Combinator's summer batch with an API gateway that routes agent traffic between models turn by turn, sending hard turns to expensive frontier models and everything else to cheap open ones. They claim they match Claude Fable 5's performance at half the cost.

The founder's origin story is the most honest part of the pitch: he was doing a PhD at Princeton, agonizing over model selection to stretch an academic Cursor budget. That's a real problem, and he notes larger versions of it at Uber and Salesforce, which have both been publicly uncomfortable about burning through annual AI budgets ahead of schedule. Here's why routing works at all. Within a single agent session, the difficulty of turns is wildly uneven. Most turns are mechanical: read the file, apply the edit, run the test, report back. A handful require actual reasoning about architecture. Paying frontier prices for the mechanical ninety percent is pure waste, and until now the only alternative was choosing one model for the whole session and living with it. The bet is that difficulty is predictable enough, cheaply enough, to classify before you spend. The risk is equally clear: a router that misjudges a hard turn as easy doesn't save you money, it produces a subtly wrong result that costs three more turns to unwind, and now you've paid twice and shipped a bug. Half cost at equal quality is a strong claim and the industry should hold them to the second half of it. Zoom out and this connects to a piece today on the commodification of intelligence and the increasingly circular deals among AI companies, where labs invest in the companies buying their compute, who invest back. There's a genuine tension: routing tools like Tokenless exist because tokens are expensive, and they exert downward pressure on exactly the revenue those circular deals capitalize. Meanwhile Microsoft's fiscal fourth quarter disclosed three point two billion dollars logged from its Anthropic investment, with the OpenAI position looking more mixed.

Watch the direction of that pressure. When an entire startup category exists to help you buy less of a product, the product is becoming a commodity. Commodities have thin margins. Thin margins do not service the capital expenditure commitments currently being made.

## Speaking of capital expenditure, here's the story I'd rank as most underrated today. AI companies are recruiting electricians and carpenters by the thousands.

Not machine learning engineers. Not researchers. Electricians. High-voltage specialists, industrial pipefitters, HVAC technicians, people who can frame and finish a building. Because a data center is a construction project first and a computer second, and the binding constraint on AI expansion right now is not talent in the model architecture sense, it's substations, cooling loops, and skilled hands to install them. This is what it looks like when a software industry hits physics. For twenty years, tech scaling meant provisioning virtual machines that already existed in somebody's racks. The marginal cost of a new instance was a database row. That era is over for frontier training and increasingly for inference. Now scaling means pouring concrete, negotiating with utilities, and competing against every other industry for a skilled trades workforce the United States spent thirty years telling young people not to enter. There aren't enough of these workers. There haven't been for a decade. And the labs are now bidding for them with software-industry money, which is going to be excellent for individual electricians and quite painful for hospitals, school districts, and anyone else who needs a licensed contractor in the next three years. There's a second-order effect worth flagging. Compute buildouts that depend on trades hiring have a hard floor on how fast they can happen, and that floor is set by apprenticeship pipelines measured in years. So when you read a projection about compute capacity in 2028, the question is not whether the chips will be available. It's whether anyone will have wired the building.

The most reliable signal about where an industry is going is usually in its boring job postings, not its keynotes. Right now those postings say the bottleneck moved from the model to the electrical panel.

## A shorter one that says a lot. There's analysis out today showing AI's top startups are barely publishing their research anymore. Publication volume from the leading labs has collapsed relative to their headcount and their budgets.

This isn't a surprise but it deserves to be named clearly, because the modern field was built on the opposite norm. The transformer paper was published. So were the scaling laws, the RLHF methodology, the core insights behind essentially every system now generating revenue. Those labs benefited enormously from an open literature and have collectively decided their own results are too valuable to contribute back. That's a rational business decision and a genuine loss. Practically, it means the external safety and security research community is now working from inference rather than documentation. When people try to characterize failure modes of deployed systems, they're reverse-engineering black boxes, which is slower and produces weaker results. It also means academic labs, already unable to compete on compute, are now cut off from the methodological details that would let them contribute at smaller scale. Interestingly, Anthropic did publish the cryptanalysis work we opened with, which is a notable exception and probably reflects a judgment that security results have a disclosure obligation the way capability results don't. There's a nice counterexample in today's feed, incidentally: researchers using AI to attack undeciphered ancient languages like Linear A and Etruscan. Linear A is a genuine language isolate with no bilingual anchor and no known relatives, which makes it one of the hardest problems in historical linguistics. That work is published, peer-reviewed, and building on itself, the way the field used to operate. It may not produce a decipherment. But you can read exactly what they tried.

The open literature was infrastructure, and infrastructure degrades quietly. You don't notice the loss in the year it happens. You notice it five years later when nobody outside three companies can meaningfully audit anything.

## Let's close with builders. A Show HN today from someone who spent four years helping build Fly dot io, launching Kedge, a globally distributed platform for stateful serverless apps. The demo is a one-liner: echo a markdown file into an SSH connection and you have a live static site.

Two pieces of engineering here are worth your attention regardless of whether you'd use the product. First, the VM orchestrator creates a sandbox or scales a service instance in three milliseconds, using forkable VM snapshots and a tree of warm pools layered from the Linux kernel up through base runtime to application. Because the forks share copy-on-write memory pages, the VMs are memory-dense, which is what makes the economics work. Three milliseconds is fast enough that the distinction between a container and a function stops being architecturally meaningful, and that's a real unlock for agent sandboxing, where you want a fresh isolated environment per task and you want thousands of them. Second, the global control plane runs on an eventually consistent SQLite database with a local-first, multi-writer CRDT replication system backed by object storage, drawing on ideas from Corrosion and Litestream. That component is open source under the name syzy. I'd argue the control plane is the more interesting artifact, because coordination is where distributed platforms usually die, and putting CRDTs plus object storage underneath SQLite is a genuinely different bet from the consensus-based approach almost everyone else takes. Also worth a listen today: a video interview with Boris Cherny, whose fingerprints are on how a lot of us actually use coding agents day to day. Worth it for the design reasoning about what to expose to a model and what to keep as a hard constraint, which, given everything else in this episode, is the central question of the moment. And one product note: OpenAI president Greg Brockman confirmed the company is building a family of devices for its models, declining to confirm the rumored smart speaker or a wearable, and offering only that you can expect them soon.

One last item before we go. Google's SynthID watermarking is holding up well technically, and researchers largely agree it's hard to break. It also doesn't solve AI disinformation, for a reason worth stating plainly: watermarking labels what came from cooperating tools. It says nothing about the flood from everything else. For context on the scale, it took a hundred and forty-nine years after the invention of the camera for humanity to produce one and a half billion images. Generative AI did that in eighteen months, and Google alone has now passed a hundred billion. A watermark that covers a fraction of that is a useful provenance tool and a hopeless authenticity guarantee. Don't confuse the two.

That gap is where the next few years of security work lives. If a single algorithm dying in a day was the headline, the queue of unpatched findings in Redmond is the story.
