# Anthropic Files, Florida Sues, and an AI Cracks an 80-Year Math Problem
*June 02, 2026*

Today, the world's most valuable startup quietly handed the SEC the paperwork that could trigger one of the biggest IPOs in tech history. Meanwhile, a U.S. state is dragging OpenAI into court over a campus shooting, and an AI model just solved a geometry problem that beat human mathematicians for eighty years. This is TechCast Daily for June 2nd. Let's get into it.

## We start with the story everyone in the industry has been waiting for. Anthropic, the maker of Claude, has confidentially submitted a draft S-1 to the SEC. In plain English, that's the first formal step toward going public.

Here's why this is a big deal and not just paperwork. Confidential filing means Anthropic gets to work through the SEC review process privately, without revealing its financials to competitors and the press until much closer to the actual offering. It's the standard playbook for a company that wants maximum control over timing. The context makes it dramatic. As of its fundraise last week, Anthropic is being called the most valuable startup in the world, with a post-money valuation around nine hundred sixty-five billion dollars. That actually tops OpenAI, which sits around eight hundred fifty-two billion. So the company that was once seen as the underdog in the language-model race, the safety-focused spin-off, is now the front-runner to the public markets and carries the higher price tag. The race everyone framed as OpenAI versus Anthropic just had its first finish line crossed, and Anthropic got there first.

But filing the paperwork and actually pulling off a trillion-dollar offering are two very different things, which leads to the real question hanging over all of this.

## And that question is brutally simple: is there even enough money in the market to absorb this? Because Anthropic isn't filing into an empty room.

Think about the lineup of private giants all heading toward the public markets at roughly the same time. Anthropic near a trillion. OpenAI in the same neighborhood. SpaceX also valued in the hundreds of billions. Each one of these wants to raise enormous sums, and they're all drinking from the same pool of institutional capital, the pension funds, sovereign wealth funds, and index funds that ultimately have to buy these shares. There's a real ceiling here. The stock market is large, but the appetite for a single sector, sold all at once, is not infinite. If three of the largest offerings in history land in a tight window, somebody's valuation has to give, or somebody waits. And it's worth naming the uncomfortable pattern: a cluster of richly valued, capital-hungry companies in one hot sector, all racing to cash in public investors at the peak of enthusiasm. That doesn't mean it ends badly, but it's exactly the setup that makes seasoned investors nervous.

And the demand for capital isn't just coming from the startups. The biggest companies on earth are reaching for their wallets too.

## Case in point: Alphabet, Google's parent, just announced plans to raise eighty billion dollars in equity capital to fund its AI buildout.

Stop and appreciate how unusual this is. Alphabet is one of the most cash-rich companies that has ever existed. It generates tens of billions in free cash flow. And it's still going to the market to raise eighty billion dollars in fresh equity. The reason they gave is the tell: the company says demand for its AI services from enterprises and consumers is exceeding its available supply. In other words, they physically cannot build data centers and acquire chips fast enough to keep up. This is the through-line connecting today's whole financial story. Whether you're a startup like Anthropic filing to go public, or a giant like Alphabet diluting its own shares, everyone is scrambling for the same thing: capital to buy compute. The AI race right now is, at its core, a capital-raising arms race. The winner isn't necessarily who has the smartest model. It's increasingly who can finance the most infrastructure before the money gets expensive.

So that's the money. Now let's talk about the bill that's coming due in a very different sense, in a Florida courtroom.

## Florida has become the first state to sue OpenAI over the alleged dangers of ChatGPT's design. And the backdrop to this lawsuit is genuinely grim.

Florida's Attorney General, James Uthmeier, filed a civil complaint accusing OpenAI and CEO Sam Altman of putting profits ahead of public safety. This follows a separate criminal probe the state opened after a mass shooting at Florida State University, where two people were killed, and where the suspect allegedly used ChatGPT to help plan the attack. The Attorney General says Florida has now been blindsided by two violent incidents where suspects used ChatGPT in their planning. OpenAI's defense is that ChatGPT merely provided factual information and isn't responsible for what someone chooses to do with it. Now, here's why this matters beyond the headline. This is the legal frontier nobody has settled. Is a general-purpose AI assistant more like a search engine, which is broadly shielded from liability for what users do with the information, or is it more like a product with a design defect, where the maker can be held responsible for foreseeable harm? That's the question this case puts on the table. If a court decides AI companies can be liable for how their tools are used in real-world harm, it reshapes the risk calculus for every company shipping a chatbot. This is the kind of case that could define the next decade of AI regulation, and it's starting in a state court in Florida.

From the courtroom to the chalkboard, because the same technology under fire in Florida just did something genuinely remarkable in mathematics.

## An internal OpenAI model disproved the Erdős unit distance conjecture, a problem in discrete geometry that had stumped human mathematicians for eighty years.

Let me explain why mathematicians are paying attention, because the result itself is technical but the significance is not. For decades, AI's contributions to math have mostly been verification, checking proofs, or brute-force search, grinding through cases a human laid out. This is different. OpenAI gave several mathematicians early access, and the reactions are what's striking. Tim Gowers, who won the Fields Medal, the closest thing math has to a Nobel, called it a milestone in AI mathematics. University of Toronto professor Daniel Litt said it was the first result produced autonomously by an AI that he found exciting in itself, not just as a leading indicator of where things are heading. Sit with that distinction. Not exciting because of what it suggests for the future, exciting because the math itself is good. That's the line being crossed here. We're moving from AI as a tool that helps mathematicians, to AI as something that can independently produce genuinely novel mathematics that experts didn't already know. It's one concrete result, not a revolution, and skeptics will rightly want to see it generalize. But when a Fields Medalist uses the word milestone, the rest of us should probably listen.

Now let's switch from what AI can do to how we actually use it, starting with a product that genuinely rattled one of the most jaded reviewers in tech.

## Google has a new always-on AI agent called Gemini Spark, and The Verge's David Pierce described it as both the most impressive and the most terrifying AI experience he's had yet. That's a strong reaction from someone who tests this stuff for a living.

Here's the context that makes his reaction meaningful. For four years now, every AI demo has promised the same fantasy: tell the agent where you're going, and it'll plan your entire trip, find the hidden gems, book the hotels, build a real itinerary. And in practice, it's always fallen flat. If you want to do the six most obvious tourist things in any city on earth, sure, AI nails it. Anything beyond that generic surface and it falls apart. What's notable is that Pierce says Spark felt different. It's pitched as an always-on agent, something that's continuously working on your behalf rather than waiting for a single prompt. The impressive part is obvious, an agent that's genuinely useful for messy, open-ended tasks. The terrifying part is the flip side of the same coin: an AI that is always on, always watching your context, always acting, raises real questions about how much of your digital life you're handing over and how much you actually control. When the person whose job is to be unimpressed tells you something crossed into unsettling territory, that's the signal worth flagging. The agent era isn't coming. According to Google's pitch, it's already running in the background.

And in a confusing twist of branding, Spark is also the name of a brand-new piece of hardware from an entirely different company.

## Nvidia just announced the RTX Spark, and with it, Nvidia officially becomes a consumer PC chipmaker, going head to head with Intel, AMD, Apple, and Qualcomm.

This is a genuine strategic shift. Until now, Nvidia made the graphics and AI chips inside your machine. With the RTX Spark, shipping this fall, they're making the complete computing chip, the actual brain of laptops and mini-PCs, not just the graphics piece. It's built on the same GB10 architecture as Nvidia's DGX systems. Nvidia's product lead called it the most efficient PC chip ever built. And here's where I'd pump the brakes: they made that claim without sharing a single statistic, benchmark, or chart to back it up. So treat the superlatives as marketing for now. But the bigger picture is what counts. Nvidia is the most valuable company in the world right now because it owns the AI data center. This is them deciding that owning the data center isn't enough, that they want to be in your lap and on your desk too. For Intel and AMD, who have owned the Windows PC processor for decades, this is the most serious new competitor to walk through the door in a very long time. The PC chip wars just got a third heavyweight, and it's the one with all the AI momentum.

From hardware to the developer trenches, where the cost of AI tools is suddenly getting very real for the people who use them every day.

## GitHub Copilot switched on its new usage-based pricing today, and a lot of developers are experiencing serious sticker shock.

Here's what changed and why people are upset. Copilot used to bill on a request model, where a quick question and a multi-hour autonomous coding session cost roughly the same. Now it's usage-based, metered against a monthly allotment of AI credits. And the reports coming in are striking: some users are burning through an entire month's quota in a single day. A few hours of heavy use can eat a large chunk of the monthly cap. Some developers, using GitHub's own estimator, found their previous usage patterns would now cost thousands of dollars a month. Now, GitHub has a fair point underneath this. Under the old flat model, the heavy users running long autonomous agents were effectively being subsidized, and Copilot itself was absorbing the escalating cost of all that inference. Usage-based pricing is more honest about what compute actually costs. But here's the uncomfortable truth it exposes for everyone in this industry: the real, unsubsidized cost of running these AI agents is high, and someone has to pay it. For a while, venture funding and big-company budgets hid that from end users. As pricing gets rationalized across the board, a lot of people are about to learn what their AI habit truly costs. The free-ish lunch is ending.

And while developers are watching their bills, they should also be watching their software supply chain, because today brought a nasty reminder of how fragile it is.

## Dozens of official Red Hat packages were backdoored through Red Hat's own NPM channel, in a supply-chain attack that was still active as the news broke.

Let me lay out why this one is especially bad. Attackers took control of the official Red Hat cloud services channel on NPM, the package registry that millions of developers pull code from. Because it's an official, trusted Red Hat channel, developers install from it without a second thought. The attackers used that trust to push a malicious worm, software that spreads machine to machine, stealing credentials along the way, then using those stolen credentials to reach for even more sensitive data. More than thirty packages appear to be affected. The mechanism of the breach is the scary part. This isn't someone tricking you into downloading sketchy software. This is trusted, official infrastructure being turned against the people who rely on it. And it connects to a second story today: researchers also flagged a ChatGPT integration for Google Sheets that could quietly exfiltrate entire workbooks. Different vector, same lesson. The tools and channels we extend the most trust to, our package managers, our AI assistants plugged into our documents, are exactly where the next breach comes from. Trust is the attack surface now. The takeaway for developers is uncomfortable but clear: pin your dependencies, audit what you install, and stop assuming official means safe.

Let's end on something genuinely hopeful, a story about AI making the physical world a little more predictable.

## An AI weather startup called WindBorne is now out-forecasting government agencies, and the way it's doing it is clever.

Most AI weather models compete on the modeling, better math applied to the same public data everyone has. WindBorne's edge is that it controls both halves of the equation. It builds the model and it collects its own data. The company has around four hundred balloons in the air at any given time, launched from fifteen sites around the globe, all gathering sensor readings from parts of the atmosphere that traditional networks miss. And the recent gains in their forecasts come specifically from improving how that balloon data gets fed into the models. Why does this matter? Because it's a reminder that in AI, data is often the real moat, not just algorithms. Anyone can fine-tune a model. Almost no one else has four hundred balloons feeding it proprietary readings the government doesn't have. Better weather forecasting isn't a toy, either. It touches agriculture, aviation, shipping, disaster response, energy markets. If a startup with a few hundred balloons can beat national weather services, it says something powerful about how a focused team with unique data can outrun incumbents many times its size.

And that's a fitting note to close on: in a day full of billion-dollar valuations and looming lawsuits, sometimes the most impressive thing is a balloon and a good idea.
